Expert Judgment in Project Management: Guide and Examples

Expert judgment in project management is advice based on the specialist knowledge, skills or experience of people who understand the work, used to support estimates, plans, risk assessments and decisions. This guide explains where it fits, who counts as an expert, the main techniques, a seven-step process and two worked examples, plus how to keep bias out of the result.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 2 October 2026
  • Last updated 18 October 2026
  • 19 min read

Key takeaways

  • Expert judgment is structured advice from people with relevant expertise; it is a tool and technique across almost every area of project management.
  • Expertise is specific: choose experts for the exact question, and use more than one where the stakes are high.
  • Structured methods such as Delphi, nominal group technique and planning poker reduce anchoring, groupthink and the pull of senior voices.
  • Ask for ranges and reasons, not single numbers, then combine expert views with historical data wherever it exists.
  • Record who gave the judgment, their assumptions and their reasoning, so it can be checked against actual results.
  • The project manager does not have to be the expert, but stays accountable for how the advice is used.

What is expert judgment in project management?

Expert judgment is guidance based on the expertise of people with relevant education, knowledge, skill, experience or training, applied to a specific project question. It is used when you need an estimate, an assessment or a decision and the answer depends on knowledge that sits in people's heads rather than in a formula or a database.

The PMBOK Guide describes it as judgment based on expertise appropriate to the activity being performed, and notes that the expertise can come from any group or person with specialised education, knowledge, skill, experience or training. In other words, the definition is about the fit between the person and the question, not about job titles.

Expert judgment or expert judgement?

Both spellings are correct. PMI publications use judgment, so that is the form you will see in the PMBOK Guide, in PMP exam material and in most search results. British English often uses judgement outside legal contexts. We use "judgment" on this page because it matches the term readers search for and the standard they are studying.

Is expert judgment a tool or a technique?

In the PMBOK Guide Sixth Edition, which organised project management into 49 processes, expert judgment is listed as a tool and technique in many of those processes, from developing the project charter to closing the project. The Seventh Edition (2021) moved away from process detail towards principles and performance domains. The Eighth Edition, which PMI published in November 2025, brings back process guidance alongside its principles and domains and includes a separate section of tools and techniques. Whatever edition you study, the idea is the same: expert input is a technique you plan and manage, not an informal chat.

Why expert judgment matters

Most project methods need inputs that cannot be calculated from first principles. A work breakdown structure needs someone who knows what the work actually involves. A risk register needs people who have seen similar projects go wrong. A supplier evaluation needs someone who can tell a sound technical proposal from a polished one.

Expert judgment earns its place in four situations:

  • New or unusual work, where there is little historical data to draw on.
  • Early stages, such as a feasibility study, when decisions are needed before details exist.
  • Time pressure, where a structured conversation with two specialists is quicker than building a model.
  • Tacit knowledge, such as how a regulator tends to interpret a rule or why a legacy system behaves the way it does, which rarely appears in documents.

The project manager does not need to be the expert, but is accountable for finding the right expertise, asking good questions and deciding well with the answers. It is part of how project integration management brings specialist knowledge into joined-up plans.

Who counts as an expert

An expert is anyone whose knowledge is relevant to the specific question. Common sources include:

  • Project team members who have done similar work before.
  • Subject matter experts in other departments, such as finance, legal, security, procurement or operations.
  • The PMO, which may hold estimating data and lessons from past projects.
  • Experienced project managers who have delivered similar projects.
  • Suppliers and consultants, who know their own products and markets.
  • Professional bodies and industry groups, which publish standards and guidance.
  • Users, customers and sponsors, who are experts in how the work is actually done and what value looks like (see stakeholder management).

Expertise is narrow: a strong solution architect is not automatically an expert on software licensing. Use simple criteria when you choose people:

CriterionQuestion to askWarning sign
RelevanceHas this person done this specific kind of work recently?Experience is from a different technology, sector or decade
Track recordHow did their past estimates or forecasts compare with what happened?No one has ever checked
IndependenceDo they gain or lose from a particular answer?A supplier estimating its own work, or a team keen to win approval
Diversity of viewDoes the group cover technical, commercial and user angles?Every expert comes from the same team
AvailabilityCan they give the question proper time?A five-minute answer in a corridor
Tip

Keep a short expert directory for your organisation or PMO: names, areas of expertise and past projects. It saves time on every new project and makes it easier to find a second opinion.

Expert judgment techniques

How you collect expert views matters as much as whom you ask. The main techniques are summarised below, followed by notes on when to use each.

TechniqueHow it worksBest forWatch out for
One-to-one interviewsStructured questions with one expert at a timeDetailed, technical or sensitive topicsInterviewer leading the answer
Facilitated workshopsA group session with a clear agenda and facilitatorRisk identification, planning, design reviewsGroupthink and dominant voices
Delphi techniqueAnonymous rounds of estimates with shared summaries and reasons until views convergeEstimates and forecasts where seniority could sway the groupTakes several days; needs a neutral facilitator
Nominal group techniqueSilent individual ideas, round-robin sharing, discussion, then a private voteGenerating and prioritising optionsCan feel formal for small teams
Planning poker / wideband DelphiSimultaneous reveal of estimates, discuss outliers, re-estimateAgile estimation and quick team estimatesBecomes routine if outliers are not discussed
Peer reviewIndependent experts review a plan, design or estimateQuality assurance and stage gatesReviewers lacking time or context
Structured elicitation with rangesExperts give a low, most likely and high value with reasonsInputs to three-point estimates and simulationRanges that are too narrow (overconfidence)

Delphi technique

The Delphi technique was developed at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s for forecasting. A facilitator sends the same question to each expert, collects answers anonymously, then shares a summary of the range and the reasons given. Experts revise their answers in the light of the summary, and the process repeats, usually for two or three rounds. Anonymity stops a senior or confident person from setting the answer for everyone else, while the shared reasons still let the group learn from each other.

Interviews, workshops and nominal group technique

Interviews give depth: prepare questions, ask for the reasoning behind every number and finish with "what would make you change this view?" Workshops give breadth but need a facilitator who draws out quieter people and keeps estimates hidden until everyone has committed. The nominal group technique adds structure by having people write ideas silently, share them in turn and vote privately.

Planning poker

Planning poker is a team version of wideband Delphi used in Agile estimation. Everyone chooses an estimate card privately and reveals it at the same time. The people with the highest and lowest estimates explain their thinking, and the team estimates again.

Structured elicitation with ranges

Instead of asking "how long?", ask for a low, most likely and high value, and for the assumptions behind each. Ranges carry more information than a single number and feed directly into a PERT three-point estimate or a Monte Carlo simulation.

How to use expert judgment: a 7-step process

Good expert judgment follows a repeatable process. The steps below draw on the practices in PMI's book *Expert Judgment in Project Management: Narrowing the Theory-Practice Gap* (Paul S. Szwed, 2016), expressed as our own practical checklist.

  1. Frame the problem. Write down exactly what you need to know, why and by when. "Estimate the effort to migrate 40 reports to the new platform, including testing, to the nearest week" is far better than "how long will the migration take?"
  2. Plan the elicitation. Choose the technique (interviews, Delphi, workshop), the number of experts, the format of answers (single value, range, rating scale) and how you will combine them.
  3. Select the experts. Use the criteria above. For important questions, aim for three to five people with different viewpoints rather than one authority.
  4. Brief the experts. Share the same background pack with everyone: scope, assumptions, constraints, relevant data and definitions. Explain the answer format and remind them about common biases, such as anchoring on a target date.
  5. Elicit the judgments. Collect answers independently before any group discussion. Ask for reasons and key assumptions as well as the number or rating.
  6. Analyse and combine. Look at the spread. Where views differ widely, find out why: often one expert knows something the others do not. Combine using an agreed method, such as a three-point estimate, an average of the second Delphi round, or a consensus discussion.
  7. Document and communicate. Record the result, the range, the experts consulted, the assumptions and any risks raised. Share it with stakeholders and feed it into the plan, the risk register or the estimate.
Tip

If the experts disagree strongly and you cannot resolve it, do not average the difference away. Treat the disagreement as a risk or an assumption to test, and plan a quick spike, prototype or supplier check to find out who is right.

Worked example 1: Delphi estimate for a data migration

A finance team is moving 40 management reports from an old reporting tool to a new analytics platform. The project manager needs an effort estimate for the business case and has no historical data for this platform.

Setting up

The project manager frames the question as "working days of effort to rebuild, test and sign off all 40 reports", selects four experts (two report developers, a data engineer and a business analyst) and sends each the report inventory, the target platform's documentation and the definition of "signed off".

Round 1

ExpertEstimate (days)Main reason given
A15Most reports are simple tables
B25Allow time for user testing
C30Data model needs to be rebuilt first
D6012 reports use custom scripts that the new platform does not support

The facilitator shares the range (15 to 60 days), the median (27.5 days) and all four reasons without names. Expert D's point about custom scripts is new information to the others.

Round 2

ExpertEstimate (days)Change and reason
A30Up 15: had not known about the custom scripts
B35Up 10: scripts need rewriting and testing
C35Up 5: agrees with the script concern
D45Down 15: data model work can run alongside

The range has narrowed from 45 days to 15 days, so the facilitator stops after two rounds.

Turning the result into an estimate

The team uses the round 2 views as a three-point estimate: optimistic 30, most likely 35 and pessimistic 45 days.

  • PERT estimate = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (30 + 4 x 35 + 45) / 6 = 215 / 6 = 35.8 days.
  • Standard deviation = (P − O) / 6 = (45 − 30) / 6 = 2.5 days.
  • Roughly 95% of outcomes would fall within two standard deviations, so about 31 to 41 days, if the assumptions hold.

The custom-script issue is logged in the risk register with an owner, and the business case states the estimate as "36 days, likely range 31 to 41 days" with the assumptions attached. Compare this with the first-round median of 27.5 days: without the structured rounds, the estimate would probably have been too low.

Worked example 2: expert risk assessment

A project is integrating a new payments supplier. The project manager asks three experts to rate one risk independently on a 1 to 5 scale for probability and impact: "The supplier's test environment is not ready by the start of integration testing, delaying go-live."

ExpertRoleProbability (1–5)Impact (1–5)Score (P x I)Reason
E1Integration lead4416The supplier has missed two environment dates on other projects
E2Test manager3515Integration testing is on the critical path
E3Supplier manager248The supplier has committed a named environment manager this time

The scores range from 8 to 16. Rather than averaging, the project manager brings the three together for 20 minutes to compare reasons. E3's information is new and credible, but E1's history is also real. They agree on probability 3 and impact 4, a score of 12 (high on the project's scale), and two actions:

  • Ask the supplier to confirm the environment date in writing, with a check-in two weeks before.
  • Prepare a contingency: build and test against the supplier's sandbox so work can start even if the full environment is late (see contingency planning).

The assessment, the three original ratings and the reasons are stored with the risk, so the team can see later whether the experts were right.

Choosing your role: decide, delegate, consult or facilitate

Not every question needs a Delphi study. A practical way to choose, discussed by several practitioners including Mosaic Projects, is to consider how much the decision matters and whether you have the expertise yourself.

You have the relevant expertiseYou lack the relevant expertise
Low consequence, needs a quick answerDecide yourself and note the reasoningDelegate to someone who has the expertise
High consequence or plenty of timeConsult: seek independent views, then decideFacilitate: run a structured process with several experts and make the decision with the sponsor if needed

This keeps effort proportionate. A five-day task estimate inside a team does not need four rounds of anonymous voting; a contract value, a safety risk or a go-live date usually does deserve more structure.

Common biases and how to reduce them

Experts are human. The biases below are well documented in decision research, and each has a practical counter.

BiasWhat it looks likeHow to reduce it
Optimism bias / planning fallacyEstimates assume everything goes wellAsk for ranges; compare with actuals from past projects; ask "what went wrong last time?"
AnchoringEveryone converges on the first number mentioned, or on a target dateCollect estimates independently before sharing any number, including the sponsor's target
OverconfidenceRanges are far too narrowAsk for a value you would be surprised to beat, and one you would be surprised to exceed; check past ranges
Groupthink and seniorityThe group follows the most senior or loudest personUse anonymous rounds (Delphi), silent writing or simultaneous reveal
Availability biasRecent or vivid events dominateUse checklists and historical data alongside memory
Motivational biasThe expert benefits from a high or low answerUse independent experts; declare interests; seek a second opinion
Narrow expertiseOne discipline's view dominatesMix technical, commercial and user perspectives
Curse of knowledgeThe expert cannot explain the reasoning to non-specialistsAsk for assumptions in plain language; ask "what would a newcomer get wrong?"

Expert judgment vs other estimating methods

Expert judgment is often described as an estimating method in its own right, but in practice it sits underneath most other methods. The table shows how it relates to them.

MethodHow it worksRole of expert judgmentBest when
Expert judgmentSpecialists estimate directly from experienceThe whole methodWork is new or data is scarce
Analogous estimatingUses the actual cost or duration of a similar past projectExperts choose the analogy and adjust for differencesEarly stages, with a good comparable project
Parametric estimatingApplies a unit rate or model, such as cost per square metreExperts check the model fits and the parameters are rightRepeatable work with reliable data
Bottom-up estimatingEstimates each work package and adds them upEach package is estimated by the people who will do itScope is well defined
Three-point estimating (PERT)Combines optimistic, most likely and pessimistic valuesExperts supply the three valuesUncertainty matters and needs to be shown
Monte Carlo simulationRuns thousands of scenarios from input rangesExperts supply ranges and correlationsLarge, uncertain projects with many risks

Our advice: use data as the starting point and experts to adjust for what is different this time. Where you have historical data, use it; where you do not, structured expert judgment is far better than a single guess. You can test how sensitive your plan is to each expert assumption with sensitivity analysis.

Advantages and disadvantages of expert judgment

Advantages

  • Fast and inexpensive compared with building models or collecting data.
  • Works when there is no historical data, such as new technology or one-off projects.
  • Captures tacit knowledge and context that formulas miss.
  • Flexible: works for estimates, risks, design choices and supplier evaluation.

Disadvantages

  • Prone to bias, especially optimism, anchoring and group pressure.
  • Hard to verify or repeat unless the reasoning is recorded.
  • Depends on finding the right experts and on their availability.
  • Can give false confidence when an opinion is presented as a fact.

Most of the disadvantages are reduced by structure: independent answers, ranges, several experts, recorded reasoning and later comparison with actual results.

How to document expert judgment

A short record makes expert judgment auditable and helps you learn which experts and methods give good results. Capture these fields wherever the judgment is used, for example in the estimate, the risk register or the decision log.

Expert judgment in Agile teams

Agile teams use expert judgment constantly, though they rarely call it that. The team doing the work is treated as the expert on its own estimates, which is why Agile practices emphasise whole-team estimation.

  • Planning poker and affinity estimation gather the team's expertise quickly while avoiding anchoring.
  • Backlog refinement brings in specialists, such as security or UX, to judge feasibility and acceptance criteria before work starts.
  • Sprint planning relies on the team's judgment of what it can complete, balanced by velocity data (see sprint planning).
  • Retrospectives turn experience into improvements, closing the loop between judgment and results.

The same rules apply: independent estimates first, then discussion of outliers.

Frequently asked questions

What is expert judgment in project management?

It is advice based on the specialist knowledge, skills or experience of people who understand the work, used to support estimates, plans, risk assessments and decisions. The PMBOK Guide treats it as a tool and technique used across many processes.

What is an example of expert judgment?

Asking three experienced developers to estimate the effort for an integration, collecting their low, likely and high values independently, discussing the differences and combining them into a three-point estimate is a typical example. Asking a security specialist to rate a risk is another.

Is it spelled expert judgment or expert judgement?

Both are correct. PMI and the PMBOK Guide use 'judgment', which is also the more common search term. 'Judgement' is common in British English outside legal writing.

What are the main expert judgment techniques?

Interviews, facilitated workshops, the Delphi technique, nominal group technique, planning poker (a form of wideband Delphi), peer review and structured elicitation using ranges.

Is the Delphi technique a form of expert judgment?

Yes. It is one of the best-known ways to gather expert judgment. Experts answer anonymously over several rounds and see a summary of the group's views and reasons each time, which reduces the influence of dominant individuals.

What are the disadvantages of expert judgment?

It can be biased, inconsistent between experts, hard to verify and dependent on finding the right people. Structure, ranges, several experts and recorded reasoning reduce these problems.

What is the difference between expert judgment and analogous estimating?

Analogous estimating uses actual data from a similar past project, scaled for size or complexity. Expert judgment is broader: experts often choose the analogy and adjust it, but they also add opinion and interpretation where no comparable data exists.

Is expert judgment qualitative or quantitative?

It can be either. Experts may give a number, such as a duration or cost, or a qualitative view, such as whether a design meets a standard. Asking for ranges and ratings makes judgments easier to combine and compare.

How many experts should you consult?

For routine questions, one relevant expert may be enough. For high-stakes estimates or risks, three to five experts with different perspectives usually give a more reliable view without becoming hard to manage.

How should expert judgment be documented?

Record the question, the date and method, who was consulted, the result and range, the key assumptions, any disagreements or risks raised, and when you will compare the judgment with actual results.

Can AI tools provide expert judgment?

AI assistants can help you prepare questions, summarise past project data and suggest risks to consider, but they are not accountable experts. Treat their output as an input for qualified people to review, and follow your organisation's rules on sharing project data.

Is expert judgment on the PMP exam?

Yes. Scenario questions often test whether you consult subject matter experts before committing to an estimate, prefer structured methods when opinions conflict and remember that the project manager stays accountable. See our PMP certification guide.

Does the project manager have to be the expert?

No. The project manager's role is to find the right expertise, structure how it is gathered and make a sound decision with it. The project manager remains accountable for that decision.
