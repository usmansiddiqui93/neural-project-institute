What Is Project Risk? Types, Examples and Risk Statements

A project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it happens, would have a positive or negative effect on at least one project objective, such as scope, schedule, cost or quality. Negative risks are threats and positive risks are opportunities. This guide explains how to describe risks clearly, the main types with examples, and how to find them before they turn into problems.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 30 September 2026
  • Last updated 9 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • A project risk is uncertain and in the future; once it has happened it is an issue.
  • Risks can be threats (negative) or opportunities (positive), and both deserve attention.
  • Write every risk as cause, event and effect so the team can analyse it and act on it.
  • Group risks by type (schedule, cost, scope, technical, resource, external and more) to spot gaps.
  • Identify risks continuously, from the charter onwards, and record them in a risk register with an owner.

What is project risk?

Project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on one or more project objectives. PMI uses this definition in the PMBOK Guide. The key ideas are uncertainty (it may or may not happen), the future (it has not happened yet) and an effect on what the project is trying to achieve.

Those three ideas are a useful test. If something is certain, it is a fact to plan for, not a risk. If it has already happened, it is an issue. If it would not affect scope, time, cost, quality or the expected benefits, it is not worth tracking as a project risk.

  • Objectives a risk can affect: scope, schedule, cost, quality, benefits, safety, reputation or stakeholder satisfaction.
  • What a risk is not: a certainty ('the office move is in March'), a current problem ('the server is down') or a vague worry ('resourcing').
  • Why it matters: projects are temporary and unique, so they always carry uncertainty. Naming it early gives you time to respond while options are still cheap.

The most recent edition of the PMBOK Guide (the eighth, published in November 2025) keeps risk as one of its performance domains and continues to distinguish risks, which have not yet occurred, from issues, which have.

Threats and opportunities

Most people hear 'risk' and think of something bad. In project management a risk can go either way.

Threat (negative risk)Opportunity (positive risk)
Effect if it happensHarms one or more objectivesBenefits one or more objectives
ExampleA key supplier may deliver three weeks lateA new test tool may halve regression testing time
Typical responsesAvoid, mitigate, transfer, accept, escalateExploit, enhance, share, accept, escalate
Common mistakeIgnoring threats outside the team's controlNot looking for opportunities at all

Teams rarely find opportunities by accident. Ask directly in risk workshops: 'What could go better than planned, and how could we make it more likely?' Typical opportunities include reusing work from another project, a price fall, an early approval or a supplier finishing ahead of schedule.

The three parts of a risk: cause, event and effect

A well-described risk has three parts. Separating them stops teams confusing the reason for a risk with the risk itself, or with its consequence.

PartQuestion it answersExample
CauseWhat fact or condition exists now?The supplier has only one production line
EventWhat uncertain thing might happen?The line may break down during our order
EffectWhat would that do to our objectives?Delivery slips by three weeks and the launch date is missed

A simple template turns the three parts into one sentence: Because of [cause], there is a risk that [event], which would lead to [effect].

Tip

The cause is certain; the event is uncertain; the effect is what you would measure. If your 'event' is already true, you have written a cause or an issue, not a risk.

How to write a project risk statement

A risk statement is the sentence you put in the risk register. Writing it well makes scoring, ownership and responses much easier.

  1. Start with the cause. State the fact or condition that creates the uncertainty, such as a constraint, an assumption or a dependency.
  2. Describe one uncertain event. Use 'may' or 'might'. If you find two events, split them into two risks.
  3. State the effect on an objective. Make it measurable where you can: days of delay, cost, scope lost or quality affected.
  4. Check it is not an issue or a certainty. If it has happened or definitely will, handle it differently.
  5. Check someone can act on it. A good statement suggests who should own it and what a response might look like.
Poor risk statementBetter risk statement
ResourcesBecause the data engineer is also on the finance project, they may be unavailable in May, delaying the data migration by two weeks.
Budget overrunBecause hardware is priced in US dollars, a weaker pound may raise equipment costs by up to 8%, exceeding the budget.
Requirements might changeBecause the sales team has not reviewed the requirements, new features may be requested after sign-off, adding rework and scope creep.
WeatherBecause the roof works are scheduled for November, heavy rain may stop work for several days, delaying the watertight milestone.

Types of project risk

Grouping risks by type helps you check that identification has covered every area, and helps you compare risks across projects. There is no single official list; use categories that suit your organisation. The table shows a common set.

TypeTypical sourcesExample risk
ScheduleOptimistic estimates, late approvals, dependenciesPlanning permission may take longer than eight weeks, delaying the start on site
CostPrice rises, exchange rates, rework, inaccurate estimatesMaterial prices may rise before orders are placed, increasing costs
ScopeUnclear requirements, changing prioritiesStakeholders may request extra reports after design sign-off
TechnicalNew technology, integration, performanceThe new API may not handle peak volumes, causing slow response times
ResourceSkills gaps, competing projects, staff turnoverThe only specialist may leave, stalling testing
QualityDefects, unclear acceptance criteriaAcceptance criteria may be interpreted differently, causing rejected deliverables
Procurement and supplierSupplier capacity, contract terms, insolvencyA subcontractor may go out of business mid-project
Stakeholder and communicationLow engagement, conflicting interestsThe sponsor may lose interest after a reorganisation
OrganisationalFunding cuts, restructuring, competing initiativesThe budget may be cut at the mid-year review
ExternalRegulation, weather, market, political eventsNew data rules may require extra security controls

Many organisations turn their categories into a risk breakdown structure (RBS), a hierarchy of risk sources similar to a work breakdown structure. Walking through it in a workshop is a quick way to find gaps.

Individual risk and overall project risk

PMI distinguishes individual project risks, the specific events in your register, from overall project risk, the effect of all uncertainty on the project as a whole. A project can have no single critical risk and still be very risky overall because of many medium risks, ambitious targets or complexity. Overall risk is usually discussed at sponsor or board level and can be estimated with techniques such as Monte Carlo simulation.

Known and unknown risks

  • Known risks have been identified and analysed. You plan responses for them and usually cover their expected cost with a contingency reserve inside the cost baseline.
  • Unknown risks cannot be identified in advance. Organisations often cover them with a management reserve held outside the baseline and released through governance.
  • Emergent risks are those that only become visible as the project progresses, which is why identification must continue throughout.

Project risk, business risk and operational risk

Project risk affects the project's own objectives: delivering the agreed scope on time, on budget and to quality. Business risk affects the wider organisation's goals, for example whether a new product sells once it is launched. Operational risk sits in day-to-day running of the business after handover, such as a system outage. The three are linked: a project often exists to reduce a business risk, and shortcuts taken during a project can create operational risk later.

Exam guides also separate speculative risk, which can lead to a gain or a loss, from pure risk, which can only lead to a loss, such as fire or theft. Pure risks are often transferred through insurance.

Common project risks and early warning signs

Some risks appear on almost every project. Knowing their early warning signs, called triggers, helps you act before they become issues.

Common riskPossible trigger
Unclear or changing requirementsStakeholders disagree in reviews; open questions grow week by week
Optimistic estimatesEarly tasks finish late; the team reports 'nearly done' for several days
Key people unavailableRepeated meeting cancellations; people booked on other projects
Supplier delayA supplier misses an interim milestone or stops replying promptly
Scope creepSmall requests bypass change control
Technical uncertaintyPrototype results differ from expectations; performance tests fail
Weak sponsor supportDecisions are deferred; the sponsor misses steering meetings
Budget pressureSpend runs ahead of progress; estimates to complete keep rising
Low stakeholder engagementFeedback arrives late or not at all; few people attend demos

Record the trigger next to each risk so the owner knows what to watch for. Our guide to stakeholder management covers many of the people-related risks in more depth.

Risk attributes you should record

A risk statement on its own is not enough to manage the risk. Most teams record these attributes for each one.

AttributeWhat it means
ID and categoryA unique reference and the type of risk
ProbabilityHow likely the event is, on an agreed scale such as 1 to 5
ImpactHow severe the effect would be, on an agreed scale
ScoreProbability multiplied by impact, used to rank risks
ProximityWhen the risk could occur, so near-term risks get attention first
OwnerThe named person who monitors the risk and makes sure the response happens
TriggerThe warning sign that the risk is occurring
ResponseThe chosen strategy and actions, with dates
StatusOpen, monitoring, closed or occurred

Risk vs issue vs assumption vs constraint

These terms are often mixed up. Each one is handled differently, so it is worth separating them.

TermDefinitionExampleWhere it is tracked
RiskAn uncertain future event that would affect objectivesThe supplier may deliver lateRisk register
IssueSomething that has happened and needs action nowThe supplier has delivered lateIssue log
AssumptionSomething believed to be true for planning, without proofThe supplier can deliver in six weeksAssumptions log or RAID log
ConstraintA fixed limit the project must work withinThe launch date is 1 MarchCharter and plan
DependencyA link where one piece of work relies on anotherInstallation depends on the deliverySchedule or RAID log

Assumptions are one of the best sources of risk. For every assumption, ask what would happen if it proved false. If the answer is serious, log a risk. A RAID log keeps risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies together, which suits smaller projects; see our RAID log template.

How to identify project risks

Risk identification works best as a team activity that starts during initiation and continues to closure.

  1. Review the project documents. Read the project charter, scope, schedule, budget, assumptions and constraints, asking what could go differently.
  2. Challenge assumptions. For each one, ask how confident you are and what happens if it is wrong.
  3. Walk through the work. Go through the work breakdown structure and schedule, especially the critical path and external dependencies.
  4. Run a risk workshop. Bring together the team, sponsor, users and key suppliers. Brainstorm threats and opportunities by category.
  5. Use past experience. Check lessons learned and risk registers from similar projects.
  6. Ask specialists. Use expert judgment for technical, legal, safety or commercial areas.
  7. Use structured techniques. Checklists, a risk breakdown structure, SWOT analysis and root cause tools such as a fishbone diagram help teams think beyond the obvious.
  8. Record and assign. Write each risk as cause, event and effect, log it in the register and name an owner.
Tip

Interview people individually as well as in workshops. Some people will not raise concerns in front of senior stakeholders.

Identification is the first step of a wider process that includes analysis, response planning and monitoring.

Project risk in Agile projects

Agile ways of working reduce many risks by design. Short iterations, frequent demos and a prioritised backlog surface problems early and limit how much work is lost if direction changes. Risk still needs attention, though.

  • Raise risks in sprint planning, daily stand-ups and retrospectives rather than only in a separate meeting.
  • Tackle high technical risks early, for example with a time-boxed spike in an early sprint.
  • Add risk-reducing work to the backlog so it is prioritised alongside features.
  • Use a clear Definition of Done to reduce quality risk.
  • Keep a lightweight risk list or board for risks that span several sprints or depend on other teams.

Common mistakes when describing project risks

  • Writing categories instead of risks. 'Staff risk' cannot be scored or owned; 'the lead developer may leave in Q2' can.
  • Recording issues as risks. If it is already happening, move it to the issue log and act now.
  • Listing certainties. A known public holiday is a planning fact, not a risk.
  • Mixing several events in one statement. Split them so each can be scored and owned.
  • Ignoring opportunities. A register with only threats misses ways to finish sooner or cheaper.
  • Identifying risks once. New risks appear at every phase; review the list at each stage gate and when anything significant changes.
  • No owner. A risk without a named owner is rarely managed.

Frequently asked questions

What is a project risk?

A project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, would have a positive or negative effect on at least one project objective such as scope, schedule, cost or quality.

Can a project risk be positive?

Yes. Positive risks are called opportunities. Examples include a price falling, a task finishing early or a new tool reducing effort. They are managed with responses such as exploit, enhance and share.

What is the difference between a project risk and an issue?

A risk might happen in the future; an issue has already happened and needs action now. When a risk occurs it becomes an issue and is usually moved to the issue log.

What is a project risk statement?

A sentence that describes a risk's cause, uncertain event and effect, for example: 'Because of [cause], there is a risk that [event], which would lead to [effect].' It makes the risk specific enough to analyse and act on.

What are the main types of project risk?

Common types are schedule, cost, scope, technical, resource, quality, procurement, stakeholder, organisational and external risk. Risks can also be classified as threats or opportunities, known or unknown, and individual or overall.

What are the most common project risks?

Unclear or changing requirements, optimistic estimates, key people being unavailable, supplier delays, technical uncertainty, scope creep and weak sponsor support appear on many projects.

What is a risk trigger?

A warning sign that a risk is about to occur or has occurred, such as a supplier missing an interim milestone. Recording triggers helps risk owners act early.

What is overall project risk?

The effect of uncertainty on the project as a whole. It combines individual risks with other sources of variability such as complexity and ambitious targets.

What is a secondary risk?

A new risk that arises directly from carrying out a response to another risk. For example, outsourcing a task to reduce a skills risk can introduce a supplier performance risk.

What is risk tolerance in project management?

The acceptable range of variation around an objective, such as a budget overrun of no more than 5%. Risks that could push the project outside its tolerances need a response or escalation.

Who is responsible for identifying project risks?

Everyone involved. The project manager facilitates identification, but team members, the sponsor, users and suppliers often see risks the project manager cannot.

Is an assumption a project risk?

Not by itself, but an assumption that might prove false is a source of risk. Reviewing assumptions is one of the most effective ways to identify risks.

What is the difference between project risk and business risk?

Project risk affects the project's objectives, such as delivering on time and budget. Business risk, in the organisational sense, affects the wider organisation, for example whether the product sells once delivered. The two are linked, and a project can reduce or create business risk.

Where should project risks be recorded?

In a risk register, or in a RAID log on smaller projects, with an owner, score, response and status for each risk.
