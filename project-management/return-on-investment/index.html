Project ROI: Formula, Examples and How to Calculate It

Project ROI (return on investment) measures how much a project gains compared with what it costs: ROI = (total benefits − total costs) ÷ total costs × 100, over a stated period. A positive figure means the benefits exceed the costs. This guide shows you which costs and benefits to include, works through a three-year example step by step, and explains how to adjust ROI for timing and risk so you can use it in a business case with confidence.

Key takeaways

  • Project ROI = net benefit ÷ cost × 100. Always state the period (for example, three-year ROI) and whether the denominator is total cost or the initial investment, because the two give different answers.
  • Include every cost, including running costs and internal staff time, and count only incremental benefits: those that would not happen without the project.
  • Simple ROI ignores when money arrives. Use annualised ROI to compare projects of different lengths and NPV to measure value in today's money.
  • Test the result. A sensitivity check (benefits 20% lower, costs 15% higher, a six-month delay) often matters more to a sponsor than the headline percentage.
  • There is no universal good ROI. Compare it with your organisation's hurdle rate, the alternatives and the risk of the benefits.
  • ROI estimated in the business case is a forecast. Measure actual ROI after delivery through benefits realisation, and feed the lessons into the next appraisal.

What is ROI in project management?

Return on investment (ROI) in project management is the net benefit a project delivers, divided by its cost and expressed as a percentage, over a defined period. It tells decision-makers how much they gain for each dollar invested. Teams estimate it in the business case to justify and rank projects, then measure it after delivery to confirm the benefits.

ROI is popular because it is simple and easy to explain: a 25% three-year ROI means the project returns its cost plus a further quarter of that cost over three years. Sponsors and portfolio boards use it to decide whether a project is worth doing, which project to fund first and, after delivery, whether the promised benefits arrived.

ROI usually sits inside a wider cost-benefit analysis and is reported in the business case alongside net present value (NPV), payback period and sometimes internal rate of return (IRR). PMI's PMBOK Guide treats the business case and benefits management plan as key inputs to starting a project. A feasibility study often produces the first ROI estimate, and the approved figure is summarised in the project charter.

The project ROI formula

The standard formula is:

ROI = (total benefits − total costs) ÷ total costs × 100

The top line, total benefits minus total costs, is the net benefit (sometimes called net gain or net return). Reading the result is straightforward:

  • Positive ROI: benefits exceed costs. A 20% ROI means you get back your cost plus 20% of it.
  • Zero ROI: the project breaks even over the period.
  • Negative ROI: the project costs more than it returns over the period. −10% means you recover only 90% of what you spend.
TermMeaningExample
Total benefitsAll the incremental money the project brings in or saves over the period$230,000 of savings over three years
Total costsAll the money and internal effort the project consumes over the same period$195,000 of build and running costs
Net benefitTotal benefits minus total costs$230,000 − $195,000 = $35,000
ROINet benefit ÷ total costs × 100$35,000 ÷ $195,000 × 100 = 17.9%

Always state the period

ROI has no built-in time unit. A 30% ROI over one year is far better than 30% over five years, so always label the figure: "three-year ROI", "ROI over the five-year life of the system". Use the same period for every project you compare, or convert to annualised ROI (covered below).

Which costs go in the denominator?

Organisations use two conventions, and they give different answers from the same numbers:

  • Total-cost basis: net benefit ÷ all costs over the period (one-off and running). This is the form used in this guide.
  • Initial-investment basis: treat running costs as negative benefits, then divide the net benefit by the up-front investment only. This produces a higher percentage because the denominator is smaller.

Neither is wrong, but mixing them makes comparisons meaningless. Check which convention your finance team uses, write it in the business case and apply it to every option.

Tip

If someone quotes an ROI figure, ask: over what period, and divided by what? The answers often explain why their number looks better than yours.

What to include: costs and benefits

Most ROI errors come from leaving costs out or counting benefits that are not real. Your cost estimation and project budget are the starting point for the cost side.

Cost categoryExamplesOften forgotten?
One-off delivery costsSupplier fees, hardware, software set-up, contractors, consultantsRarely
Internal staff timeProject team, subject-matter experts, testers, managers' time on steering groupsOften: it is not a new payment, but it has a cost
Training and changeCourse fees, time away from normal work, communications, temporary backfillOften
Running costsSubscriptions, licences, support, maintenance, hosting, extra headcountOften, especially beyond year one
Risk allowancesContingency for identified risks, rework, compliance checksSometimes
End-of-life costsDecommissioning the old system, data archiving, exit feesOften
Benefit categoryExamplesHow firm?
New or protected revenueAdditional sales, higher prices, retained customersDepends on market evidence
Cash savingsLower supplier spend, cancelled licences, reduced overtime or temporary staffFirm if budgets are actually reduced
Avoided costsFines, penalties, failure costs or planned hires that will no longer be neededFirm if the avoided cost was genuinely going to happen
Productivity gainsHours saved per person per weekSoft until the time is redeployed or the cost removed
Strategic and intangibleCustomer satisfaction, brand, staff engagement, reduced riskHard to value; usually described, not counted

Hard and soft benefits

Hard benefits change the organisation's cash position: a budget line goes down or revenue goes up. Soft benefits are real improvements that do not automatically become cash, such as an hour saved per person per week; unless that time is redeployed or a cost removed, it never appears in the accounts. Calculate ROI on hard benefits and show soft benefits separately, with a plan for realising them.

Count only incremental benefits

Compare the future with the project against the future without it, not against today. If sales were going to grow 5% anyway, only growth above that counts. Equally, if the old system would have needed a $50,000 upgrade next year, the avoided upgrade is a genuine benefit of replacing it.

How to calculate project ROI step by step

Use this process for a business case, a project selection exercise or a post-project review.

  1. Define what you are appraising. Write down the option, the baseline (what happens if you do nothing) and the appraisal period, usually the useful life of the deliverable, often three to five years.
  2. Agree the rules with finance. Confirm the ROI convention (total cost or initial investment), the discount rate for NPV, how internal staff time is costed and whether inflation is included.
  3. List every cost by year. Separate one-off costs (year 0) from running costs (years 1 onwards). Include internal time, training and end-of-life costs.
  4. Estimate incremental benefits by year. Allow for ramp-up: benefits rarely arrive in full on day one. Mark each benefit as hard or soft and name an owner who will be accountable for realising it.
  5. Calculate net benefit and ROI. Total the benefits and costs, subtract and divide. Also record the net cash flow for each year, because you will need it for payback and NPV.
  6. Add timing measures. Calculate payback period, annualised ROI and NPV so that readers can see when the money arrives, not only how much.
  7. Test the assumptions. Recalculate with lower benefits, higher costs and a delay. Note which assumption moves the result most.
  8. Report and track. Put the figures, assumptions and sensitivities in the business case. After delivery, measure actual costs and benefits against the same baseline.

Worked example: three-year ROI for an automation project

The following example is illustrative. The company and figures are invented to show the method.

Scenario. A mid-sized distributor wants to automate its accounts payable process. A supplier will configure invoice-scanning and approval software. The finance director asks for a three-year ROI on a total-cost basis.

Step 1: costs

  • Year 0 (one-off): $120,000. Implementation partner $60,000; internal staff time 800 hours at a loaded rate of $50 an hour, $40,000; training $8,000; data migration and contingency $12,000.
  • Years 1–3 (running): $25,000 a year. Software subscription $18,000 and part-time system administration $7,000.

Step 2: hard benefits

  • Reduced temporary staff: the team no longer needs agency staff at month-end, saving $60,000 a year once fully running ($40,000 in year 1 while the new process beds in).
  • Early-payment discounts: faster approvals let the company take suppliers' prompt-payment discounts worth $20,000 a year ($15,000 in year 1).
  • Late-payment penalties avoided: $5,000 a year.

Managers also expect to save around 600 hours a year of approval chasing. That is a soft benefit, so it is recorded but not counted in the ROI.

Step 3: lay out the cash flows

Year 0Year 1Year 2Year 3Total
Costs$120,000$25,000$25,000$25,000$195,000
Hard benefits$0$60,000$85,000$85,000$230,000
Net cash flow−$120,000$35,000$60,000$60,000$35,000
Cumulative−$120,000−$85,000−$25,000$35,000

Step 4: calculate

  • Net benefit = $230,000 − $195,000 = $35,000.
  • Three-year ROI (total-cost basis) = $35,000 ÷ $195,000 × 100 = 17.9%.
  • Three-year ROI (initial-investment basis) = $35,000 ÷ $120,000 × 100 = 29.2%. Same project, same cash, a different figure: this is why the convention must be stated.
  • Payback period = 2 + ($25,000 ÷ $60,000) = about 2.4 years. The project recovers its costs part-way through year 3.

Step 5: interpret

The project pays for itself within three years and returns 17.9% on everything spent: positive but not large, with a late payback that leans on year 3. Counting the 600 manager hours a year at $50 an hour would lift ROI to 64%, but a sponsor who approves on 64% will be disappointed when the accounts show 17.9%. Report 17.9% and describe the time saving, and what managers will do with it, as an additional benefit.

Tip

Show the year-by-year table, not just the final percentage. Sponsors can see at a glance when the money goes out, when it comes back and how much depends on later years.

Taking timing into account: annualised and discounted ROI

Simple ROI treats a dollar in year 3 as worth the same as a dollar today. Two adjustments fix that.

Annualised ROI

Annualised ROI converts a multi-year ROI into an equivalent yearly rate, so you can compare projects of different lengths:

Annualised ROI = (1 + ROI)^(1 ÷ n) − 1, where ROI is a decimal and n is the number of years.

For the automation project: (1 + 0.179)^(1 ÷ 3) − 1 = about 5.7% a year. Dividing 17.9% by three (6.0%) gives a slightly higher and less accurate answer because it ignores compounding; the gap widens as ROI and the number of years increase.

Discounted ROI and NPV

Discounting converts each year's cash into today's money using a discount rate set by finance, usually the organisation's cost of capital or hurdle rate. At an 8% discount rate, the automation project's figures become:

Year 0Year 1Year 2Year 3Total
Net cash flow−$120,000$35,000$60,000$60,000$35,000
Discount factor at 8%1.0000.9260.8570.794
Present value−$120,000$32,407$51,440$47,630$11,478 (NPV)
  • NPV = about $11,500. The project still creates value after allowing for the cost of money, but less than half the undiscounted $35,000.
  • Discounted ROI = (present value of benefits − present value of costs) ÷ present value of costs = ($195,905 − $184,427) ÷ $184,427 = about 6.2% over three years.
  • Discounted payback = about 2.8 years, against 2.4 years undiscounted.
  • IRR = about 12.8%, the discount rate at which NPV would be zero. It is above the 8% rate, which confirms the positive NPV.

The undiscounted ROI of 17.9% and the discounted ROI of 6.2% describe the same project. The difference is the cost of waiting for the money, and it is why most finance teams treat NPV as the headline measure and ROI as a supporting one. Comparing projects with ROI

ROI is often used to rank competing projects in a portfolio. The table shows three illustrative projects competing for the same budget.

ProjectCostNet benefitPeriodSimple ROIAnnualised ROI
A: process improvement$50,000$20,0001 year40%40.0%
B: new customer platform$200,000$120,0003 years60%17.0%
C: reporting upgrade$80,000$40,0002 years50%22.5%

Each measure tells a different story:

  • Simple ROI ranks B first, but it is measured over three years against one year for A, so it is not a fair comparison.
  • Annualised ROI ranks A first: it earns the highest rate of return per year on the money tied up.
  • Net benefit (and NPV, once discounted) ranks B first: it creates the most value in total, because it is the largest project.

The right choice depends on what is scarce. If budget is tight and A can be followed by other projects, the rate of return matters; if the organisation can fund B and wants the greatest total value, NPV matters more. Portfolio boards also weigh strategic fit, risk and capacity, which is why portfolio management rarely relies on a single number.

Testing the result: sensitivity and risk

Every ROI rests on forecasts. Before you present it, show how the result changes if the forecasts are wrong. For the automation project:

ScenarioTotal benefitsTotal costsNet benefitThree-year ROI
Base case$230,000$195,000$35,00017.9%
Benefits 20% lower$184,000$195,000−$11,000−5.6%
Costs 15% higher$230,000$224,250$5,7502.6%
Go-live six months late (year 1 benefits halved)$200,000$195,000$5,0002.6%
Benefits 20% lower and costs 15% higher$184,000$224,250−$40,250−17.9%

The return is thin and most sensitive to the benefits estimate. Before approval, the sponsor should test the temporary-staff saving and ask whether a phased rollout could bring benefits forward. Our guide to sensitivity analysis shows how to find the break-even point for each assumption.

Expected-value (risk-adjusted) ROI

When a benefit is uncertain, weight it by probability. Suppose the team judges there is a 70% chance of the full $230,000 and a 30% chance that only half ($115,000) arrives. Expected benefits = (0.7 × $230,000) + (0.3 × $115,000) = $195,500. Expected ROI = ($195,500 − $195,000) ÷ $195,000 = about 0.3%: roughly break-even. A positive base-case ROI can hide a project that is a coin toss once risk is considered.

For larger investments, a Monte Carlo simulation produces a range of ROI outcomes and the probability of a loss. Record the main threats to the benefits in your project risk management process so they are owned and monitored.

ROI compared with other financial measures

No single measure answers every question. Most business cases report three or four together.

MeasureQuestion it answersAccounts for timing?Best used for
ROIHow much do we gain per dollar spent over the period?NoA simple, intuitive summary
Annualised ROIWhat is the equivalent yearly rate of return?Partly (compounding, not discounting)Comparing projects of different lengths
Payback periodHow quickly do we get our money back?Partly; discounted payback doesJudging liquidity and exposure
Net present value (NPV)How much value does the project create in today's money?YesThe headline measure of value; choosing between options
Internal rate of return (IRR)What rate of return does the project earn?YesComparing with a hurdle rate
Benefit-cost ratio (BCR)How many dollars of benefit per dollar of cost?Usually yes (uses present values)Public-sector and cost-benefit appraisals

ROI and BCR are closely related: on undiscounted totals, BCR = 1 + ROI (as a decimal), so a BCR of 1.18 corresponds to an ROI of 18%. See our cost-benefit analysis guide for BCR in a full appraisal.

What is a good ROI for a project?

There is no single figure that makes an ROI good. Judge it against four things:

  • Your hurdle rate. Many organisations set a minimum acceptable rate of return, often called the hurdle rate, based on the cost of capital plus a margin for risk. Compare annualised ROI or IRR with it, not the multi-year figure.
  • The alternatives. A 15% ROI is good if the next-best use of the money returns 5%, and poor if a similar project returns 40%.
  • The risk of the benefits. A modest ROI on firm cash savings can be worth more than a high ROI on speculative revenue.
  • The scale. A high percentage on a small project may create less total value than a lower one on a large project.

Some projects go ahead with a low or negative financial ROI because they are mandatory or strategic: regulatory compliance, safety, replacing an unsupported system or entering a new market. For these, ROI helps you choose the cheapest way to meet the requirement rather than deciding whether to act. Make the reason explicit in the business case and link the project to its objectives and critical success factors.

Anticipated vs actual ROI

Anticipated ROI is the forecast in the business case. Actual ROI (sometimes called realised ROI) is calculated after delivery from real costs and measured benefits.

Anticipated ROIActual ROI
WhenBefore approval, refreshed at major decision pointsAfter delivery, once benefits have had time to appear
Based onEstimates, quotes and assumptionsActual costs from the project and finance systems; measured benefits
PurposeDecide whether to start and how to rank the projectConfirm the benefits, hold owners to account, improve future estimates
Typical ownerProject sponsor with the project manager and financeBenefit owners in the business, often supported by a PMO

Actual costs are usually easy to obtain from cost control and earned value management reports. Benefits are harder, because many arrive after the project has closed and the team has moved on. To make actual ROI measurable:

  1. Record the baseline before the project starts: current costs, volumes, error rates or revenue.
  2. Name an owner for each benefit in the business, not in the project team.
  3. Agree how and when each benefit will be measured, for example quarterly for two years.
  4. At project closure, hand the benefits tracking over formally and schedule the first review.
  5. Compare actual with anticipated ROI and record the differences in your lessons learned, so the next business case starts from better estimates.

Refresh the anticipated ROI whenever costs, scope or the benefits case change significantly. A project whose ROI has fallen below the hurdle rate is a candidate for re-scoping or stopping.

Common ROI mistakes

These errors appear in business cases again and again. Each one makes the ROI look better than it will turn out to be.

  • Leaving out running costs. Subscriptions, support and maintenance over the appraisal period often exceed the build cost.
  • Ignoring internal staff time. People's time has a cost even when no invoice is raised.
  • Counting soft benefits as cash. Hours saved are not dollars saved unless the time is redeployed or the cost removed.
  • Claiming benefits that would happen anyway. Compare with the do-nothing baseline, not with today.
  • Relying on ROI alone. Without NPV or payback, a project whose benefits arrive late looks as good as one that pays back quickly.
  • Presenting one number with no sensitivity. Decision-makers need to know how fragile the result is.
  • Never measuring actual ROI. Without a post-delivery check, optimistic estimates go unchallenged and repeat in the next business case.

Frequently asked questions

What is ROI in project management?

Return on investment is the net benefit of a project (total benefits minus total costs) divided by its total cost and expressed as a percentage over a stated period. It is estimated in the business case and measured again after delivery.

What is the ROI formula?

ROI = (total benefits − total costs) ÷ total costs × 100. For example, $230,000 of benefits against $195,000 of costs gives ($35,000 ÷ $195,000) × 100 = 17.9%.

What is a good ROI for a project?

It depends on your organisation's hurdle rate, the alternatives and the risk of the benefits. Compare annualised ROI or IRR with the hurdle rate set by finance, and look at NPV for the total value created.

Can ROI be negative?

Yes. A negative ROI means the project costs more than it returns over the period measured. Mandatory projects, such as compliance work, sometimes go ahead with a negative financial ROI; in that case use ROI to compare ways of meeting the requirement.

What is the difference between ROI and NPV?

ROI is a percentage that, in its simple form, ignores when money arrives. NPV is a money value that discounts each year's cash flow to today's money. NPV is the better measure of value; ROI is easier to explain.

What is annualised ROI?

The equivalent yearly rate of a multi-year ROI: (1 + ROI)^(1 ÷ years) − 1. A three-year ROI of 17.9% is about 5.7% a year. Use it to compare projects that run for different periods.

How do you measure ROI for internal projects?

Value the cash savings, avoided costs and any revenue effect against a do-nothing baseline. Treat time savings as soft benefits unless the time is redeployed or the cost removed, and agree how each benefit will be measured after delivery.

Should internal staff time be included in ROI?

Yes. Staff time has a cost even though it is not a new payment. Use the loaded hourly rate your finance team provides, so that internal and external costs are treated consistently.

How do you include intangible benefits in ROI?

Usually you do not put them in the calculation. Describe them alongside the ROI, explain how they will be measured (for example, customer satisfaction scores) and only convert them to money where there is a credible, agreed method.

When should ROI be measured?

Estimate it in the business case, refresh it at major decision points during the project, and measure actual ROI after delivery once benefits have had time to appear, often over one to three years.

Is ROI covered in the PMP exam?

Project selection measures such as ROI, NPV, IRR, payback and benefit-cost ratio commonly appear in PMP and CAPM preparation materials, usually at a conceptual level. Check PMI's current exam content outline for the exact scope; our PMP certification guide summarises the exam.
