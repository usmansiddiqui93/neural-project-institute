Project Management Office (PMO): Types, Roles and Setup

A project management office (PMO) is a team or function that sets the standards for how projects are run and supports, monitors or directly manages them, so that an organisation delivers its projects more consistently and can see all of them in one place. This guide explains the types and levels of PMO, the services and roles inside one, how to set a PMO up step by step, and how to prove it is worth the cost.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 4 October 2026
  • Last updated 4 November 2026
  • 18 min read

Key takeaways

  • A PMO standardises, supports and oversees project delivery across a department or a whole organisation.
  • Supportive, controlling and directive PMOs differ in how much authority they hold over projects.
  • PMOs also differ by level: project or programme office, departmental PMO, enterprise PMO and centre of excellence.
  • Start with a short list of services that solve a real problem, prove their value, then expand.
  • Measure the PMO by delivery outcomes, such as forecast accuracy and benefits, not by the number of reports it collects.
  • Most PMO failures come from adding bureaucracy without giving project managers anything useful back.

What is a project management office (PMO)?

A project management office (PMO) is a team or organisational function that defines how projects are managed and supports, monitors or directly runs them. It provides common methods, templates, reporting, governance and expertise, so projects are delivered more consistently and leaders can see the health of all project work in one place.

The letters PMO can also stand for a programme management office (supporting one large programme) or a portfolio management office (supporting decisions about which projects to fund). The functions overlap, and many organisations simply call all of them "the PMO". The differences are covered in the levels section below and in our guide to programme vs project vs portfolio management.

PMOs are common in medium and large organisations. CIO.com, citing PM Solutions' 2022 State of the PMO research, reports that nearly three-quarters of midsize and large companies have one. The PMBOK Guide 8th edition, published by PMI in November 2025, includes an appendix on project management offices that covers PMO types, models, structure and maturity, and describes the PMO moving from a process-focused unit that standardises methods and tools towards a partner tailored to the organisation's strategy.

What does a PMO do?

What a PMO does depends on the problem it was set up to solve, but most PMO services fall into eight areas. Few PMOs offer all of them, and a new PMO should not try to.

Service areaTypical activitiesWhat good looks like
GovernanceStage gates, approval workflows, assurance reviews, decision logsDecisions are made at the right level, quickly, with clear criteria. See project governance
Standards and methodsLifecycle, templates, tailoring rules, guidanceA light, documented method that teams actually follow
ReportingStatus reports, portfolio dashboards, RAG ratings, executive packsOne version of the truth, produced from live data, not retyped
Portfolio supportIntake, prioritisation, business case review, roadmapLeaders can see what is running, what is queued and why
Resources and capacityCapacity plans, allocation across projects, conflict resolutionClashes for key people spotted weeks before they cause delays
CapabilityTraining, coaching, onboarding, community of practiceProject managers improve and new starters ramp up quickly
ToolsAdministering the project management platform, templates, integrationsData is entered once and reused for every report
KnowledgeLessons learned, estimating data, risk librariesLessons from one project change how the next one is planned

A typical month in a PMO

To make this concrete, here is what an illustrative month looks like for a three-person PMO supporting around 30 projects:

WeekMain activities
Week 1Collect status updates, check data quality, publish the portfolio dashboard, flag projects with red or worsening ratings
Week 2Portfolio review meeting with executives; prepare decision papers for new requests and change requests; update the capacity plan
Week 3Stage gate reviews for projects reaching a decision point; coaching sessions; one assurance health check
Week 4Lessons learned workshop for a closing project; improve one template or process; plan next month's priorities

The PMO also answers ad hoc questions, maintains the RAID log for cross-project risks and dependencies, and keeps the tool configuration tidy.

Types of PMO: supportive, controlling and directive

The best-known classification groups PMOs by how much control they have over projects. It was popularised by earlier editions of the PMBOK Guide and remains the standard answer in exams and job interviews.

SupportiveControllingDirective
Level of controlLowMediumHigh
Main roleAdvise and provide resourcesSet standards and check complianceManage projects directly
Who project managers report toTheir business unitTheir business unit, with PMO oversightThe PMO
Typical servicesTemplates, training, lessons learned, coachingMandatory methods, reviews, reporting, stage gatesStaffing, running and closing projects
Best forOrganisations with capable teams that want consistency without central controlRegulated or high-risk work, or organisations with uneven deliveryStrategic, high-value portfolios where central accountability is needed
Main riskBeing ignored because nothing is requiredBeing seen as bureaucracyCost, and becoming a bottleneck

Supportive PMO

A supportive PMO acts as a consultant and library. It offers templates, training, expertise and lessons from previous projects, but does not insist that anyone use them. It works well where project managers are experienced and the main problem is inconsistency, or as a first step in an organisation wary of central control. Its weakness is that its influence depends entirely on being useful.

Controlling PMO

A controlling PMO requires compliance. Projects must use agreed methods, templates and tools, submit regular reports and pass reviews or stage gates. It suits regulated industries, organisations with repeated delivery problems and portfolios where executives need reliable data. To avoid becoming pure overhead, a controlling PMO should tailor its rules to project size and risk.

Directive PMO

A directive PMO manages projects itself. Project managers are employed by, or report to, the PMO and are assigned to projects as needed. This gives the most consistency and the clearest accountability, and suits organisations where projects are the main way strategy is delivered. It is also the most expensive model and needs strong leadership to avoid becoming a bottleneck.

How to choose the right type

Answer these questions honestly before you decide:

  1. What problem are we solving? Poor visibility usually needs a reporting-led supportive or controlling PMO; repeated failure of strategic projects may need a directive one.
  2. How mature is project delivery today? Teams with little experience need more support and more structure.
  3. How much regulation or risk is involved? Audit, safety and financial controls push towards a controlling model.
  4. What will executives actually back? A directive PMO without visible executive support will fail.
  5. What can we afford? Each step up in control adds people and process.

Many PMOs are hybrids: directive for a handful of strategic projects, controlling for large ones and supportive for everything else.

PMO levels and structures

A second way to classify PMOs is by the scope of work they cover. An organisation can have several of these at once.

LevelScopeTypical focusLifespan
Project officeOne large projectPlanning, scheduling, reporting, document controlTemporary
Programme officeOne programme of related projectsDependencies, benefits, programme reportingTemporary
Departmental PMOAll projects in a function, such as IT, engineering or marketingStandards, reporting and resourcing for that functionPermanent
Enterprise PMO (EPMO)All projects across the organisationStrategic alignment, portfolio decisions, enterprise reportingPermanent
Portfolio officeThe investment portfolioPrioritisation, funding, capacity, benefits trackingPermanent
Centre of excellenceMethods and capability across all levelsStandards, training, tools, continuous improvementPermanent

The P3O (Portfolio, Programme and Project Offices) guidance, now owned by the PeopleCert group, uses a similar model: portfolio, programme and project offices, supported by a centre of excellence that owns standards and capability.

PMO roles and responsibilities

Job titles vary, but most PMOs are built from the roles below. A small PMO may combine several of them in one person.

RoleTypical responsibilitiesUseful background
Head of PMO or PMO directorSets the PMO's direction, reports to executives, owns the PMO's value and budgetSenior delivery or programme management, stakeholder skills
PMO managerRuns PMO services day to day, manages PMO staff, improves processesProject management, process design
Portfolio managerRuns intake and prioritisation, balances the portfolio against capacity and strategyBusiness analysis, finance, strategy
PMO analystCollects and checks data, builds dashboards and reports, supports governanceData and spreadsheet skills, attention to detail
Planner or schedulerBuilds and maintains integrated schedules, critical path and dependency analysisScheduling tools and techniques
PMO coordinator or support officerAdministration, document control, meeting support, onboardingOrganisation and communication; a common entry role
Project and programme managersIn a directive PMO, deliver projects and report to the PMODelivery experience and certification

The PMO coordinator role overlaps with the project coordinator role, and many people move between them.

How big should a PMO be?

There is no reliable universal ratio of PMO staff to projects; it depends on the services offered and how much is automated. Size the PMO from its service catalogue instead: estimate the hours each service needs per month, add time for improvement work and convert to people using full-time equivalents (FTE). A reporting-only PMO with good tooling can be very lean; a directive PMO that employs the project managers is much larger.

How to set up a PMO step by step

  1. Define the problem. Interview executives, sponsors and project managers. Write down the two or three problems the PMO must solve, such as "we cannot see which projects are late" or "the same specialists are booked on four projects at once".
  2. Find a sponsor. A senior executive must own the PMO, defend its mandate and attend its key forums. Without one, a PMO cannot enforce anything.
  3. Agree the type, level and scope. Decide whether the PMO is supportive, controlling or directive, which projects it covers, and which it does not. Many start with projects above a size or risk threshold.
  4. Design a short service catalogue. Choose three to five services that address the problems from step 1. Write what each service provides, who it is for and how often.
  5. Write a PMO charter. Record purpose, scope, services, authority, roles, funding and success measures, and have the sponsor approve it. Our project charter template is a good starting structure.
  6. Set minimum standards. Agree a light lifecycle, a one-page status report, a risk register format and a change control route. Scale them by project size so small projects are not overloaded.
  7. Choose one shared tool. Put all in-scope projects in one platform so data is entered once. See how to choose project management software.
  8. Set the reporting and governance rhythm. Fix dates for status updates, the portfolio review and stage gates, and publish them for the year.
  9. Pilot with friendly projects. Test templates and reports with two or three willing project managers, then fix what does not work.
  10. Roll out and support. Train project managers, offer drop-in help and show them what they get back.
  11. Measure and publish results. Track the KPIs agreed in the charter and report them to the sponsor every quarter.
  12. Expand deliberately. Add services only when the current ones run well and there is demand.

A 90-day plan for a new PMO

PeriodGoalsOutputs
Days 1-30: understandInterview stakeholders, list all projects, assess current practiceProject inventory, problem statement, draft charter
Days 31-60: design and pilotAgree services, standards and tool; pilot reportingApproved charter, status template, first draft dashboard
Days 61-90: launchRoll out to all in-scope projects; first portfolio reviewLive dashboard, governance calendar, first quick wins reported

What to include in a PMO charter

  • Purpose and the problems the PMO will solve
  • Sponsor and reporting line
  • Type (supportive, controlling or directive) and scope: which projects, departments and thresholds
  • Service catalogue with service levels, such as "dashboard published by the fifth working day"
  • Authority: what the PMO can require, approve or stop
  • Roles, staffing and budget
  • Success measures and how often they are reported
  • Review date for the charter itself
Tip

Write the charter in the language of the problem, not of process. "Executives will see the status of every project over £100,000 on one page by the fifth working day of each month" wins more support than "implement portfolio reporting standards".

Worked example: a new PMO in a 40-project organisation

This example is illustrative. A professional services firm runs about 40 internal and client projects at any time. There is no shared tool, status reports arrive in five different formats, and the leadership team learns about problems only when a client complains.

Quarter 1: visibility

  • The operations director sponsors a two-person supportive PMO: a PMO manager and a PMO analyst.
  • The charter names two problems: no single view of project health, and specialists double-booked across projects.
  • The PMO builds a project inventory (it finds 46 projects, not 40) and introduces a one-page status template with RAG ratings, forecast finish date and top three risks.
  • All projects move into one work management platform; the analyst builds a portfolio dashboard from live data.
  • The first monthly portfolio review takes place in week 10.

Quarter 2: capacity and decisions

  • A simple capacity planning sheet shows that two senior engineers are allocated at about 180% of their time over the next eight weeks.
  • The leadership team delays one internal project by six weeks to free them, and records the decision.
  • Two projects with overlapping scope are merged into one.
  • A light stage gate is introduced for new projects above an agreed budget, using a short business case template.

Results after two quarters

MeasureStartAfter 6 months
Projects with a current status reportUnknown (no central list)All 46
Time to produce the executive portfolio packAbout 3 days of manual collationHalf a day, mostly review
Resource conflicts identified in advanceNone tracked7, of which 5 resolved before they caused delay
Projects stopped or merged after review03

The figures are illustrative, but the pattern is typical: the first wins come from simply seeing all the work in one place. Only after that does the PMO earn the authority to introduce stage gates and move towards a controlling model for larger projects.

PMO KPIs and how to measure value

A PMO costs money, so it must show what it returns. Choose a few measures linked to the problems in the charter, set a baseline and report the trend.

KPIHow to measure itWhy it matters
On-time delivery% of projects finishing within an agreed tolerance of the baselined dateThe most visible sign of delivery reliability
Forecast accuracyGap between forecast and actual finish dates or costsShows whether reports can be trusted
Schedule and cost performanceSPI and CPI from earned value managementObjective view of progress against plan
Benefits realisedBenefits achieved vs the business caseLinks projects to business value
Portfolio alignment% of spend on projects linked to a strategic objectiveShows the PMO is steering, not just counting
Decision speedDays from request to approval or rejectionSlow governance is a common complaint
Resource conflicts resolved earlyNumber of clashes flagged and resolved before causing delayDirect evidence of preventive value
Stakeholder satisfactionShort survey of executives and project managersCaptures whether the PMO is seen as useful

Avoid vanity measures such as the number of templates published or reports collected: they show activity, not value.

PMO maturity: from administrator to strategic partner

PMOs usually grow through recognisable stages. Several PMO maturity models exist, and the PMBOK Guide 8th edition appendix on PMOs discusses maturity models; the simplified view below is a practical summary rather than any one official model.

StageWhat the PMO doesTypical evidenceNext step
1. AdministrativeCollects reports and maintains templatesStatus reports exist but are inconsistentStandardise reporting and data
2. StandardisingSets common methods and reportingOne status format, one tool, regular portfolio reviewAdd governance and capacity planning
3. ControllingRuns governance, stage gates and assuranceDecisions documented; struggling projects spotted earlyLink projects to strategy and benefits
4. Portfolio-drivenSupports prioritisation and funding decisionsProjects stopped or reprioritised based on dataTrack benefits and improve continuously
5. Strategic partnerShapes strategy execution with leadershipPMO leader part of planning and investment forumsSustain, adapt to new ways of working

Do not try to jump stages. A PMO that introduces portfolio prioritisation before it has reliable project data will base decisions on guesses.

Why PMOs fail and how to avoid it

Failure modeWhat it looks likeHow to avoid it
Bureaucracy without valueProject managers fill in forms and get nothing backGive something back for every request: help with risks, resources or escalations
No executive sponsorRules are ignored with no consequenceSecure a named sponsor before launch and involve them in portfolio reviews
Unclear purposeThe PMO tries to do everything at onceLimit the service catalogue to the problems in the charter
One-size-fits-all processSmall projects buried in paperworkTailor standards by project size, risk and delivery approach
Reporting the pastReports explain what went wrong last monthReport forecasts, trends and decisions needed
Tool before processAn expensive platform nobody uses consistentlyAgree the minimum data and process first, then configure the tool
Not measuring itselfThe PMO cannot answer "what have you done for us?"Baseline KPIs at launch and report quarterly

Most of these share one root cause: the PMO was set up to control rather than to help. Effective PMOs behave like a service, with project managers as their main customers.

PMO tools and software

A PMO needs one place where project data lives, so reports can be generated rather than assembled by hand. Most PMOs use a work or portfolio management platform plus a reporting tool. Options from our reviews:

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool (9.4/10), combines projects, request intake, workflows, resource planning and dashboards that pull data from across projects; its top plans add deeper portfolio, financial and resource analysis aimed at PMO-level insight.
  • Smartsheet offers a spreadsheet-style grid with Gantt charts, dependencies and roll-up reporting, and its scheduling depth is a key reason PMOs choose it.
  • monday.com is easy to adopt, with dashboards across boards and prebuilt AI agents for PMO tasks such as project intake, status reporting and portfolio health; portfolio and resource management arrive on the Pro and Enterprise plans.
  • Asana connects projects to portfolios and goals on its Advanced plan, which suits organisations coordinating work across many teams.
  • Microsoft Planner Plan 3 adds portfolios, baselines and the critical path for organisations already on Microsoft 365.

PMO careers and certification

PMO work suits people who like organisation, data and seeing the bigger picture. A common path is PMO coordinator or support officer, then PMO analyst, then PMO manager or portfolio manager. Many PMO leaders also have project or programme management experience.

PMI Project Management Office Certified Professional (PMI-PMOCP)PMIDedicated PMO credential. Requires a secondary degree, 3 years of project-related experience in the last 8 years or a current PMP, and 10 hours of PMO education; the exam has 120 questions in 165 minutes

Frequently asked questions

What is a PMO in project management?

A project management office: a team or function that sets standards for how projects are run and supports, monitors or directly manages them across a department or organisation.

What does PMO stand for?

Usually project management office. It can also mean programme management office or portfolio management office, depending on what the office supports.

What are the three types of PMO?

Supportive (low control: templates, training and advice), controlling (medium control: required methods, reporting and reviews) and directive (high control: the PMO manages projects directly).

What does a PMO do day to day?

Collects and checks project data, publishes dashboards, supports portfolio and stage gate reviews, resolves resource conflicts, coaches project managers and maintains templates and tools.

What is the difference between a PMO and a project manager?

A project manager delivers one project. A PMO supports and oversees many projects, sets common standards and gives leaders a consolidated view.

What is an enterprise PMO?

An enterprise PMO (EPMO) works at organisation level. It links projects to strategy, supports portfolio decisions and provides enterprise-wide reporting and standards.

How many people does a PMO need?

It depends on its services. Estimate the effort for each service in your catalogue and convert it to FTE; a reporting-focused PMO with good tooling can start with one or two people.

How long does it take to set up a PMO?

A basic PMO with reporting and a governance rhythm can be running in about three months. Reaching a mature, portfolio-driven PMO usually takes a few years.

What are good PMO KPIs?

On-time delivery, forecast accuracy, schedule and cost performance, benefits realised, portfolio alignment, decision speed, resource conflicts resolved early and stakeholder satisfaction.

Why do PMOs fail?

Mostly through adding bureaucracy without value, lacking executive sponsorship, trying to do too much at once and failing to measure and show their own value.

Does an Agile organisation need a PMO?

Many still have one, reshaped as an Agile PMO or value management office that supports portfolio funding, flow, dependencies and outcomes rather than detailed plans.

Is there a PMO certification?

Yes. PMI offers the PMI Project Management Office Certified Professional (PMI-PMOCP), and PeopleCert offers P3O Foundation and Practitioner. The PMP is also common among PMO managers.

Is a PMO the same as a portfolio office?

Not quite. A portfolio office focuses on choosing and balancing the investments; a PMO focuses on how projects are delivered. In practice many PMOs do both.
