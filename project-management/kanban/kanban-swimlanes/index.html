Kanban Swimlanes: Types, Examples and How to Use Them

Kanban swimlanes are horizontal rows across a Kanban board that group cards by category, such as urgency, work type, team or customer, while the columns still show the stages of the workflow. Used well, they make priorities and policies visible at a glance and help you protect capacity for different kinds of work. This guide covers the main types of swimlanes, classes of service, WIP limits per lane, a worked board design and a step-by-step setup.

Key takeaways

  • Columns show where work is in the process; swimlanes show what kind of work it is. Sequential steps belong in columns, parallel streams in lanes.
  • Common lane types are class of service, work type, team or person, customer or project, and epic or feature. Pick one dimension per board.
  • An expedite lane needs a strict limit, usually one item, or it quickly becomes the normal way to get work done.
  • WIP limits can apply per lane as well as per column; lane limits are a simple way to reserve capacity, for example for improvement work.
  • Keep lanes few (two to four is typical), write an explicit policy for each, and measure cycle time and throughput by lane to check they help.

What is a Kanban swimlane?

A Kanban swimlane is a horizontal row that runs across every column of a Kanban board and holds one category of work. Cards still move left to right through the workflow stages, but they stay in their own lane, so you can see the flow of each category separately and apply different policies, priorities or WIP limits to it.

Picture a board with the columns Ready, In progress, Review and Done. Add three rows labelled Expedite, Standard and Improvement, and you have a grid. Every card sits in one cell: its column says how far along it is, and its lane says what kind of work it is and which rules apply. Most digital boards start with a single default lane; you add lanes only when you need them.

Swimlanes are an optional part of Kanban. Kanban University's Official Kanban Guide notes that some boards use horizontal swimlanes to make policies, types of work or classes of service more visible. Nothing in Kanban requires them; they are a visual tool to use when they change how the team acts.

Swimlanes vs columns

Columns and swimlanes are often confused, especially when people try to show two teams that hand work to each other. The rule of thumb: if work moves from one to the other in sequence, it is a column; if the categories run in parallel, they are lanes.

ColumnsSwimlanes
RepresentStages of the workflow (Ready, Build, Test, Done)Categories of work (Expedite, Feature, Defect, Client A)
Card movementCards move through them from left to rightA card normally stays in the same lane
WIP limitsThe usual place for WIP limitsOptional limits per lane or per lane and column
Question answeredHow far along is this work?What kind of work is this, and which rules apply?

If Development hands items to Testing, they are stages, so make them columns. If Team London and Team New York each work through the same stages in parallel, they can be lanes. See our guide to Kanban boards for how to design the columns first.

Why use swimlanes (and when not to)

A single-lane board treats every card the same. Swimlanes help when different kinds of work need different treatment or when you need to see a balance between them.

  • Make priorities explicit. An expedite lane at the top shows everyone what must be pulled first.
  • Protect capacity. A lane with its own limit guarantees some time for maintenance, improvement or technical debt, even when feature demand is high.
  • See the mix of work. Lanes by work type show at a glance whether defects are crowding out new work.
  • Spot where work piles up. Lanes by team, client or epic show which stream is stuck.
  • Apply different policies. Fixed-date items, for example, can be started earlier than standard work.
  • Share one board. Several small groups can use one board without losing sight of their own work.

You may not need lanes at all. Skip them when all work is treated the same way, the board has only a few cards, or one person works the board, as in personal Kanban. Every lane adds visual noise, so add one only when it would change a decision.

Types of Kanban swimlanes

Most boards use one of the types below. Choose one dimension per board; mixing, say, team lanes and priority lanes on the same board makes it unclear where a card belongs. Other categories can be shown with card colours, tags or avatars instead.

Swimlanes by class of service

The most powerful lane type, because each lane carries a different policy for how work is selected and handled. It is covered in detail in the next section.

Swimlanes by work type

Lanes for features, defects and maintenance make the balance of demand visible. A service team might use Incidents, Requests and Changes. Work-type lanes pair well with lane WIP limits, for example no more than two maintenance items in progress at once.

Swimlanes by team or person

Team lanes help when several groups share a board and the same workflow. A lane per person shows who is overloaded, but it can push people towards "my lane" thinking and discourage swarming on blocked work. Many teams show assignees as avatars on the Kanban cards instead, and keep lanes for something that affects how work is treated.

Swimlanes by customer or project

Agencies, consultancies and internal shared services often give each major customer or project its own lane, plus an Internal lane. This makes it easy to show a client their work and to see if one customer is taking more than their share. If you have more than four or five active customers, use card tags and filters instead.

Swimlanes by epic or feature

Product teams sometimes give each epic in flight its own lane. You can see which feature is stalled, and lanes can be removed as epics are finished. Keep the number of epics in progress low, and the number of lanes stays low too.

Classes of service and the expedite lane

A class of service is a set of policies for how a type of work item is treated, based on what happens if it is delayed. Classes of service were popularised by David J. Anderson's Kanban Method, and Kanban University's glossary defines four common archetypes by the impact of delay: expedite (high and immediate), fixed date (the impact arrives at a specific date), standard (typical of current conditions) and intangible (unknown, or not felt yet).

ClassCost of delayTypical pull policyTypical WIP ruleExample
ExpediteHigh and immediatePulled at once, ahead of everything; may interrupt other workLimit of one across the boardPayment system outage; security breach
Fixed dateLow until a date, then highStarted early enough to finish before the date, based on cycle time dataSmall limit; reviewed at replenishmentRegulatory change due on a set date; event materials
StandardSteady; rises the longer it waitsPulled in priority order, first in first out within priorityMost of the board's capacityNew features, routine requests
IntangibleLow or unclear now, may rise sharply laterPulled when there is reserved capacityFixed share, such as one or two itemsTechnical debt, upgrades, process improvements

Lanes are the clearest way to show classes of service, but not the only one. Some teams use card colours instead, and some flow practitioners prefer no permanent expedite lane at all: urgent items simply breach the WIP limit by explicit, recorded exception. Choose whichever your team will follow consistently.

Rules for an expedite lane

  1. Define what qualifies in writing, for example "a production outage affecting customers" or "a legal deadline within 48 hours".
  2. Name who can authorise an expedite, such as the service owner, rather than anyone who shouts loudest.
  3. Limit the lane to one item. A second expedite request waits or is escalated.
  4. Let the expedite item break column WIP limits, and agree who drops what to swarm on it.
  5. Track how often the lane is used. If it is busy every week, the real problem is in planning or quality, not prioritisation.

Fixed-date and intangible lanes

Fixed-date items fail when they are started too late. Use your cycle time data to decide when to start: if 85% of similar items finish within 10 working days, start at least 10 working days before the deadline, plus a margin. Intangible work fails the opposite way: it never starts. A lane with a fixed allocation, such as one or two items at all times, keeps it moving without letting it take over.

WIP limits and swimlanes

Swimlanes give you three ways to limit work in progress, and you can combine them:

  • Column limits across all lanes, such as a maximum of six items In progress. This is the standard Kanban approach.
  • Lane limits across the whole board, such as no more than one Expedite item and two Intangible items anywhere.
  • Cell limits for a lane within a column, such as a maximum of three Standard items In progress.

Lane limits are also a simple form of capacity allocation. A team that wants roughly 70% of its effort on standard work, 20% on improvement and 10% kept free for urgent work can express that as lane limits that add up to the column limit.

Worked example (illustrative). A team of six has an In progress limit of 6. It sets cell limits of 4 for Standard, 1 for Intangible and 1 for Fixed date, with the Expedite lane normally empty. On Tuesday the In progress column holds 4 Standard, 1 Intangible and 1 Fixed date item: the column is full. An outage arrives and is pulled into Expedite. The column now shows 7, one over the limit, which the expedite policy allows. Two developers swarm on the outage; the Standard items they leave stay where they are rather than being replaced. When the outage is fixed, the column drops back to 6 and normal pull resumes. Nobody starts new work while the column is over its limit.

Tip

Lane and cell limits should add up to no more than the column limit, otherwise the column limit stops doing anything. Start with your current average WIP per lane and lower it gradually.

Worked example: redesigning a team's board with swimlanes

This illustrative example follows a seven-person digital team that builds and runs a customer website. The details are made up to show the method.

The problem. The team used a single-lane board with the columns Backlog, Ready, In progress, Review and Done. Urgent fixes kept jumping the queue without anyone seeing it, a fixed-date compliance change was nearly missed, and planned upgrades had not moved in months. In the replenishment meeting, every conversation turned into an argument about priority.

The design. The team chose class of service as its single lane dimension and agreed this layout:

LaneReadyIn progress (limit 6)Review (limit 3)Policy
ExpediteNone heldMax 1, may exceed column limitReviewed firstLive site down or data at risk; authorised by product lead
Fixed dateMax 2Max 1SharedStart when the deadline is within the 85th percentile cycle time plus 5 days
StandardMax 6Max 4SharedPulled top to bottom in the order agreed at replenishment
ImprovementMax 2Max 1SharedAlways one item in progress; chosen at the monthly service delivery review

What the team watched. For the next eight weeks the team tracked how often the expedite lane was used, the cycle time of each lane and the age of the oldest improvement item. In this example the expedite lane was used three times, each time for a genuine outage, and the first improvement item, an overdue framework upgrade, was finished within a month. The board also revealed that most "urgent" requests were simply standard work with an impatient requester, which the team handled by publishing a cycle time forecast for standard items.

What changed later. After two months the team noticed the Fixed date lane was empty most of the time and merged those items into Standard with a red date flag. Lanes should be reviewed and simplified like any other policy.

How to set up Kanban swimlanes: step by step

  1. Start with the problem. Write down what you want swimlanes to fix: urgent work invisible, improvement never done, one client crowding out others. If you cannot name a problem, do not add lanes.
  2. Get the columns right first. Map your workflow stages as columns. Lanes cannot fix a board whose columns do not match the real process.
  3. Choose one lane dimension. Pick the category that changes how work is treated, usually class of service or work type. Show other attributes with colours or tags.
  4. Keep it to two to four lanes. Fewer lanes keep the board readable. Put the most urgent lane at the top, since people scan from the top.
  5. Write a policy for each lane. State what qualifies, who decides, how items are pulled and any limits. Make the policy visible on the board.
  6. Set lane or cell WIP limits. Base them on current WIP, make sure they fit within the column limits, and decide how the expedite lane interacts with them.
  7. Move existing cards into lanes. Do this as a team, so everyone applies the policies the same way.
  8. Measure by lane. Track cycle time, throughput and item age for each lane from day one.
  9. Review after four to six weeks. In a retrospective or service delivery review, ask whether each lane changed behaviour. Merge or remove lanes that are empty or ignored.

A free Kanban board template is enough to test a lane design on paper or in a spreadsheet before you change your software. If you need to map the process first, start with the workflow template.

Writing swimlane policies

Making policies explicit is one of the core Kanban practices, and lanes are only useful if everyone knows their rules. Keep each policy to a few lines and display it on the board, next to the lane name. Examples:

  • Expedite: "Customer-facing outage or security incident only. Authorised by the on-call lead. One item maximum. Anyone may be asked to help."
  • Fixed date: "Items with a contractual, legal or event date. Card shows the date. Pulled when the date is within our 85th percentile cycle time plus five working days."
  • Standard: "Pulled from the top of Ready. Order agreed at the weekly replenishment meeting."
  • Improvement: "At least one and at most two items in progress at all times. Chosen at the monthly review."
  • Client lanes: "Each client lane has a WIP limit agreed in the contract. New requests beyond the limit wait in Ready."

Review policies when the lane is not behaving as intended. A policy that is broken every week is either wrong or not understood.

Measuring flow by swimlane

The only way to know whether lanes help is to measure them. Most Kanban metrics become more useful when you split them by lane:

  • Cycle time by lane. Expedite items should be fast; if standard items are slowing down, expedites may be interrupting too often.
  • Throughput mix. Count finished items per lane each week. This shows whether your intended allocation, such as 20% improvement, is really happening.
  • Item age. A WIP aging chart split by lane shows intangible work quietly stalling.
  • Cumulative flow. A cumulative flow diagram per lane shows whether one stream is building a queue.
  • Service level expectations. Publish a forecast per class, such as "85% of standard items finish within 12 days", much like an internal service level agreement.

Blockers are worth tracking by lane too. If one client lane or work type collects most blockers, blocker clustering will show you why.

Common swimlane mistakes

  • Too many lanes. Eight lanes on a small board hide the flow. Merge until each lane changes behaviour.
  • Lanes for workflow stages. Development and Testing are steps, so they belong in columns.
  • Mixing dimensions. Team lanes and priority lanes on one board leave nobody sure where a card goes.
  • An unlimited expedite lane. If anything can be expedited, everything will be, and standard work stalls.
  • Lanes without policies. A lane name alone does not tell people how to treat the work.
  • Lane per person by default. It shows workload but discourages collaboration. Use avatars unless individual lanes solve a real problem.
  • Never removing lanes. Empty lanes for finished epics or former clients add clutter. Review lanes regularly.
  • Ignoring total WIP. Lane limits that add up to more than the column limit quietly raise work in progress.

Swimlanes in Kanban software

Most Kanban tools now support swimlanes, though the options differ. Check the current documentation for your plan before you design around a feature.

Kanban swimlanes vs swimlane diagrams

The word swimlane also appears in process mapping. A swimlane diagram is a flowchart divided into lanes by role or department, showing who does each step of a process and where hand-offs happen. A Kanban swimlane is a row on a live board that tracks real work items as they move. The diagram describes a process; the board manages the work flowing through it. Mapping the process with a swimlane diagram first is often a good way to choose your Kanban columns.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Kanban swimlane?

A horizontal row across a Kanban board that groups cards of one category, such as urgent work, a work type, a team or a client. Cards move left to right through the columns but stay in their lane.

What are the common types of Kanban swimlanes?

Lanes by class of service (expedite, fixed date, standard, intangible), by work type, by team or person, by customer or project, by epic or feature, and by priority.

What is the difference between a Kanban swimlane and a column?

Columns show the stages of the workflow, so cards move through them. Swimlanes group cards by category, so a card normally stays in the same lane.

How many swimlanes should a Kanban board have?

As few as possible, usually two to four. Add a lane only if it changes how the team treats the work, and remove lanes that stay empty.

What is an expedite swimlane?

A lane for urgent items with a high and immediate cost of delay, such as an outage. Items are pulled ahead of everything else, and the lane is usually limited to one item.

What are classes of service in Kanban?

Sets of policies for how different work is treated, based on the impact of delay. Common archetypes are expedite, fixed date, standard and intangible, and many teams give each its own lane.

Should Kanban swimlanes have WIP limits?

They can. Lane or cell limits reserve capacity for certain work, such as improvement items, but they should add up to no more than the column limit.

Should I use a swimlane for each team member?

Lanes per person show who is overloaded but can create silos and discourage swarming. Most teams prefer avatars on cards and lanes by work type or class of service.

Why give intangible work its own swimlane?

Intangible items, such as technical debt or upgrades, have no deadline, so they tend never to start. A lane with a fixed allocation keeps some capacity on them.

Does Trello or Jira have swimlanes?

Jira boards support swimlanes by assignee, epic or priority. Trello has no native swimlanes on the base board, so teams use labels, separate lists or Power-Ups.

Can a card move from one swimlane to another?

Yes, if its category changes, for example when a standard item becomes urgent and is formally expedited. Record the reason, as frequent lane changes suggest unclear policies.

When should you not use Kanban swimlanes?

When all work is treated the same way, the board is small, or one person works it. Lanes add visual noise, so add them only when they change decisions.

Is a Kanban swimlane the same as a swimlane diagram?

No. A swimlane diagram is a process flowchart split into lanes by role. A Kanban swimlane is a row on a board that tracks live work items.
