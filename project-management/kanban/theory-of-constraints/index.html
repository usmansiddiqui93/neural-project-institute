Theory of Constraints (TOC): Five Focusing Steps Explained

The theory of constraints (TOC) is a management approach which holds that every system has at least one constraint limiting its output, so the fastest way to improve the whole system is to find that constraint and manage everything around it. It gives you a simple, repeatable cycle, the five focusing steps, that works on a factory floor, a Kanban board or a portfolio of projects. This guide walks through the five steps with a worked Kanban example and covers drum-buffer-rope, throughput accounting and TOC in projects.

Key takeaways

  • Every system has a constraint, the one resource, policy or market limit that caps its output. Improving anything else barely changes the result.
  • The five focusing steps are identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate and repeat. Always exploit and subordinate before you spend money elevating.
  • On a Kanban board the constraint is usually the stage with the biggest queue in front of it and idle or blocked stages after it.
  • Drum-buffer-rope paces the system to the constraint; in Kanban, WIP limits act as the rope and a small ready queue acts as the buffer.
  • Non-constraint resources should have spare capacity. Keeping everyone 100% busy simply builds queues and lengthens cycle time.

What is the theory of constraints?

The theory of constraints is a way of improving any system with a goal, such as a business, a production line or a delivery team, by focusing on the single factor that most limits its performance. Improving other parts of the system produces little or no overall gain.

Think of a chain: it is only as strong as its weakest link. If testers can check six pieces of work a week, the team delivers about six a week, however fast the developers write code.

TOC was developed by Israeli physicist Eliyahu M. Goldratt (1947 to 2011). He introduced it to a mass audience in The Goal (1984, written with Jeff Cox), a business novel about a plant manager who rescues his failing factory by finding and managing its bottlenecks. Goldratt set out the five focusing steps in What is this Thing Called Theory of Constraints (1990), launched the TOC thinking processes in 1991 and popularised them in It's Not Luck (1994), and applied the theory to projects in Critical Chain (1997).

TOC began in manufacturing but is now used in supply chains, healthcare, software and projects. It sits alongside Lean manufacturing and Kanban, which share its focus on flow.

The core ideas behind TOC

TOC rests on a few ideas that often run against normal management instinct. Start by agreeing the goal and how you measure it, such as items delivered per week: you cannot find the constraint until you know what it is constraining. Then think in systems, not departments. An hour the constraint loses to idle time or rework is lost to the whole system, while an hour saved at a non-constraint usually just makes work arrive sooner at the queue in front of the constraint.

Common beliefTOC view
Every team should be fully utilisedOnly the constraint must be fully utilised; other resources need slack
Improve every process a littleImprove the constraint a lot; leave the rest alone until it becomes the constraint
Start work as early as possibleRelease work at the pace the constraint can absorb
Add people where the work is lateAdd capacity only at the constraint, and only after exploiting it

Constraint vs bottleneck: types of constraints

The two words are often used interchangeably. Strictly, a bottleneck is any resource whose capacity is lower than the demand placed on it. A constraint is broader: it is whatever limits the system's performance against its goal, which may be a bottleneck, but may also be a policy, a supplier or the market itself. Every system has a constraint; not every system has a bottleneck. If you can produce more than customers want to buy, demand is the constraint.

Constraints are often grouped as internal (inside your control, such as capacity, skills or rules) and external (outside it, such as market demand, regulation or a supplier's lead time).

TypeOperations exampleProject or knowledge-work example
Physical capacityOne machine slower than the rest of the lineTwo testers serving six developers
Skills or peopleOnly one certified welderOne architect who must review every design
PolicyMinimum batch size of 500 unitsAll changes wait for a fortnightly approval board
Material or supplierLate component deliveriesClient content arrives late; a vendor's API is not ready
MarketDemand lower than capacityNot enough approved projects to keep the team supplied

In knowledge work, policy constraints are common and cheap to fix: an approval step, a batching habit or a rule about who may do what. Look for these before concluding that a team needs more people.

TOC constraints are not the same as the scope, time and cost limits a project works within. Our guide to project constraints covers the triple constraint.

The five focusing steps

The five focusing steps are TOC's ongoing improvement cycle. Many practitioners add a "step zero": agree the system's goal and how you measure it.

StepKey questionCost
1. IdentifyWhat limits our output?Low
2. ExploitHow do we get more from the constraint as it is?Low
3. SubordinateHow does everything else support the constraint?Low
4. ElevateHow do we add capacity to the constraint?Medium to high
5. RepeatHas the constraint moved?Low

Step 1: Identify the constraint

Find the part of the system that limits output. In a physical process it is usually where inventory piles up; in a team it is the stage with the largest queue in front of it, or the people everyone waits for. Do not wait for perfect data: a wrong first guess is quickly corrected.

Step 2: Exploit the constraint

Exploiting means squeezing the most output from the constraint with the resources you already have. It costs little or nothing. Typical actions:

  • Make sure the constraint never sits idle waiting for work; keep a small queue of ready items in front of it.
  • Remove tasks that others can do, such as administration, environment set-up or reporting.
  • Stop defective input reaching the constraint, so it does not waste time on work that will come back.
  • Protect focus time and cut task switching; let the constraint finish one item before starting the next.

Step 3: Subordinate everything else

Subordinating means adjusting everything else to support the constraint, even if that leaves other resources partly idle. Release new work only at the pace the constraint can handle, keep a buffer in front of it, and let people with spare capacity help it rather than start new work. Culturally this is the hardest step, because managers must accept visible slack.

Step 4: Elevate the constraint

If the constraint still limits output, add capacity: hire or train people, automate, buy equipment or outsource part of the work. Elevating costs money and time, which is why it comes fourth; teams that jump straight to hiring often bury the extra capacity under waste the earlier steps would have removed.

Step 5: Repeat, and do not let inertia become the constraint

Once the constraint is broken, another part of the system becomes the limit, so go back to step 1. Goldratt warned that rules introduced for the old constraint can become the new one, so review them each time it moves. Over time, many organisations decide where they want the constraint to sit, often an expensive resource, and keep it there deliberately.

Tip

If you remember one rule, make it this: exploit and subordinate before you elevate. Cheap changes at the constraint usually come before expensive ones.

Worked example: applying TOC to a Kanban board

This illustrative example follows a software team using a four-stage Kanban board. About nine new items are started each week. Average capacity per stage:

StagePeopleCapacity (items/week)
Analyse1 analyst12
Build5 developers10
Test3 testers6
ReleaseAutomated pipeline, 1 engineer15

Step 1: identify

Nine items a week enter, but only six a week leave Test, so the queue in front of Test grows by three items every week. Release is often idle. Test is the constraint. After a couple of months there are about 42 items in progress. Using Little's Law (average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput), the average item takes 42 ÷ 6 = 7 weeks to get through the system.

Step 2: exploit

Looking at how testers spend their week reveals two problems:

  • Each tester spends roughly a day a week setting up test environments and writing status reports. A developer scripts the environment set-up and the board replaces the written report. Capacity rises from 6 to 7 items a week.
  • About one item in four fails basic checks and has to be retested. The team adds a "ready for test" checklist that developers complete, including unit tests. Fewer retests lift capacity to 8 items a week.

Throughput rises to 8 with no new spending. But nine items are still entering each week, so the queue keeps growing by one a week and cycle time stays around five weeks.

Step 3: subordinate

  • The rope: the team agrees to pull only eight new items a week into Analyse, matching the constraint, and sets WIP limits on Analyse and Build.
  • The buffer: a "Ready for test" column with a WIP limit of four keeps testers supplied without letting a large queue build up.
  • Helping the constraint: when Build reaches its WIP limit, developers pair with testers or write automated tests instead of starting new items.

Over the next five weeks the excess work drains away. WIP settles at about 16 items while throughput stays at eight, so average cycle time falls to 16 ÷ 8 = 2 weeks. Developers now have some slack, which is intended.

Step 4: elevate

Demand is growing, so the team invests in automated regression tests. Test capacity rises to about 12 items a week and the release rate to 10.

Step 5: repeat

With Test at 12, Build (10) is now the slowest stage and becomes the constraint. The team returns to step 1, and also reviews the rule that developers must help Test whenever Build is full, because that rule would now starve the new constraint.

MeasureStartAfter exploitAfter subordinateAfter elevate
Test capacity (items/week)68812
Items started per week99810
Throughput (items/week)68810
Work in progress42 and growing42 and growing16About 20
Average cycle time7 weeksAbout 5 weeks2 weeks2 weeks
ConstraintTestTestTestBuild

The biggest gain in speed came from subordination, not new capacity: it cut cycle time from about five weeks to two by no longer starting work faster than it could be finished. That is why TOC and WIP limits work so well together.

How to find the constraint in your workflow

Use this sequence on any board or process:

  1. Map the workflow from request to delivery, including waiting states and handoffs. A value stream map or our workflow template helps.
  2. Count throughput by stage over the last four to eight weeks: how many items each stage finished per week.
  3. Look for the queue. The constraint usually has the most work waiting in front of it and the oldest items in its queue.
  4. Look downstream. Stages after the constraint are often idle or starved; stages before it are often blocked or told to slow down.
  5. Check the cumulative flow diagram. On a cumulative flow diagram, the band that keeps widening is the queue building in front of the constraint.
  6. Check item age. A WIP aging chart shows where items stall for longest.
  7. Look at blockers and ask the team. Blocker clustering often reveals a policy or external constraint, and people usually know who they wait for.
  8. Confirm by asking: if this stage had 20% more capacity, would the whole team deliver more? If not, keep looking.

Be wary of false constraints. Large batch releases, expedited work that jumps the queue and holidays can all make a stage look like the bottleneck for a week or two. Look at several weeks of data, and remember that low flow efficiency usually means work is waiting, which points to a queue somewhere upstream.

Drum-buffer-rope

Drum-buffer-rope (DBR) is TOC's method for scheduling a system around its constraint. In The Goal, Goldratt illustrated it with a scout hike where the slowest walker sets the pace.

DBR elementPurposeKanban equivalent
DrumThe constraint sets the pace for the whole systemThroughput of the constraint stage sets the replenishment rate
BufferProtect the constraint so it never starvesA "Ready for" column in front of the constraint with its own WIP limit
RopeRelease new work only as fast as the constraint consumes itWIP limits and a replenishment meeting that pulls only when there is room
Buffer managementSpot threats to the constraint earlyItem age in the buffer, WIP aging chart, blocker review

Buffer management

TOC tracks buffers in three zones of equal size. If the buffer is mostly full, the constraint is safe; as it empties, risk rises.

ZoneMeaningAction
Green (top third)The constraint is well protectedNo action needed
Yellow (middle third)Upstream is slowing downCheck what is coming and plan a response
Red (bottom third)The constraint is about to starveExpedite work into the buffer; swarm on upstream blockers

Size the buffer to cover normal variation upstream: start with a few days of the constraint's work and adjust depending on how often it runs into red or overflows.

TOC and Kanban

Kanban came from the Toyota Production System and TOC from Goldratt, but they meet in Kanban for knowledge work. David J. Anderson, who later shaped the Kanban Method, first wrote Agile Management for Software Engineering: Applying the Theory of Constraints for Business Results (2003).

  • WIP limits are the rope. They stop teams starting work faster than the constraint can finish it.
  • Swarming exploits the constraint. When the constraint stage is full, people elsewhere help clear it rather than start new work; see agile swarming.
  • Little's Law links the two. Less WIP at the same throughput means shorter cycle time, which is exactly what subordination delivers.

The difference is emphasis. Kanban limits WIP at every stage and improves flow everywhere; TOC focuses improvement on one point and accepts slack elsewhere. Use Kanban's WIP limits and metrics to run the system, and the five steps to decide where to improve next.

Theory of constraints in project management

Projects have constraints too, and they are usually people: a specialist shared across several projects, a sponsor who must approve every decision, or a test environment everyone needs. TOC thinking changes how you plan around them.

In TOC terms, you identify the resource most projects wait for, exploit it by protecting its time and making sure its inputs are complete, subordinate by scheduling other work around its availability, and elevate by training a second person or changing who may approve.

Multitasking and staggering projects

When a shared specialist splits time across five projects, every project waits for them and all five finish late. TOC's answer is to start fewer projects at once and stagger them according to the constraint resource's capacity. This multi-project form of drum-buffer-rope can shorten delivery of every project without adding people. Our guide to managing multiple projects covers the practical side, and capacity planning helps you see the constraint before it bites.

Critical chain

Goldratt's Critical Chain (1997) applied TOC to scheduling: plan the longest chain of dependent tasks allowing for resource availability, pool safety time into shared buffers instead of padding each task, and track how fast the buffers are consumed. Throughput accounting

TOC comes with its own management accounting, designed to support decisions about the constraint. It uses three measures:

  • Throughput (T): the rate at which the system generates money through sales, calculated as revenue minus truly variable costs (usually materials).
  • Investment or inventory (I): money tied up in things the system intends to sell, plus equipment and other assets.
  • Operating expense (OE): money spent turning inventory into throughput, such as wages, rent and overheads.

Net profit is T minus OE, and return on investment is (T minus OE) divided by I. The aim is to raise throughput rather than chase cost cuts that do not affect the constraint.

Worked example: prioritising by throughput per constraint hour

In this illustrative example, a small workshop makes two products. Both pass through a cutting machine that is available 40 hours a week and is the constraint.

ProductPriceMaterialsThroughput per unitCutter time per unitThroughput per cutter hourWeekly demand
A$50$20$3030 minutes$6060 units
B$40$25$1510 minutes$90120 units

Meeting all demand would need 50 cutter hours (30 for A, 20 for B), but only 40 are available. Product A earns more per unit, so instinct says make A first. TOC says rank by throughput per hour of the constraint instead:

  • A first: 60 units of A use 30 hours, leaving 10 hours for 60 units of B. Throughput = (60 × $30) + (60 × $15) = $2,700 a week.
  • B first: 120 units of B use 20 hours, leaving 20 hours for 40 units of A. Throughput = (120 × $15) + (40 × $30) = $3,000 a week.

Prioritising B earns $300 more a week from the same constraint. The same logic applies to a team: when the constraint is a scarce specialist, give them the work that delivers the most value per hour of their time.

The thinking processes

Many constraints are policies, assumptions or conflicts rather than machines or people. Goldratt's thinking processes are logic tools for these, answering three questions: what to change, what to change to, and how to cause the change. They include the current reality tree (traces symptoms to root causes), the evaporating cloud (resolves a conflict by exposing its assumptions), the future reality tree, the prerequisite tree and the transition tree. For simpler problems, a 5 whys analysis with our root cause analysis template is often enough.

The three improvement approaches overlap and are often combined.

A common combination is to use TOC to pick the target, then Lean to remove waste at the constraint and Six Sigma to reduce variation in its inputs. See Lean project management and Lean vs Six Sigma for more.

Benefits and limitations of TOC

TOC's main benefit is focus: it shows where to improve first, and early gains usually come from changing habits and rules, not investment. Its limitations:

  • Constraints move in knowledge work. A changing work mix can shift the bottleneck, so look at trends over several weeks.
  • Idle time is uncomfortable. Subordination clashes with utilisation targets.
  • Evidence is mixed. Critics note that TOC borrows from earlier operations research and that rigorous studies of its results are limited.
  • The people side matters. Labelling a team "the constraint" can feel like blame; frame it as the system's limit.

Common mistakes when applying TOC

  • Elevating first. Hiring or buying before exploiting and subordinating, which hides waste and adds cost.
  • Improving non-constraints. Speeding up a stage that is not the constraint only moves the queue.
  • Keeping everyone busy. Letting non-constraint stages start new work whenever they are free, which floods the constraint.
  • Starving the constraint. Not keeping a buffer in front of it, so it waits for work after every upstream hiccup.
  • Ignoring policy constraints. Looking only for slow people or machines when an approval rule or batching habit is the real limit.
  • Treating TOC as a one-off, or chasing every fluctuation. Repeat the cycle, but decide where the constraint should sit rather than reacting to every weekly change.
  • Leaving old rules in place. Policies built for the old constraint become the new constraint, which is exactly the inertia step 5 warns about.

Frequently asked questions

What is the theory of constraints in simple terms?

Every system has one part that limits how much it can produce, like the weakest link in a chain. Find it, get the most out of it and organise everything else around it.

Who developed the theory of constraints?

Eliyahu M. Goldratt, an Israeli physicist, who introduced it in the 1984 business novel The Goal, written with Jeff Cox.

What are the five focusing steps?

Identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, and repeat when it moves, without letting inertia become the new constraint.

What is the difference between a constraint and a bottleneck?

A bottleneck is a resource whose capacity is below demand. A constraint is anything limiting the system's performance, including policies, suppliers and market demand.

What are some examples of constraints?

A slow machine, too few testers, one expert who reviews all work, a fortnightly approval board, late supplier deliveries or too little customer demand.

What does it mean to exploit the constraint?

Getting the most from it with existing resources: no idle time, no work others could do, no defective input and protected focus time.

What does subordinate mean in TOC?

Aligning everything else to the constraint's pace, such as releasing work only as fast as it can process it, even if other resources are partly idle.

What is drum-buffer-rope?

A TOC scheduling method: the constraint sets the pace (drum), a buffer protects it, and the rope releases new work only as fast as the constraint consumes it.

How do you identify the constraint on a Kanban board?

Look for the largest, oldest queue, idle columns after it and a widening band on the cumulative flow diagram, then confirm with throughput by stage.

How does the theory of constraints relate to Kanban?

WIP limits act as TOC's rope, the board makes the constraint visible and swarming exploits it. TOC tells a Kanban team where to focus improvement first.

What is throughput accounting?

TOC's management accounting, using throughput (revenue minus truly variable costs), investment and operating expense, and ranking work by throughput per unit of constraint time.

How is TOC used in project management?

Through critical chain scheduling with shared buffers, and by staggering projects around the scarcest shared resource to reduce multitasking.

What is the difference between TOC and Lean?

Lean removes waste across the whole value stream; TOC focuses on the one constraint that limits output. TOC is often used to decide where to apply Lean first.
