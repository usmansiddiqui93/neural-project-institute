Cumulative Flow Diagram: How to Read and Use a CFD

A cumulative flow diagram (CFD) is a stacked area chart that shows how many work items have reached each stage of your workflow, day by day or week by week. In one picture it tells you how much work is in progress, where it is piling up and how quickly it is being finished. This guide explains how a CFD is built, how to read work in progress, cycle time and throughput from it, what the common patterns mean and how to build your own, using an eight-week worked example.

Key takeaways

  • A CFD has one band per workflow stage. Each line counts every item that has reached that stage or any later one, which is why the lines never fall.
  • The vertical distance between two lines is the work in progress between those stages on that date.
  • The horizontal distance between the line where work starts and the Done line is an approximate average cycle time.
  • The slope of the Done line is your delivery rate (throughput); the slope of the top line is your arrival rate.
  • A band that keeps getting wider is the clearest sign of a bottleneck. Look for the first widening band, working up from Done.
  • Read the CFD for trends over several weeks, and pair it with a cycle time scatterplot and a WIP aging chart for item-level detail.

What is a cumulative flow diagram?

A cumulative flow diagram is a stacked area chart that plots, over time, the total number of work items that have reached each stage of a workflow. Each coloured band represents one stage. The thickness of a band shows how much work is in that stage, and the slope of the lines shows how fast work is arriving and leaving.

The chart comes from queueing theory, where it is sometimes called a cumulative input-output diagram, and it is used in traffic engineering and manufacturing as well as in knowledge work. Agile and Kanban teams adopted it because it shows the health of a whole process at once, not just the result at the end. The PMBOK Guide (7th edition) also describes it, as a chart showing features completed over time alongside features in other states and those in the backlog.

A CFD answers three practical questions:

  • How much work is in progress? Read the vertical gap between the lines.
  • Where is work waiting? Look for the band that is getting wider.
  • How fast is work being finished, compared with how fast it arrives? Compare the slope of the Done line with the slope of the top line.

It is one of the core charts of the Kanban method, alongside the cycle time scatterplot, the throughput run chart and the WIP aging chart. For how they fit together, see our guide to Kanban metrics.

Most tools use the same colour order as your board columns. If the chart looks upside down, check whether the tool stacks Done at the top; the reading rules are the same, only mirrored.

How a CFD is built: the counting rule

Count everything that has reached a stage or beyond

The single idea that makes a CFD readable is its counting rule. Each line does not show the items sitting in a stage today; it shows every item that has ever reached that stage or any stage after it. So the In progress line counts items in progress, plus items in review, plus items done. The Done line counts everything delivered since the chart started.

Plot those cumulative totals as lines, shade the space between neighbouring lines, and each shaded band is exactly the number of items in one stage on that date. That is why the gap between two lines equals work in progress.

Why the lines never go down

Because each line is a running total of arrivals into a stage, it can rise or stay flat but it cannot fall in normal use. A line only drops if items are deleted, moved backwards on the board, or filtered out of the report. If you see a dip, check for board clean-ups, items reopened or a change to the report filter before reading anything into it.

Which stages to include

Include every column between your commitment point (where the team agrees to do the work) and your delivery point. Adding the backlog or an options column above the commitment point is optional. It shows demand, but a large backlog can dwarf the rest of the chart. Many teams show the backlog on a separate chart or leave it out and keep a short, replenished Ready column instead. If your board has split columns such as Review: doing and Review: done, keep both, because the done half is a queue and the CFD will show it filling up. For board design, see Kanban boards.

Weeks 1 to 4: steady flow. The bands rise roughly in parallel. The team starts about six items a week and finishes about five, and total WIP creeps up only slightly, from 5 to 8.

Weeks 5 to 7: a bottleneck in Review. The one person who does most reviews is pulled onto a customer escalation. The Review band swells from 4 items to 10 while the Done line flattens to two or three items a week. Notice what the team does upstream: it keeps starting four or five items a week, so total WIP climbs to 14. This is the classic shape of a bottleneck. Work keeps flowing in, but it cannot get out.

Week 8: recovery. At the retrospective in week 7 the team sets a WIP limit of four on Review, agrees that developers pair on reviews before pulling new work, and stops starting new items until Review is below its limit. Within a week the Review band shrinks to 7, throughput jumps to 7 items and total WIP falls to 10. The Ready band grows, because the team deliberately started less, which is the right trade-off while the queue clears.

Tip

Notice that the bottleneck was visible on the CFD from week 5, two weeks before anyone raised it. That early warning is the main reason to look at the chart every week rather than only at the end of a project.

How to read WIP, cycle time and throughput from a CFD

Vertical distance: work in progress

Draw a vertical line at any date. The distance between two lines along it is the number of items between those stages on that date. In the example, at week 4 the Started line is at 26 and the Done line at 18, so 8 items are in progress. At week 7 the gap is 40 − 26 = 14 items: WIP has almost doubled in three weeks.

Horizontal distance: approximate average cycle time

Draw a horizontal line at any count. The distance along it between the Started line and the Done line is roughly how long items took to get from starting to finishing. It is an approximation of the average, not the cycle time of any particular item, because items do not necessarily finish in the order they started.

  • At week 4: the Done line reaches 18. The Started line reached 18 about two-thirds of the way through week 3 (it went from 14 to 20 that week). The horizontal gap is about 1.3 weeks.
  • At week 7: the Done line reaches 26. The Started line was at 26 at the end of week 4, so the gap is about 3 weeks.
  • At week 8: the Done line reaches 33, which the Started line passed during week 6 (it was 31 at week 5 and 36 at week 6). The gap is about 2.6 weeks and starting to close.

The approximate cycle time more than doubled during the bottleneck. Measure the gap between the top line and the Done line instead and you get an approximate lead time, which includes waiting in Ready. For item-level detail and percentiles, use a cycle time scatterplot; for the difference between the two measures, see cycle time vs lead time.

Slope: throughput and arrival rate

The steepness of the Done line is your delivery rate. In weeks 2 to 4 it rises by 5 items a week; in weeks 5 to 7 by fewer than 3. The steepness of the top line is the rate at which new work arrives: about 6 items a week throughout. When the top line is persistently steeper than the bottom line, WIP is growing and cycle time will follow. Healthy systems have arrival and departure lines that are, over a few weeks, roughly parallel. For more on measuring and forecasting with delivery rate, see throughput.

Tip

Find the first widening band, working up from Done. Bands above a bottleneck tend to widen as a knock-on effect, so the lowest widening band is usually closest to the real constraint. The Theory of Constraints explains what to do once you have found it.

How to create a cumulative flow diagram step by step

Most Kanban tools draw a CFD automatically, but building one in a spreadsheet takes about 20 minutes and is worth doing once: it makes the counting rule obvious and works for physical boards too.

  1. List your stages in board order, from the commitment point to Done. Decide whether to include the backlog.
  2. Choose a snapshot frequency. Daily gives the most detail; weekly is fine for teams that finish a handful of items a week. Pick a fixed time, such as 5 pm or Friday afternoon.
  3. Record the count in each column at every snapshot, one row per date and one column per stage. On a physical board, one person counts cards; in a tool, use a filtered list. For Done, record the running total of everything finished since the chart began, even if you archive done cards.
  4. Check the cumulative totals. Add a helper column for each line: Started = In progress + Review + Done, Entered review = Review + Done, and so on. Every helper column should rise or stay flat from row to row; a fall means a counting error or an item moved backwards.
  5. Order the stage columns so Done comes first in the data range. Spreadsheet tools stack series in data order, so Done becomes the bottom band.
  6. Insert a stacked area chart of the stage counts (not the helper columns). Stacking does the cumulative adding for you, so the top edge of each band lands exactly on the helper totals. Plotting the helper columns as a stacked chart would double-count.
  7. Format for reading: use the same colours as your board, label the axes, start the vertical axis at zero, and add a short note at any date when a policy changed.
  8. Update it at every snapshot and look at it weekly. The value comes from the trend, not from any single day.

If you are setting up a board from scratch, a template saves time: ours includes To Do, In Progress, Review and Done columns, WIP limits and card details, and you can add a snapshot tab with one row per date beside it.

Using a CFD with Little's Law

Little's Law, published by John Little in 1961, links the three measures a CFD displays. In flow terms:

Average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput.

In weeks 2 to 4 of the example, average WIP was (6 + 7 + 8) ÷ 3 = 7 items and throughput was 5 items a week. Little's Law gives 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4 weeks, which agrees closely with the 1.3 weeks read horizontally off the chart.

Now try weeks 5 to 7. Average WIP was (10 + 13 + 14) ÷ 3 ≈ 12.3 and average throughput was (3 + 2 + 3) ÷ 3 ≈ 2.7, giving about 4.6 weeks, well above the 2.3 to 3 weeks the chart showed. The numbers disagree because the law only holds when its assumptions are met, and in those weeks they were not. Daniel Vacanti, who has written extensively on applying the law to knowledge work, sets out conditions along these lines:

  • Average arrival rate roughly equals average departure rate over the period.
  • Work that is started is eventually finished, rather than abandoned in the system.
  • WIP is roughly the same at the start and end of the period.
  • The average age of work in progress is neither growing nor shrinking.
  • WIP, cycle time and throughput are measured in consistent units and over the same boundaries.

So use the CFD in two ways: as a picture that tells you whether the system is stable, and, when it is, as a source for Little's Law estimates. When the bands are diverging, treat any calculation as a warning, not a forecast. The practical lesson is the one Kanban teams act on every day: with throughput steady, reducing WIP shortens cycle time.

How to use a CFD in reviews and meetings

A CFD is most useful as a regular conversation starter, not as a report for managers. A simple weekly or fortnightly routine works well, for example at a service delivery review or a retrospective (see Kanban ceremonies).

  1. Look at the last four to eight weeks, not the whole history. Long ranges flatten recent problems.
  2. Compare the top and bottom slopes. Is work arriving faster than it leaves?
  3. Scan for widening bands, starting from Done. Name the first one.
  4. Check WIP against your limits. If a band is thicker than its column limit allows, the limit is being ignored; ask why.
  5. Estimate the horizontal gap now and a month ago. Is time in process growing?
  6. Agree one experiment, such as lowering a WIP limit, pairing on reviews or splitting large items, and mark the date on the chart.
  7. Review the effect at the next meeting by comparing the bands before and after the marker.

Day to day, a WIP aging chart is the better tool, because it shows which individual items are stuck now. The CFD tells you where the system is struggling; the aging chart tells you which card to help with this morning. When a band widens because items are blocked, record why and look for patterns with blocker clustering.

Can Scrum teams use a cumulative flow diagram?

Yes. A CFD works for any team that moves work through visible stages, whether or not it uses Kanban. Scrum teams can use it in two ways:

  • Within a sprint: a CFD starting on day one of the sprint shows whether items are moving into Review and Done steadily or all at once on the last day. A Done band that only rises at the end is a sign of large items or too much work started in parallel.
  • Across sprints: a rolling CFD over several sprints shows trends in WIP and delivery that a burndown resets every two weeks.

The chart complements rather than replaces Scrum's own measures. For the metrics Scrum teams commonly track, see Scrum metrics and KPIs, and for how the two frameworks compare, Kanban vs Scrum.

Cumulative flow diagrams in project management tools

Most Agile tools generate a CFD from your board's status history, so the main job is keeping card statuses up to date. A few notes from vendor documentation (checked 25 October 2026):

  • Jira offers a cumulative flow diagram report for boards, showing how many work items are in each column over time, and a separate control chart for time in status. See our Jira review.
  • Azure DevOps provides a cumulative flow report and dashboard widget. Microsoft's documentation describes rolling periods of 14 to 180 days for continuous flow, or a fixed start date for sprint-style work.
  • ClickUp dashboards include cycle time cards, and its help centre also documents a sprint cumulative flow card (now marked as legacy). See our ClickUp review.
  • Wrike, our top-ranked tool overall, describes a work progress chart in its analytics as a way to get a similar view of work by status over time, which suits teams managing several projects and stakeholders. See our Wrike review.
  • Spreadsheets work well for physical boards and small teams, using the step-by-step method above.

Whichever tool you use, check three settings before trusting the chart: which statuses are mapped to which bands, whether filters or swimlanes are excluding work, and the date range.

Common mistakes when using a CFD

  • Reading a single day. A CFD is a trend chart. One thick day means little; a band widening for two weeks means a lot.
  • Including a huge backlog. It flattens every other band. Leave it out or chart it separately.
  • Mixing units. Count items. Switching to story points or hours partway through breaks the chart.
  • Forgetting that statuses drive the chart. If people move cards late, the CFD shows false bottlenecks and false jumps.
  • Treating horizontal distance as exact. It approximates average time in process; use item-level cycle times for percentiles and service level expectations.
  • Applying Little's Law to an unstable system. When bands are diverging, calculations based on averages mislead.
  • Blaming the stage with the thick band. The constraint is often a policy, a dependency or a hand-off, not the people in that column.
  • Changing the workflow without a note. Adding or merging columns changes the bands; mark the date so later readers are not confused.
  • Using the CFD to compare teams. Workflows, item sizes and policies differ. Use it to improve your own system.

Swarming on the stage that is struggling is often the fastest fix; see Agile swarming. And because much of the delay a CFD reveals is waiting rather than working, it pairs naturally with flow efficiency analysis.

Frequently asked questions

What is a cumulative flow diagram in simple terms?

It is a chart with one coloured band for each stage of your workflow. The bands stack up over time, so you can see how much work is in each stage, where it is piling up and how fast it is getting to Done.

How do you read a cumulative flow diagram?

Read vertically for work in progress (the gap between two lines), horizontally for approximate average time in process (the gap between the start line and the Done line), and look at slopes for rates: the top line is arrivals and the bottom line is throughput. Then look for any band that keeps getting wider.

What does a widening band mean on a CFD?

Work is entering that stage faster than it is leaving, so a queue or bottleneck is forming. If several bands widen, start with the lowest one, nearest to Done, because problems upstream are often knock-on effects.

What does a healthy cumulative flow diagram look like?

Bands that rise smoothly and roughly in parallel, with fairly constant thickness, and a top line and Done line with similar slopes over a few weeks. That shows stable WIP, steady delivery and arrivals that match capacity.

What does a flat line on a CFD mean?

Nothing crossed into that stage during the period. A flat Done line means nothing was delivered, which might be a holiday, a batch release or a blocker. A flat top line means no new work was accepted.

How do you calculate cycle time from a CFD?

Pick a point on the Done line and measure horizontally back to the line where work starts, such as In progress. That distance is an approximate average cycle time for items finishing around then. For individual items and percentiles, use a cycle time scatterplot.

What is the difference between a CFD and a burnup chart?

A burnup chart shows two things: total scope and completed work. A CFD shows every stage in between as well, so it reveals where work is accumulating and how much is in progress, not just how much is done.

Why does the bottom band of a CFD never go down?

Because each line is a running total of items that have reached that stage or beyond. Finished items stay counted. A line only falls if items are deleted, moved backwards or filtered out of the report.

Should the backlog be included in a CFD?

It is optional. Including it shows demand, but a large backlog can dwarf every other band. Many teams start the chart at the commitment point, such as a Ready column, and track the wider backlog separately.

How often should a team review its CFD?

Weekly or fortnightly is common, for example at a service delivery review or retrospective. Use a WIP aging chart for daily attention to individual items.

Can you make a cumulative flow diagram in Excel or Google Sheets?

Yes. Record the number of items in each column at a fixed time each day or week, put Done first in the data range, and insert a stacked area chart of the stage counts. The step-by-step method above takes about 20 minutes to set up.

Is the cumulative flow diagram in the PMBOK Guide or the PMI-ACP exam?

The PMBOK Guide (7th edition) describes the cumulative flow diagram, and it is a standard Agile chart that exam preparation materials for PMI-ACP commonly cover. Check PMI's current exam content outline for the latest scope.
