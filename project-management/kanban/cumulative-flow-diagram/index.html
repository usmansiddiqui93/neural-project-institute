Cumulative Flow Diagram (CFD): How to Read and Use It

A cumulative flow diagram (CFD) shows how many work items are in each stage of your workflow over time. In one chart, it reveals bottlenecks, growing work in progress and changes in delivery rate.

By Neural Project InstitutePublished 5 October 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • A CFD stacks the number of items per stage over time.
  • Widening bands show bottlenecks; a flat Done line shows no delivery.
  • It approximates WIP, cycle time and throughput visually.

How a CFD is built

Each day, the number of items in each column of the board is recorded. The chart stacks these counts as coloured bands over time, usually with Done at the bottom and the earliest stage at the top. Because items are counted cumulatively, the bands never go down for Done.

Reading the bands

What you seeWhat it means
Parallel bands of steady widthStable, healthy flow
A band getting widerWork is piling up in that stage: a bottleneck
A band getting thinnerThat stage is starved of work
Flat top line of DoneNothing is being finished
Steep rise in the top bandLots of new work arriving

Approximations from a CFD

  • Vertical distance between the top of the in-progress bands and the Done line at a point in time ≈ work in progress.
  • Horizontal distance between when items enter progress and when they reach Done ≈ average cycle time.
  • Slope of the Done line ≈ throughput.

Using a CFD in reviews

  • Look at it in service delivery reviews or retrospectives.
  • Find the first band that is widening; that is usually the constraint.
  • Check whether WIP limits are being respected.
  • Compare before and after process changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is a CFD used for?

To see bottlenecks, WIP trends and delivery rate at a glance.

Can Scrum teams use a CFD?

Yes. A CFD is useful for any team that tracks work through stages.
