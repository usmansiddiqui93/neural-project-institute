Cycle Time vs Lead Time: Differences, Formulas, Examples

The difference between cycle time and lead time is where the clock starts: lead time runs from the moment a customer asks for something until they receive it, while cycle time runs only from when the team starts work until the work is finished. Cycle time sits inside lead time, so lead time shows what your customers experience and cycle time shows how quickly your process moves once work begins. This guide shows where each clock starts and stops on a Kanban board, how to calculate both from the same card dates, two worked examples, what the gap between them tells you and how to shorten each one.

Key takeaways

  • Lead time = request to delivery (the customer's wait). Cycle time = start to finish (the team's process). Lead time = waiting before work starts + cycle time + any wait before delivery.
  • Definitions vary between sources. The Kanban Guide defines cycle time but not lead time; Kanban University, DORA and tools such as Azure DevOps each draw the lead time boundary differently. Write down your own start and stop points.
  • Calculate both from card dates with the same convention, for example finish date − start date + 1 in calendar days, and report medians and 85th percentiles rather than averages.
  • A short cycle time with a long lead time means work is waiting in the queue, not that the team is slow. Fix it by shortening the queue and deciding faster, not by working harder.
  • Little's Law applies to both: average lead time ≈ items in the whole system ÷ throughput; average cycle time ≈ items in progress ÷ throughput.
  • Show lead time to customers and stakeholders; use cycle time and work item age inside the team to improve the process.

What is the difference between cycle time and lead time?

Lead time is the elapsed time from when a request is made to when it is delivered to the customer. Cycle time is the elapsed time from when the team starts work on an item to when it is finished. Lead time includes the wait before work starts, so cycle time is always part of lead time.

Put simply, lead time is measured from the customer's point of view and cycle time from the team's. A customer who asks for a report on Monday and receives it three weeks later experiences three weeks, even if the report was only in progress for four days. The relationship is:

Lead time = waiting before work starts + cycle time + any waiting between finishing and delivery

Where the clock starts and stops on a Kanban board

Most confusion disappears once you mark the points on your Kanban board where each clock starts and stops. Five points matter:

  1. Request logged: the item is added to the backlog or arrives through a request form.
  2. Commitment point: the team agrees to deliver it, usually by pulling it into a Ready column. Before this, it is only an option.
  3. Work started: the item enters the first in-progress column.
  4. Work finished: the item reaches Done.
  5. Delivered: the customer can use it, for example after a scheduled release.
MeasureFromToWhat it shows
Lead time (customer view)1. Request logged5. DeliveredThe customer's total wait
Lead time from commitment2. Commitment point5. DeliveredHow long the team takes once it has said yes
Cycle time3. Work started4. Work finishedHow long work takes once started
Wait after finish4. Work finished5. DeliveredRelease batching and handover delays

On many teams commitment and start are the same column, and finish and delivery happen together, so the only difference between the two measures is the backlog wait. Where they are separate, the extra segments often hide the largest waits.

Why definitions differ between sources

If three articles gave you three definitions, you are not imagining it. Different communities draw the boundaries in different places:

SourceLead timeCycle time
The Kanban Guide (v2025.5)Not defined; the guide uses cycle time onlyElapsed time from when a work item started to when it finished
Kanban University (2023)Customer lead time: from receiving the request to delivering it. System lead time: from the commitment point to the first column with no WIP limitNot a core glossary term
DORA metricsChange lead time: from a code commit to successful deployment in productionNot one of the four DORA metrics
Azure DevOpsFrom when a work item is created until it is completedFrom when it first enters In Progress until it is completed
ManufacturingOrder to delivery, including queues and transportOften the time to complete one unit at a process step

Kanban University changed its definition of customer lead time in 2023: it used to start at the commitment point and now starts when the request is received. That is why older articles start lead time at commitment and newer ones at the request. Both measure something real; they just measure different spans.

Write your definitions down as a policy

The label matters much less than the boundary. Agree the start and stop points as an explicit board policy and use them on every report:

Tip

Example policy: 'Lead time runs from the date a request is logged to the date it is released to users. Cycle time runs from the date a card first enters In Progress to the date it enters Done. Both are counted in calendar days as end date − start date + 1. Items that move backwards keep their original start date.'

How to calculate lead time and cycle time

Both are simple date arithmetic on finished items. Using the same convention as our cycle time guide:

  • Lead time = delivery date − request date + 1 (calendar days)
  • Cycle time = finish date − start date + 1 (calendar days)

The + 1 is a counting convention: an item requested, started and finished on the same day counts as 1 day rather than 0. Some tools count elapsed hours instead. Either works if you are consistent and say which you use. Calendar days suit lead time in particular, because customers wait through weekends too.

  1. Agree the boundaries for both measures and write them down.
  2. Record the dates on every card: requested, started, finished and, if different, delivered. Digital tools log these from status changes; on a physical board, write them on the card, as shown in our Kanban cards guide.
  3. Calculate per item once it is finished, with a third column for queue time before start (start date − request date). For unfinished items, track work item age instead.
  4. Summarise the distribution with the median and 85th percentile for each measure.
  5. Compare the two medians. The gap is the typical waiting time outside the process, and it tells you where to look first.
  6. Review trends regularly, over several weeks rather than single values.

A spreadsheet with columns for item, type, requested, started, finished, delivered, queue time, cycle time and lead time is enough for most teams. Our free Kanban board template already has started, finished and cycle time columns that you can extend with a requested date.

Averages, medians and percentiles

Lead and cycle time data are usually skewed: most items finish quickly and a few take much longer, pulling the average up. Report the median (half of items finished within this time) and the 85th percentile (85% finished within this time). The cycle time guide shows the nearest-rank method for calculating percentiles by hand.

Worked example: one request from start to finish

This illustrative example follows one request through a digital team's board. The team releases to users every Tuesday, so finished work waits for the next release.

PointDateWhat happened
Request loggedMon 14 SepA sales manager asks for a new filter on the customer report
Commitment pointMon 21 SepPulled into Ready at the weekly replenishment meeting
Work startedMon 28 SepA developer picks it up
Work finishedFri 2 OctBuilt, reviewed and tested; card moves to Done
DeliveredTue 6 OctReleased in the Tuesday release
  1. Cycle time = 2 Oct − 28 Sep + 1 = 5 days.
  2. Lead time = 6 Oct − 14 Sep + 1 = 23 days.
  3. Lead time from commitment = 6 Oct − 21 Sep + 1 = 16 days.
  4. Break the lead time down: 7 days before commitment, 7 days committed but not started, 5 days of cycle time and 4 days waiting for the release. 7 + 7 + 5 + 4 = 23.

The sales manager waited 23 days for something the team took 5 days to build. Asking the developer to work faster could save a day or two at most; replenishing twice a week and releasing as soon as work is done could save up to 11 days without anyone working harder.

Tip

If your team only reports cycle time, this request looks excellent. If your stakeholders only see lead time, it looks slow. Seeing both side by side shows where the time actually went.

  • Waiting dominates. Of 181 days of lead time, 132 (about 73%) were spent in the queue. The process is reasonably quick; the queue is not.
  • Lead time is much less predictable. Queue time ranges from 0 to 29 days depending on prioritisation, so the 85th percentile lead time is four times the cycle time figure.
  • Expedites have a cost. R-03 went straight in, which is right for a genuine emergency, but every jump lengthens the wait for everything behind it.
  • Customer promises should use lead time. 'We usually finish in about 5 days' is true of cycle time and misleading about the requester's experience. '85% of requests are delivered within 32 days' is honest and gives the team a clear target to improve.

What the gap between lead time and cycle time tells you

Comparing the two medians is one of the quickest diagnostics you can run on a board:

PatternLikely causeWhat to do
Short cycle time, short lead timeSmall queue, quick decisions, healthy WIPProtect WIP limits and replenish little and often
Short cycle time, long lead timeLong backlog, slow prioritisation, release batchingShorten the queue, decide faster, release more often
Long cycle time, lead time slightly longerToo much WIP, large items, internal waits for reviewsLower WIP limits, split work, speed up reviews
Both long and highly variableOverloaded system: starting and committing to too muchStop starting and start finishing: cut WIP and the queue together

Watch the trend too. If lead time is rising while cycle time is flat, demand is outrunning throughput and the queue is growing. A cumulative flow diagram shows this as a widening backlog band.

Lead time, cycle time and Little's Law

Little's Law, published by John Little in 1961, links average time in a system with the number of items in it and the rate at which they leave. It works for either boundary, as long as you count WIP and throughput over the same one:

  • Average cycle time ≈ average items in progress ÷ average throughput
  • Average lead time ≈ average items in the whole system (committed queue + in progress) ÷ average throughput

Worked example: halving lead time without working faster

An illustrative team finishes about 4 items a week, with 6 items in progress and 20 committed items waiting in Ready.

BeforeAfter
Items in progress66
Committed items waiting208
Throughput4 a week4 a week
Average cycle time (6 ÷ 4)1.5 weeks1.5 weeks
Average lead time26 ÷ 4 = 6.5 weeks14 ÷ 4 = 3.5 weeks

The team caps Ready at 8 items, holds the rest as uncommitted options and replenishes weekly. Nobody works faster and throughput is unchanged, yet average lead time falls by almost half. Lowering WIP limits inside the process would shorten cycle time in the same way.

Two cautions. Little's Law describes averages in a reasonably stable system, so use it to understand cause and effect, not to forecast a single item. And an uncommitted backlog of ideas is not a queue: many options will be discarded, so apply the law from the commitment point, or from the request only if every request will be done.

This table shows how lead time and cycle time sit among the other common flow measures.

MeasureWhat it isQuestion it answers

Cycle timeStart to finish, per finished itemOnce started, how long does work take?
Work item ageTime since an unfinished item startedWhich items in progress are at risk now?
ThroughputItems finished per periodHow much do we deliver?

Two similar-sounding terms are different. In manufacturing, cycle time often means the time to produce one unit at a workstation; see lean manufacturing. In scheduling, a lead is an overlap that lets a successor start before its predecessor finishes, explained in leads and lags.

Which one should you report, and to whom?

AudienceMain measureWhy
Customers and requestersLead time, as a probabilityIt is the wait they actually experience
Stakeholders and sponsorsLead time and throughputWhen things will arrive and how much will be delivered
The delivery teamCycle time, work item age and WIPWhat the team controls day to day
Service desks with an SLALead time against the agreed targetContracts usually start the clock when the ticket is raised

To give customers an honest forecast from lead time data:

  1. Collect lead times for at least 15–20 recent finished items of a similar type.
  2. Find the 85th percentile, as in the ten-item example.
  3. Phrase it as a probability: '85% of requests like this are delivered within 32 days.'
  4. Offer a limited expedite route for genuine emergencies.
  5. Recalculate monthly and publish the improved figure as queue time falls.

The Kanban Guide's service level expectation applies the same idea to cycle time. A contractual commitment belongs in a service level agreement (SLA). To forecast many items at once, combine throughput history with a Monte Carlo simulation, as explained in Kanban planning.

How to reduce lead time

Because most lead time is usually waiting, the biggest gains come from managing the queue and the decisions around it.

Shorten and prune the queue

A long committed backlog is a promise you cannot keep quickly. Cap committed items, move the rest back to options and remove requests that are no longer needed. Regular backlog refinement keeps the queue small and current.

Replenish little and often

If you pull new work once a month, a request that arrives the day after replenishment waits a month before it is even considered. Weekly or on-demand replenishment cuts that wait. This is the core of a pull system: work enters when there is capacity.

Decide faster, and say not yet sooner

Requests often wait for someone to decide whether they will be done at all. Agree who decides, how often and on what criteria. A clear 'not yet' lets the requester find another route.

Remove waits after done

If finished work waits for a monthly release, lead time includes that wait. More frequent releases, or continuous delivery in software, close the gap. Elsewhere, hand work over as soon as it is ready.

Use expedite lanes sparingly

Every expedited item delays everything behind it, so limit expedites to one at a time. To find the longest waits across the whole flow, including outside your team, use value stream mapping.

How to reduce cycle time

  • Limit work in progress. Fewer items in progress means each finishes sooner.
  • Split large items into smaller useful pieces; techniques for splitting user stories apply to most work.
  • Shrink internal waits for review, testing and approval.
  • Remove recurring blockers and swarm on items older than your 85th percentile.
  • Automate repetitive steps such as testing and deployment.

Cutting cycle time also cuts lead time, because cycle time is part of it; a shorter queue, though, leaves cycle time unchanged. See our full guide to measuring and reducing cycle time.

Measuring lead time and cycle time in project management tools

Most digital boards calculate both from status history, but each draws the boundaries its own way, so check them against your policy.

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, combines request intake forms with custom workflow statuses and dashboards. Logging requests through a form records when each one arrived, giving a clean start point for lead time. See our Wrike review.
  • Jira includes cumulative flow diagrams and a control chart for cycle time in which you choose the statuses that count. See our Jira review.
  • Azure DevOps has separate Lead Time and Cycle Time widgets, using the Microsoft definitions in the table above.
  • ClickUp offers cycle time cards for dashboards and cumulative flow in sprint reporting. See our ClickUp review.
  • A spreadsheet works with any board: record the dates, apply the formulas and use percentile functions.

Whatever the tool, lead time is only accurate if requests are logged when they arrive. A simple work request template or intake form solves that.

Common mistakes when comparing cycle time and lead time

  • Using the terms interchangeably without defining where each starts and stops.
  • Reporting cycle time to customers. It understates their wait.
  • Measuring only active time and calling it cycle time.
  • Relying on averages instead of medians and 85th percentiles.
  • Starting the lead time clock late, when a request is typed in weeks after it arrived by email.
  • Ignoring the wait after done when delivery happens later than finish.
  • Mixing very different work types in one figure.
  • Turning either number into a target for individuals, which leads to gaming.

Review lead time, cycle time and the other flow measures together at a regular Kanban review meeting, and change one thing at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between cycle time and lead time?

Lead time runs from when a customer requests an item to when it is delivered. Cycle time runs from when the team starts work on it to when it is finished, so cycle time is part of lead time.

Is lead time always longer than cycle time?

It is never shorter when both are measured on the same item, because cycle time sits inside it. It is usually longer, because items wait before work starts and sometimes after it finishes. The two are equal only when work starts the moment it is requested and is delivered the moment it is finished.

How do you calculate lead time and cycle time?

Lead time = delivery date − request date + 1, and cycle time = finish date − start date + 1, in calendar days. Calculate them for finished items and summarise with medians and 85th percentiles.

What is lead time in Kanban?

It depends on the source. Kanban University defines customer lead time as the time from receiving a request to delivering it. The Kanban Guide does not define lead time and uses cycle time instead.

What is the commitment point?

The point where the team decides it will deliver an item, usually when it is pulled into a Ready or Committed column. Before that, items are options that may be discarded. Some definitions of lead time start here.

Which is more important, lead time or cycle time?

Neither on its own. Lead time matters most to customers because it is their wait; cycle time matters most to the team because it is what they improve directly. Tracking both shows whether delays come from the queue or the process.

What is the difference between lead time, cycle time and takt time?

Lead time and cycle time measure how long items take. Takt time is a target rate: available working time divided by customer demand, telling you how often you must finish an item to keep up.

Is DORA lead time for changes the same as cycle time?

No. DORA's change lead time runs from a code commit to successful deployment in production, so it covers the delivery pipeline only. Cycle time usually starts earlier, when work on the item begins.

How do lead time and cycle time relate to throughput?

Lead time and cycle time measure how long each item takes; throughput counts items finished per period. Little's Law links them: average time in a system ≈ average items in it ÷ throughput.

Should I use calendar days or working days?

Calendar days are usually better, especially for lead time, because customers wait through weekends. Whichever you choose, use it for both measures and say so on reports.

Does a 'lead' in project scheduling relate to lead time?

No. A lead is a scheduling overlap that lets a successor activity start before its predecessor finishes. Lead time is a flow measure of how long a request takes to be delivered.

What is the difference between lead time and work item age?

Lead time is measured on finished items. Work item age is how long an unfinished item has been in progress so far, so you can act on it before it turns into a long lead time.
