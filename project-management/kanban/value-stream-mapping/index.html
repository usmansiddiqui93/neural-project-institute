Value Stream Mapping: Steps, Metrics, Symbols and Examples

Value stream mapping (VSM) is a Lean method for drawing every step, handoff and wait involved in delivering a product or service, from the customer's request to the customer receiving it, with time and quality data for each step. A first map often shows work spending far more time waiting than being worked on, which points the team straight at the queues, approvals and rework worth fixing. This guide shows you how to build a current and future state map step by step, which metrics and symbols to use, and how to turn the result into a Kanban board, with three worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • A value stream map shows the whole flow of one product, service or request type, including information flow and a timeline of process time against waiting time.
  • For each step, record process time, lead time and % complete and accurate (%C&A). Totals give the activity ratio (flow efficiency) and rolled %C&A.
  • Map the current state by walking the process with the people who do the work, then design a future state and an action plan that removes the biggest delays.
  • In knowledge work the largest gains usually come from shorter queues, fewer approvals and handoffs, better intake quality and smaller, more frequent releases.
  • A map is only useful if it leads to change: turn improvement ideas into owned actions, track the metrics on a Kanban board and re-map when you reach the future state.

What is value stream mapping?

Value stream mapping is a visual Lean technique for documenting how work and information flow through every step needed to deliver a product or service to a customer. Each step is recorded with its process time, waiting time and quality, so the map shows where time is lost and helps a team design a faster, simpler process.

A value stream is every action, value-adding or not, that turns a customer request into something the customer receives. You map one at a time: one product family, service or request type, such as "standard software change" or "new client onboarding".

Three features set a value stream map apart from an ordinary process diagram:

  • End-to-end scope: it runs from customer to customer across team and department boundaries.
  • Information flow: it shows how work is triggered and communicated (emails, tickets, meetings), not only the work steps.
  • A timeline: a line along the bottom compares time worked with time waiting.

Teams usually draw two maps. The current state map shows how the process works today; the future state map shows how it should work within a set horizon, often six to twelve months, with an implementation plan to get there. Steps are classed as value-adding, necessary but non-value-adding (such as a regulatory check) or non-value-adding (waiting, rework, duplicate data entry), which you aim to remove.

Where value stream mapping came from

Value stream mapping grew out of the Toyota Production System, where Toyota used what it called material and information flow diagrams to see how parts and instructions moved through a plant. Mike Rother and John Shook turned the practice into a step-by-step method in their workbook *Learning to See*, published by the Lean Enterprise Institute in 1998, which received a Shingo Research award in 1999 and gave VSM its standard icons.

Karen Martin and Mike Osterling's *Value Stream Mapping* (McGraw-Hill, 2013) adapted the method for office, service and knowledge work, using measures such as process time, lead time, % complete and accurate and the activity ratio. Software teams now map the path from idea to production, and healthcare and public sector teams map patient and case journeys.

Value stream mapping vs process mapping

People often use "process map" and "value stream map" interchangeably, but they answer different questions. A process map asks "what are the steps?". A value stream map asks "how long does the customer wait, and why?".

FeatureValue stream mapProcess map or flowchartSwimlane diagram
ScopeOne value stream, end to end, often across departmentsAny process, at any level of detailAny process, split by role or team
DataProcess time, lead time, %C&A, WIP, peopleUsually noneUsually none
Information flowShown explicitlyRarelySometimes, as messages between lanes
Main purposeFind and remove waste and delayDocument or standardise a processClarify responsibilities and handoffs
Best used whenLead times are long and nobody can see whyTraining, procedures, auditsOwnership is unclear

The tools work well together: a swimlane diagram shows who does each step, and the value stream map adds time and quality data. To list steps, owners, inputs and outputs first, use our free workflow template.

Key value stream mapping metrics

Every step on the map gets a small data box. You do not need every metric below; for knowledge work, process time, lead time and %C&A are usually enough to start.

MetricWhat it meansHow to collect it
Process time (PT)Hands-on time to complete the step for one item, without interruptionAsk the people doing the work
Lead time (LT)Elapsed time from work arriving at the step to passing it on, including waitingTicket or card timestamps, emails
Wait timeLT minus PT: time queued or blockedCalculated
% complete and accurate (%C&A)Share of work the next step can use without correcting, adding or clarifyingAsk the downstream step
Rolled %C&A (rolled first pass yield)Approximate share of items that pass every step with no reworkMultiply the step values
Activity ratioTotal PT ÷ total LT, also known as flow efficiencyCalculated
WIP and batch sizeItems queued at each step, and how many move togetherCount cards or tickets
Takt timePace of customer demand: available time ÷ demandCalculated

Manufacturing maps also record uptime and changeover time for machines; most knowledge-work maps can skip them.

The core calculations

  • Total lead time = sum of the step lead times along the longest path through the map.
  • Total process time = sum of the step process times.
  • Activity ratio = total process time ÷ total lead time × 100%.
  • Rolled %C&A = %C&A of step 1 × %C&A of step 2 × … × %C&A of the last step.

Use one time unit throughout; for office work, convert process hours to working days (8 hours) before you divide. The activity ratio is the same measure our guide to flow efficiency calculates for Kanban boards, here on a lead-time basis across the whole value stream.

A note on the term cycle time

On manufacturing maps, C/T (cycle time) usually means the time one operator takes to complete their work elements. In Kanban, cycle time is the elapsed time an item takes from start to finish. In knowledge work, label data boxes "process time" and "lead time" to avoid confusion; our guide to cycle time vs lead time explains where each measure starts and stops.

Takt time

Takt time is how often the process must finish one item to keep up with demand. An illustrative service desk with 450 working minutes a day and 30 access requests a day has a takt time of 450 ÷ 30 = 15 minutes; any step needing more capacity than that per request will build a queue. Takt time suits repetitive work; for varied project work, throughput and lead time are more meaningful.

You do not need the official icons. Sticky notes for steps, another colour for queues, arrows for information flow and a timeline along the bottom are enough.

How to create a value stream map step by step

These steps work on a wall or a digital whiteboard. Allow half a day for a simple service and up to two days for a complex, cross-department value stream.

Phase 1: Prepare

  1. Choose the value stream: one product, service or request type with a visible problem, such as long lead times or complaints.
  2. Write a problem statement, for example "standard changes take about seven weeks; the business expects two". Our problem statement template helps.
  3. Find a sponsor with authority across the departments involved, or the future state will stall at the first boundary.
  4. Set the boundaries: agree the first step ("request received") and the last ("change live"), and note what is out of scope.
  5. Form the team from people who do the work at each step, plus the requester and the receiving customer.

Phase 2: Map the current state

  1. Walk the process. Follow real items, in person or by tracing tickets and emails. Ask what people actually do, not what the procedure says.
  2. List 5 to 15 major steps from left to right. Detailed sub-steps belong in a process map.
  3. Add a data box to each step: PT, LT, %C&A and people. Use typical values from recent items, not best cases.
  4. Mark queues between steps with the number of items waiting.
  5. Draw the information flow along the top: how requests arrive and who sets priorities.
  6. Draw the timeline and calculate total lead time, total process time, activity ratio and rolled %C&A.

Phase 3: Analyse

  1. Rank the delays: the longest waits and the steps with the lowest %C&A.
  2. Find root causes: batching, approval rules, capacity, priorities or missing information. A fishbone diagram or our root cause analysis template helps.
  3. Mark kaizen bursts where a change would most affect lead time or rework.

Phase 4: Design the future state

*Learning to See* frames future state design around questions about takt time, continuous flow, pull and scheduling. For knowledge work, ask:

  • Which steps add no customer value and can be removed or merged?
  • Where can work flow straight to the next step, and where must a queue remain, with what WIP limit?
  • Which approvals can be delegated or replaced with clear criteria?
  • How can intake improve so later steps receive complete work?
  • Can batches (releases, approval boards) be smaller and more frequent?
  • Where is the bottleneck, and how will you protect it? See the theory of constraints.

Phase 5: Plan, implement and sustain

  1. Turn each kaizen burst into an action with an owner, date and target metric, using our action plan template.
  2. Start with the biggest delay the team controls.
  3. Track the metrics monthly against the current state baseline.
  4. Re-map once you reach the future state or the process changes significantly.

Worked example: current state map for a software change request

This illustrative example follows a standard change request at an internal IT team, from request to live change. Process time is in hours; lead time is in working days, including waiting.

StepProcess time (hours)Lead time (working days)%C&ANotes
1. Intake and triage0.5360%Requests often lack business detail
2. Analysis and specification4580%Analyst chases requester for answers
3. Approval (weekly change board)0.5495%Waits for the next board meeting
4. Development16885%Long ready queue; developers juggle many items
5. Code review2290%Reviews picked up when convenient
6. Testing6475%Mostly manual regression tests
7. Release (monthly window)1998%Finished changes wait for the release date
Total3035

Work through the calculation:

  1. Total lead time: 3 + 5 + 4 + 8 + 2 + 4 + 9 = 35 working days, about seven weeks.
  2. Total process time: 0.5 + 4 + 0.5 + 16 + 2 + 6 + 1 = 30 hours, which is 30 ÷ 8 = 3.75 working days.
  3. Activity ratio: 3.75 ÷ 35 = 10.7%. Nearly 90% of the customer's wait is waiting.
  4. Rolled %C&A: 0.60 × 0.80 × 0.95 × 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.75 × 0.98 = about 26%. Only about one change in four gets through without someone correcting or clarifying something.
  5. Rank the waits (lead time minus process time in days): release window (about 8.9), development queue (6), analysis (4.5), approval (about 3.9), testing (3.25), intake (about 2.9) and code review (1.75).

Working faster would barely help: halving every step's process time would cut lead time by less than two days. The big gains lie in the monthly release window, the development queue, the weekly approval board and poor intake quality, which causes rework downstream.

MeasureCurrent stateFuture state

Future state lead times are 1, 2, 0.5, 4, 1, 2 and 3 days for the seven steps (13.5 in total), and the only process time change is testing, from 6 to 4 hours. Rolled %C&A is 0.90 × 0.80 × 0.95 × 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.90 × 0.98, about 46%. Process time barely changes, but lead time falls by about 61% ((35 − 13.5) ÷ 35) because work waits less, approvals and releases happen in smaller batches and fewer items bounce back for rework. These targets are planning assumptions until the changes are made and re-measured.

Worked example: an office value stream with parallel paths

Not every value stream is a single line. In this illustrative onboarding process, an HR path and an IT path start when a new employee signs their contract and both must finish before day one.

PathStepProcess time (hours)Lead time (working days)
HREmployee record and payroll set-up13
HRInduction scheduled12
ITAccount request raised and created0.52
ITLaptop ordered and built38
ITSystem access approved by each system owner16
BothDay-one readiness check0.51
  • Lead time follows the longest path. The IT path takes 2 + 8 + 6 = 16 days, against 5 for HR, so total lead time is 16 + 1 = 17 working days.
  • Process time adds up across all steps: 7 hours, or about 0.9 days.
  • Activity ratio: 0.875 ÷ 17 = about 5%.

Speeding up HR would change nothing for the new starter, because the IT path sets the lead time. The future state keeps a few pre-built laptops in stock and uses pre-approved, role-based access bundles, cutting the IT path to about 3 days (accounts in 1 day, then laptop and access in parallel within 2). The HR path, at 5 days, is now the longest, so lead time becomes about 6 working days and HR set-up is the next target. It is the same logic as a schedule's critical path: once you fix the longest path, another takes its place.

How to spot waste on a value stream map

Lean identifies seven classic wastes. Mary and Tom Poppendieck's *Lean Software Development* (2003) translated them for software, and the same ideas apply to most knowledge work.

WasteKnowledge-work equivalentWhere it shows on the map
WaitingQueues for review, approval or releaseLarge gaps between LT and PT
InventoryPartly done work, long backlogsInventory triangles with high counts
OverproductionFeatures or reports nobody usesSteps with no clear customer
Over-processingExtra sign-offs, gold-plating, duplicate data entrySeveral approval steps in a row
TransportHandoffs between people and teamsMany arrows and people for one item
MotionTask switching, searching for informationHigh WIP per person
DefectsBugs, incomplete requests, rework loopsLow %C&A and return arrows

At the map, ask: where does work wait longest? Which step gets the poorest input? Where do several people approve the same thing? Which queue has no limit? The answers usually point to policies, not people.

From value stream map to Kanban board

A value stream map is a snapshot; a Kanban board keeps the flow visible every day. The map gives you most of the board design:

  1. Steps become columns. Use the future state steps, so the board reflects the process you are moving towards.
  2. Queues become waiting columns, such as "ready for review" or "ready for release", so waiting is visible rather than hidden in active columns.
  3. Queue sizes inform WIP limits. Start near current counts and lower them gradually; see our guide to WIP limits.
  4. Policies become explicit. Write the future state rules (definition of ready, review pick-up, approval criteria) on the board.
  5. Metrics continue. Track cycle time, throughput, work item age and flow efficiency, as our Kanban metrics guide explains.

Moving from push, where work is emailed onward whenever it is done, to a pull system is often the biggest single change in a knowledge-work future state. A cumulative flow diagram shows whether queues are shrinking, and blocker clustering helps with delays the map did not predict. Our free Kanban board template gives you a starting board.

Running a value stream mapping workshop

The people who do the work know where it waits, and they need to own the future state for it to stick. Invite:

  • A sponsor or value stream owner with authority across the departments involved, who approves the future state.
  • A facilitator, ideally neutral, who keeps the level of detail consistent and challenges assumptions.
  • People who do the work at each step, not just their managers.
  • A requester and a receiving customer to represent both ends of the flow.

Sample agenda for a one-day workshop

TimeActivityOutput
30 minutesProblem statement, scope and boundariesAgreed start, end and goal
90 minutesMap current state steps and information flowSteps, queues and arrows on the wall
60 minutesAdd data boxes and timeline; calculate totalsPT, LT, %C&A, activity ratio
60 minutesIdentify waste and root causesRanked delays and kaizen bursts
90 minutesDesign the future stateFuture state map and target metrics
30 minutesAction plan and next review dateOwned actions with dates

Before the day, pull timestamps for 15 to 30 recently completed items, and prepare a long wall with paper and sticky notes in three colours (steps, queues, problems) or a digital whiteboard with the icons ready.

Value stream mapping in software development and DevOps

Software teams map the journey from idea to working software in production. Typical findings are waits for environments, manual testing, change boards and infrequent releases, as in the worked example above.

The map is a natural starting point for DevOps improvement because it separates delays inside the delivery pipeline from those outside it, in prioritisation, funding or approvals. DORA metrics such as lead time for changes and deployment frequency measure the pipeline; the map covers the whole journey. Automated testing and continuous delivery often appear as kaizen bursts. Value stream management tools go further, pulling timestamps from delivery systems to keep a live view of flow.

Benefits and limitations of value stream mapping

BenefitsLimitations
Shows the whole flow, including the parts no single team ownsA snapshot: it ages quickly unless you re-map or track flow continuously
Separates waiting from working with simple numbersData is often estimated, so treat totals as approximate
Builds shared understanding across departmentsNeeds time from busy people and a sponsor with authority
Focuses improvement on the customer's experienceWorks best for repeatable work; one-off projects vary too much
Produces a concrete, measurable action planCan become a documentation exercise if nobody acts on the future state

Common value stream mapping mistakes

  • Mapping the procedure, not reality. Walk real items and ask what actually happens.
  • Too much detail. Fifty boxes hide the big queues. Stay at 5 to 15 steps.
  • Leaving out the people who do the work. Managers rarely know where items wait.
  • Mapping only your own team. The longest waits are often at the boundaries with other teams.
  • Skipping the data. Without PT, LT and %C&A, you have a process map, not a value stream map.
  • Using best-case times instead of typical values from recent items.
  • Speeding up the work instead of the waits. Shorter process time rarely moves lead time much.
  • No owner for the future state. Every kaizen burst needs a name and a date.

Frequently asked questions

What is value stream mapping?

Value stream mapping is a Lean technique for drawing every step, handoff and wait involved in delivering a product or service, with process time, lead time and quality for each step, so you can see and remove waste and delay.

What are the steps of value stream mapping?

Choose a value stream and problem, set boundaries, form the team, walk the process, map steps with data and information flow, calculate the timeline, identify waste, design a future state and turn improvements into an owned action plan.

What is the difference between a process map and a value stream map?

A process map shows the steps. A value stream map adds process time, lead time, quality and information flow across the whole journey from customer request to delivery, so it shows where time is lost.

What is a current state and future state value stream map?

The current state map shows how the process works today, with real times and queues. The future state map shows the improved process you aim to reach, usually within six to twelve months.

What data do you collect for a value stream map?

For each step, collect process time, lead time, % complete and accurate, the number of people and the queue size. Totals give lead time, process time, activity ratio and rolled %C&A.

What does %C&A mean in value stream mapping?

% complete and accurate is the share of work a step receives that it can use without correcting, adding or clarifying anything. Multiplying all the step values gives rolled %C&A, the share of items that pass through with no rework.

What is the activity ratio on a value stream map?

Total process time divided by total lead time, as a percentage. It is the same measure as flow efficiency on a lead-time basis.

What symbols are used in value stream mapping?

Common icons include process and data boxes, inventory triangles, push arrows, supermarkets, information arrows, kaizen bursts and a timeline. Sticky notes work just as well for knowledge work.

Who created value stream mapping?

It grew from Toyota's material and information flow diagrams. Mike Rother and John Shook's 1998 workbook Learning to See made it a widely used method.

How long does a value stream mapping workshop take?

Typically half a day for a simple service and one to two days for a complex, cross-department value stream, plus preparation to gather data from recent items.

Can value stream mapping be used in software development?

Yes. Software teams map the path from idea to production to find waits in approvals, testing, environments and releases, and the results often feed DevOps and continuous delivery improvements.

How does a value stream map help design a Kanban board?

The future state steps become columns, the queues become waiting columns with WIP limits, and the policies agreed in the future state are written on the board.

How often should you update a value stream map?

Re-map when you reach the future state or when the process changes significantly, often every six to twelve months. Track flow metrics on your board in between.
