Blocker Clustering: How to Find and Fix Recurring Delays

Blocker clustering is a Kanban improvement technique in which a team records every blocked work item, groups the blockers by cause and adds up the days each group cost, so it can fix the causes that delay delivery most. Instead of arguing from memory about what slows you down, you end up with a ranked, evidence-based list of problems. This guide explains what counts as a blocker, how to capture the data, how to run the clustering session, and how to prioritise and fix the results, using a full worked example you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • Blocker clustering turns individual blocked cards into data: reason, start date, end date and whether the cause was internal or external.
  • Group similar blockers, then rank the clusters by total blocked days, not by how often they occurred.
  • A few clusters usually account for most of the lost time, so fixing the top two or three gives the biggest gain.
  • Prioritise with three rules: fix what is cost-effective, fix blockers at the constraint first, and weigh impact against ease of solving.
  • Use 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram to find the root cause of a cluster, then run a small, measurable experiment.
  • Clusters also feed risk reviews and forecasts: they tell you how likely an item is to be blocked and for how long.

What is blocker clustering?

Blocker clustering is a structured way of analysing blocked work. Each time a work item cannot progress, the team records why and for how long. After several weeks, the team groups the blocker records into clusters of similar causes, totals the blocked days per cluster, and targets the clusters that cost the most time with improvement experiments.

The technique was developed by Klaus Leopold and popularised with Troy Magennis, who presented it at the Lean Kanban Central Europe conference in 2014 and described it in a 2015 InfoQ article on blockers and defects. Their central observation, reported by InfoQ, is that blockers are not isolated events: they have systemic causes, and they keep coming back until those causes are dealt with.

The output of blocker clustering is simple: a short table of causes ranked by blocked days, with a decision about which one or two to tackle next. That table is far more persuasive in a conversation with another team or a manager than a general complaint that things are slow.

It fits the Kanban practice of improving collaboratively and evolving experimentally; see Kanban principles.

Why blocked work matters more than it looks

A blocked card looks harmless on a board. It sits in its column with a red marker and nobody is working on it. In practice, blocked work damages flow in four ways.

  • It adds straight to cycle time. Every day an item is blocked is a day added to the time the customer waits. If your typical item takes eight days and spends two of them blocked, a quarter of your cycle time is blocker delay.
  • It lowers flow efficiency. Blocked time is pure waiting time, so it lowers the share of elapsed time spent actively working. See flow efficiency for how to calculate it.
  • It still uses work in progress. A blocked item usually counts against the column's WIP limit. Teams then either stall or start new work to stay busy, which increases WIP and, by Little's Law, lengthens average cycle time further.
  • It costs more at the constraint. A blocker at the stage that limits the whole system's output delays everything behind it. A blocker at a stage with spare capacity may cost almost nothing. See the Theory of Constraints.

There is also a human factor: once a blocker is cleared, people forget it. Retrospectives recall the latest or most dramatic blocker, not the one that quietly cost two days on a dozen items. Blocker clustering replaces memory with a record.

What counts as a blocker?

Before you collect anything, agree what you will record. The most common confusion is between work that is blocked and work that is simply waiting in a queue.

StateWhat it meansRecord as a blocker?
BlockedSomeone wants to work on the item now but cannot, because of something outside their control: missing information, a broken environment, an approval, another teamYes
Waiting in a queueThe item is finished at one stage and waiting for the next stage to pull it, for example in a 'Ready for test' columnNo, this is queue time; make it visible with a waiting column instead
Not startedThe item is in the backlog or options columnNo
Paused by choiceThe team deliberately set the item aside to work on something more urgentUsually no, but track it if it happens often, because it may point to an expedite or prioritisation problem
Defect foundRework is needed before the item can move onOptional. Leopold and Magennis treat defects as blockers too, because they tie up people and delay flow

A simple test: if someone could and would work on the item today but something stops them, it is blocked.

Internal and external blockers

The first split in any blocker clustering exercise is between causes the team controls and causes it does not.

  • Internal blockers are caused within the team and can be solved by the team alone: unclear acceptance criteria written by the team, a skill held by only one person, a broken local build, or work started before it was ready.
  • External blockers come from outside the team: another team's dependency, a customer sign-off, a shared test environment, a supplier, a legal or security approval.

Internal clusters can usually be improved at the next retrospective. External clusters need data and a conversation with whoever owns the cause, which is exactly where the blocked-day totals earn their keep.

Tip

Write your definition down as a one-line blocker policy, such as 'An item is blocked when the person working on it cannot continue for more than half a day because of something they cannot resolve themselves.' Put it on the board so everyone records blockers the same way.

How to record blockers on your board

The data collection must be quick, or people will stop doing it. The original approach is deliberately low-tech: put a red sticky note on the blocked card with a short reason, add a tick mark for every day it stays blocked during the daily meeting, and when the blocker is cleared, move the note to a separate 'blocker parking' area instead of throwing it away. Our guide to Kanban cards shows common ways to mark blocked cards.

Digital boards work the same way. Most tools let you flag a card as blocked and add a comment; some record the blocked period automatically. Whatever you use, make sure you can get these fields out at the end of the collection period.

Agree up front whether you count calendar days or working days and stick to it. Working days are fairer for teams that do not work weekends; calendar days match how the customer experiences the delay. Either is fine if you are consistent.

How to run blocker clustering, step by step

Here is the full cycle, from agreeing the rules to checking whether your fix worked.

  1. Agree the definition. Write the blocker policy and decide on calendar or working days.
  2. Mark blockers as they happen. Flag the card as soon as it is blocked, with a short reason and the date.
  3. Track duration daily. At the daily meeting, add a tick or confirm the blocked status. Clear the flag and note the date when the blocker is removed.
  4. Keep every record. Never discard a resolved blocker note. Move it to the parking area or make sure the tool keeps the history.
  5. Collect for long enough. Four to six weeks is a common first period. Low-volume teams may need longer to gather enough records to see patterns.
  6. Cluster the records. In a short workshop, split the records into internal and external, then group similar reasons together and give each cluster a clear name.
  7. Total and rank. For each cluster, count the blockers and add up the blocked days. Rank by blocked days.
  8. Prioritise and analyse. Apply the prioritisation rules below, choose one or two clusters, and find their root cause.
  9. Run an experiment and review. Agree a change, a measure and a review date. Keep collecting blocker data so you can see whether the cluster shrinks.

Running the clustering workshop

The clustering session itself rarely needs more than 90 minutes. A simple agenda:

TimeActivityOutput
10 minRecap the purpose and the blocker policy; show the period covered and number of items finishedShared context
15 minLay out all blocker records (cards on a wall or rows on a shared screen) and split them into internal and externalTwo groups
20 minSilently group similar reasons, then discuss and name each clusterNamed clusters
10 minCount blockers and total blocked days per clusterRanked table
20 minPlace the top clusters on the solvability matrix and choose one or twoAgreed focus
15 minStart the root cause discussion and agree who will design the experimentOwner and next step

Many teams run this session as part of a retrospective or a monthly service delivery review; see Kanban ceremonies for where it fits.

Worked example: one quarter of blocker data

The numbers below are illustrative, but the method is exactly what you would do with your own data. A product team of six tracked blockers for 12 weeks. In that period it finished 60 work items and recorded 23 blockers totalling 162 working days.

After the clustering workshop, the table looked like this:

ClusterTypeBlockersBlocked daysAverage days per blockerShare of blocked daysCumulative share
Test environment or test data unavailableExternal7466.628%28%
Waiting for customer sign-offExternal5418.225%54%
Waiting for another team's API changeExternal3279.017%70%
Unclear acceptance criteriaInternal4225.514%84%
Defect found in reviewInternal3155.09%93%
Database specialist unavailableInternal11111.07%100%
Total231627.0100%

How to read the results

  • Three clusters cause 70% of the delay. This Pareto pattern is common: a few causes account for most blocked time. The team does not need to fix everything.
  • Frequency and cost differ. Unclear acceptance criteria happened almost as often as customer sign-off delays but cost about half as many days. Ranking by count alone would have pointed the team at the wrong problem.
  • External causes dominate. Three-quarters of blocked days came from outside the team. That is a signal to take the data to the platform team and the customer, not to work harder.
  • One-off outliers need judgement. The single 11-day database blocker is expensive, but one record is not a pattern. Note it, and watch whether it recurs.
  • Blocker cost per item. 162 blocked days across 60 finished items is an average of 2.7 days of blocker delay per item. If the team's typical cycle time is around ten days, roughly a quarter of it is blocker delay.

You can also express each cluster as a risk. Seven of the 60 items, about 12%, hit a test environment blocker, and when they did, they lost about 6.6 days. That framing is useful in forecasting and in conversations about service levels, covered further below.

Tip

Draw the clusters as a bar chart sorted from largest to smallest blocked days, with the cumulative share as a line. Teams and stakeholders understand a Pareto chart at a glance.

How to prioritise blocker clusters

The biggest cluster is not automatically the one to fix first. In their InfoQ article, Leopold and Magennis offer three quick rules.

  1. Only fix what is cost-effective. Some blockers cost more to eliminate than the delay they cause. If removing a rare blocker would need a new environment that costs more than the time lost, live with it.
  2. Fix blockers at the constraint first. A blocker at the bottleneck delays the whole system; a blocker at a stage with spare capacity may not change delivery dates at all.
  3. Weigh impact against how easy it is to solve. Use a simple matrix to compare clusters on both dimensions.

A solvability matrix places each cluster by its impact (blocked days) and by how easy it is to solve. Read it from the top-left: high impact and easy to solve is where you start.

Ease of solvingHigh impactMedium impactLow impact
EasyDo now (quick win)Do soonDo when convenient
MediumPlan and do nextConsiderProbably leave
HardEscalate or make a business caseMonitorAccept

Applied to the worked example:

ClusterImpactEaseDecision
Test environment or test dataHighMedium: the platform team already provisions environments for othersPlan and do next; testing is also the team's constraint, so it moves to first place
Customer sign-offHighHard: depends on the client's own approval processMake the case with data; propose an agreed turnaround time
Another team's API changeMediumMediumConsider; raise in the cross-team planning meeting
Unclear acceptance criteriaMediumEasy: fully within the team's controlDo soon as a quick win

So the team chose two actions: an internal quick win on acceptance criteria, and a data-backed request to the platform team about test environments.

When to use simulation

For larger systems with many stages, intuition about which blocker matters most can be wrong, because the effects interact. Leopold and Magennis describe running a Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis: reduce each blocker type by a fixed percentage in a simulation model, one at a time, and see which change saves the most delivery time. They suggest using simulation only when the simple heuristics do not give a clear answer. Our guide to Monte Carlo simulation explains the general approach.

Find the root cause of a cluster

A cluster name such as 'unclear acceptance criteria' describes a symptom. Before you change anything, ask why it keeps happening. The 5 Whys technique is usually enough for a well-defined cluster:

  1. Why were items blocked on unclear acceptance criteria? Developers found missing edge cases halfway through the work.
  2. Why were edge cases missing? The criteria were written by one person shortly before the item was pulled.
  3. Why only one person? Refinement had been reduced to save meeting time.
  4. Why did that matter? Testers no longer saw items before they were started, so their questions came late.
  5. Why was there no other check? The team had no explicit policy for when an item is ready to pull.

The root cause here is a missing replenishment policy, not careless writing. A fix might be a short three-person review (product, development, testing) before an item can enter 'In progress'.

For broad clusters with many contributing factors, such as 'test environment unavailable', a cause-and-effect diagram works better than a single chain of whys.

Turn clusters into improvement experiments

Treat each fix as an experiment with a clear hypothesis, a measure and a review date. That makes it easy to see whether the change worked and to drop it if it did not.

ElementExample: acceptance criteriaExample: test environments
HypothesisIf product, development and testing review each item before it is pulled, blockers from unclear criteria will fallIf the platform team provides on-request test environments within one working day, environment blockers will fall
ChangeAdd a 15-minute 'three amigos' check as an explicit pull policyAgree a request process and turnaround time with the platform team
MeasureBlocked days in this cluster per monthBlocked days in this cluster per month; cycle time 85th percentile
TargetBelow 3 blocked days per monthBelow 6 blocked days per month
ReviewNext monthly blocker reviewAfter eight weeks

Keep collecting blocker data during the experiment. If the target cluster shrinks but another grows, you may have moved the problem rather than solved it.

Record what you learned, including experiments that did not work. A short entry in your lessons learned log stops the next team from repeating the same test.

Using blocker clusters for risk and forecasting

Blocker data is also risk data. Each cluster tells you two things: how likely an item is to be hit, and how much delay it causes when it is. In the worked example, about 8% of items waited for customer sign-off and lost an average of 8.2 days. That is a known, quantified risk to every new item.

David J Anderson's writing on the Kanban Method describes blocker clustering as a key input to the Risk Review, one of the Kanban Method's feedback cadences, where the probability and impact of each blocker type inform how the service is planned and protected. You can use the same numbers in several ways:

  • Service level expectations. If one cluster regularly pushes items beyond your target cycle time, either fix it or reflect it honestly in the service level you agree. See Kanban planning and our guide to service level agreements.
  • Forecasts. Probabilistic forecasting methods already include past blocker delay, because it is part of historical cycle time. Clustering explains where that spread comes from and what could narrow it.
  • Risk registers. Large external clusters belong in the project risk register with an owner and a response, especially when they depend on suppliers or clients.
  • Early warning. A sudden rise in one blocker type, such as items waiting for review, often shows a new queue forming. Spot it on a WIP aging chart before it hits delivery dates.

Common blocker clusters and countermeasures

Every team's clusters are different, but some appear again and again in knowledge work. Use this table as a starting point for ideas, not as a list of answers; the right fix depends on your root cause.

ClusterUsuallyPossible countermeasures
Waiting for another teamExternalMake dependencies visible earlier, agree turnaround times, invite the other team to planning, or reduce the dependency by changing team boundaries
Approvals and sign-offExternalAgree approval service levels, delegate approval limits, approve smaller batches, or approve the policy instead of each item
Test environments or dataExternal or internalAutomate environment set-up, share booking visibility, create reusable test data sets
Unclear or changing requirementsInternalExplicit ready-to-pull policy, joint refinement, clearer acceptance criteria
Specialist or single-person skillsInternalPairing, a skills matrix, cross-training, swarming on specialist work
Defects and reworkInternalStronger Definition of Done, earlier testing, automated checks
Missing access or toolsExternalOnboarding checklist, standard access bundles, a named contact in IT
Supplier or vendor delaysExternalClear contractual turnaround times, earlier requests, escalation route
Customer information missingExternalIntake form that captures what you need before work starts

If you are not sure where the delays sit in your end-to-end process, a value stream map will show the hand-offs where blockers tend to gather.

Blocker clustering in Scrum

Scrum teams usually say impediment rather than blocker, but the technique works on any board where blocked items are visible.

  • Raise and mark impediments during the Daily Scrum, recording the reason and start date.
  • Keep resolved impediments in a log instead of deleting them.
  • Every few sprints, use part of the Sprint Retrospective to cluster the log and pick one improvement for the next sprint.
  • The Scrum Master can take data on external impediments to the people who can remove them; the Scrum Guide lists causing the removal of impediments among the Scrum Master's services to the team.

Tools for blocker clustering

You do not need special software. A wall of red sticky notes is how the technique started and remains the easiest way to engage a team.

  • Spreadsheet. A simple table with the fields listed earlier, plus a pivot table summarising blocked days by cluster, is enough for most teams.
  • Jira and similar developer tools. You can flag an issue as blocked on a board and add a reason in a comment or custom field. Export the data for clustering. See our Jira review.
  • Kanban analytics tools. Some Kanban platforms provide a dedicated chart; Businessmap, for example, offers a blocker clustering chart that groups blocked time by blocker type.
  • Work management platforms. Tools such as Wrike, which we rank first overall, let you add custom statuses and fields for blocked reasons and report on them across projects. That is useful when blockers span several teams.

Check that your tool keeps the history of blocked periods after the flag is removed; if not, add date fields. Our project management tools rankings compare the main options.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Throwing away resolved blockers. The whole technique depends on keeping the records.
  • Recording queues as blockers. Waiting for the next stage to pull an item is queue time. Mixing the two hides both problems.
  • Ranking by count, not by days. A frequent, short blocker may matter less than a rare, long one.
  • Blaming people. If marking a blocker feels like admitting failure, people stop marking them. Keep it blame-free and focus on the system.
  • Clustering too early. A week of data rarely shows a reliable pattern. Collect for at least several weeks.
  • Trying to fix every cluster. Pick one or two, run an experiment, and come back. Some blockers are not worth solving.
  • Ignoring the constraint. Fixing a cluster at a stage with spare capacity can feel productive but change nothing for the customer.
  • Stopping after one round. Systems change. New clusters appear as old ones shrink, so make blocker review a regular cadence, monthly for most teams.

Frequently asked questions

What is blocker clustering?

Blocker clustering is a Kanban technique for recording every blocked work item with its reason and duration, grouping the records by similar cause and ranking the groups by total blocked days. The team then fixes the clusters that cost the most delivery time.

Who created blocker clustering?

The technique was developed by Klaus Leopold and popularised with Troy Magennis. They presented it at Lean Kanban Central Europe in 2014 and described it in a 2015 InfoQ article on blockers and defects.

What is the difference between blocked and waiting work?

Blocked work is an item someone wants to work on now but cannot, because of something outside their control. Waiting work is finished at one stage and sitting in a queue until the next stage pulls it. Record blockers; show queues with waiting columns.

How long should we collect blocker data before clustering?

Four to six weeks is a common first period. Teams that finish only a few items a week may need longer to gather enough records. Keep collecting afterwards so you can measure the effect of your improvements.

Who should attend a blocker clustering session?

The whole team, plus a representative of any team or function that appears often in the external clusters. Their presence turns the session into joint problem-solving rather than a list of complaints.

How do you measure the cost of a blocker?

The standard measure is blocked days: the number of days between the item becoming blocked and being unblocked. Add them up per cluster. You can translate days into money or missed dates for a business case, but days are usually persuasive enough.

What should we do about external blockers we cannot control?

Take the data to the people who own the cause. A table showing that waiting for one team cost 40 days last quarter supports a request for an agreed turnaround time, earlier involvement or a change in team boundaries.

Can Scrum teams use blocker clustering?

Yes. Scrum teams usually call blockers impediments. Record them during the Daily Scrum, keep a log, and cluster it during a Sprint Retrospective every few sprints.

How do you do blocker clustering in Jira or another digital tool?

Flag blocked issues and record the reason in a comment or custom field, then export the issues with their blocked dates. Cluster the reasons in a spreadsheet or on a virtual whiteboard. Some Kanban analytics tools provide a ready-made blocker clustering chart.

How does blocker clustering relate to a Kanban risk review?

In the Kanban Method, blocker clustering is a key input to the Risk Review cadence. Each cluster gives a probability of an item being blocked and an average delay, which helps the team plan and protect its service.

Are defects blockers?

They can be. Leopold and Magennis recommend treating defects as blockers because they tie up people and stop items progressing. Many teams record them as a separate cluster so rework is visible alongside other delays.

Is blocker clustering the same as root cause analysis?

No. Blocker clustering identifies and ranks the problems. Root cause analysis, using 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram, then explains why the top clusters happen so you can design a fix.
