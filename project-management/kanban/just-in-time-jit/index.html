Just-in-Time (JIT): How It Works, Examples, Pros and Cons

Just-in-Time (JIT) is a way of running operations so that each process makes or receives only what is needed, when it is needed and in the amount needed. Toyota uses those words to describe it, and it is one of the two pillars of the Toyota Production System. This guide explains how JIT works, works through takt time and delivery-cost examples, sets out the benefits and risks, and shows how JIT thinking applies to projects.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 16 September 2026
  • Last updated 25 October 2026
  • 21 min read

Key takeaways

  • JIT means making or delivering only what the next step needs, when it needs it and in the amount it needs, so production is triggered by real demand rather than forecasts.
  • It rests on pull, takt time, continuous flow and level scheduling, held together by kanban signals, small batches, quick changeovers and built-in quality.
  • Ordering more often without making each delivery cheaper usually costs more. JIT works as a whole system, not as a purchasing policy.
  • The main risk is fragility: with little buffer, a supplier failure or demand spike stops the line. Most firms now keep targeted buffers for critical, hard-to-source items.
  • Start with stability (quality, reliable equipment and suppliers, levelled demand), then reduce inventory step by step.
  • In projects, JIT becomes just-in-time planning, backlog refinement just before work starts, WIP limits and material deliveries timed to the programme.

What is Just-in-Time (JIT)?

Just-in-Time (JIT) is an operations approach in which materials, parts and products are made or delivered only when the next process or the customer needs them, in exactly the quantity needed. Production is pulled by actual demand rather than pushed by forecasts. This keeps inventory low, exposes problems quickly and shortens the time from order to delivery.

The term covers three closely related ideas, and it helps to keep them apart:

  • JIT production (or manufacturing): each process makes only what the downstream process has used. This is the original Toyota meaning.
  • JIT inventory: a stock policy that keeps raw materials, work in progress and finished goods to a minimum, replenished as they are consumed.
  • JIT delivery: suppliers deliver small quantities frequently, sometimes in the exact sequence the line will use them.

Two misunderstandings are common. JIT does not mean zero inventory: a JIT plant holds small, deliberate stocks (in supermarkets, kanban containers and buffers) sized to the time it takes to replenish them. And JIT is not simply ordering more often. Without the supporting practices described below, more frequent ordering usually raises costs, as the worked example later in this guide shows.

Where JIT came from

JIT grew out of Toyota's search for a way to build cars efficiently in small volumes in post-war Japan, when the company could not afford the large inventories of American mass production. Toyota's own account credits these people:

Who or whatContributionWhen
Sakichi Toyoda (1867–1930)Invented automatic looms that stopped when a thread broke, the origin of jidoka ("automation with a human touch")Weft-breakage stopping device 1896; Type-G automatic loom 1924
Kiichiro Toyoda (1894–1952)Advocated Just-in-Time and introduced flow production with a chain conveyor; the approach was applied to body assembly at the Koromo (now Honsha) PlantPlant completed 1938
Taiichi OhnoTurned JIT and jidoka into a working production system through years of trial and error, later documented as the Toyota Production SystemLate 1940s onwards
The "supermarket method"Inspired by how supermarkets restock only what customers take; it evolved into the kanban system once a production instruction was addedIntroduced 1954
Roll-outWhole Honsha Plant in the late 1950s, all Toyota plants from 1960, Toyota Group suppliers from the late 1960s1950s–1980s

Western manufacturers studied the approach from the 1970s and 1980s, and researchers later used the term Lean to describe it. Today JIT is treated as one part of Lean manufacturing, alongside jidoka, standard work and kaizen.

Tip

Toyota describes the Toyota Production System as resting on two pillars: Just-in-Time and jidoka. JIT without jidoka (stopping to fix problems at source) tends to fail, because low stock leaves no room for defects.

The building blocks of JIT

The Lean Enterprise Institute describes JIT as built on heijunka (level scheduling), with three operating elements: the pull system, takt time and continuous flow. In practice several supporting practices make those elements possible.

ElementWhat it meansPractical example
PullA process makes or moves something only when the next process has used itAn empty container returning to the paint shop authorises it to paint 20 more panels
Takt timeThe pace of customer demand: available time divided by units demandedA takt of 60 seconds means one finished unit should leave the line every minute
Continuous flowItems move one (or a few) at a time between steps without waiting in pilesMachining, washing and inspection placed side by side in a cell
Heijunka (levelling)Smooth the mix and volume of production over time instead of building in large runsBuild A-B-A-C-A-B each hour rather than all As on Monday
KanbanCards, bins or electronic signals that carry the pull instructionA two-bin system for fasteners at each workstation
Small lots and quick changeoversReduce set-up times so small batches are economicalCutting a die change from 60 minutes to 9 minutes
Jidoka and quality at sourceStop and fix defects immediately so bad parts do not flow onAn operator pulls an andon cord when a fault appears
Supplier partnershipFew, reliable, often nearby suppliers delivering small quantities frequentlyDaily deliveries timed to shift patterns
Standard work and kaizenA documented best way to do each job, improved continuouslyWeekly improvement on the biggest source of waiting

Takt time, with a worked example

Takt time sets the heartbeat of a JIT system. The formula is: takt time = available working time ÷ customer demand in the same period.

  1. Work out available time. A plant runs two 8-hour shifts (480 minutes each). Each shift has a 30-minute meal break and two 10-minute breaks, leaving 430 minutes. Two shifts give 860 minutes, or 51,600 seconds, a day.
  2. Work out demand. Customers order an average of 860 units a day.
  3. Divide: 51,600 ÷ 860 = 60 seconds. The line should complete one unit every minute, no faster and no slower.
  4. Balance the work. If the total work content per unit is 6 minutes, you need about 6 operators (6 minutes ÷ 1-minute takt), each with roughly 60 seconds of work.
  5. Recalculate when demand changes. If demand falls to 645 units a day, takt rises to 80 seconds (51,600 ÷ 645) and you rebalance the line with fewer people, instead of building stock nobody has ordered.

Producing faster than takt creates overproduction, which Ohno counted as the worst of the seven wastes because it hides all the others. Producing slower than takt means you will miss demand.

Pull and kanban signals

Pull is what makes JIT self-regulating. A downstream step uses parts, and the empty container or card goes back upstream as an instruction to replace exactly what was used. No card, no production. Our guides to pull vs push systems and kanban inventory management explain the mechanics and show how to calculate the number of kanbans.

Small batches and quick changeovers

Large batches are economical only when changing over is slow. If a press takes an hour to change dies, you run thousands of parts between changes and store them. Reduce the changeover to a few minutes (the approach known as SMED, single-minute exchange of die) and small batches become affordable. Inventory then falls without any loss of capacity. This is why JIT programmes usually start on the shop floor, not in the purchasing department.

How a JIT system works, step by step

Here is one customer order travelling through a simple JIT system, from the order back to the supplier:

  1. A customer order arrives and joins a levelled production schedule (heijunka). Only the final assembly line receives a schedule; no other process is scheduled centrally.
  2. Final assembly builds at takt, taking parts from small containers at each station.
  3. A container empties. Its withdrawal kanban goes to the parts supermarket, the small controlled store that feeds the line.
  4. A material handler collects a full container from the supermarket on a fixed route and delivers it to the line. The production kanban from that container is placed in the producing cell's queue.
  5. The upstream cell makes exactly one container's worth, in the order the kanbans arrived, and puts it back in the supermarket.
  6. That cell's own parts are consumed, sending kanbans further upstream, and eventually supplier kanbans go to external suppliers.
  7. Suppliers deliver small quantities frequently, often on a milk run (one truck collecting from several suppliers on a fixed loop).
  8. Problems stop the flow visibly. A defect, a breakdown or a missing part shows up within minutes, because there is little stock to cover it, and the team fixes the root cause.

The key point is that information flows upstream in small, frequent signals, and material flows downstream in small, frequent movements. A value stream map is the standard way to draw both flows and see where JIT is breaking down.

Worked example: the true cost of more frequent deliveries

Many organisations try to "go JIT" by asking suppliers to deliver more often. The numbers show why that alone rarely works. The figures below are illustrative.

A part costs £5. Annual usage is 48,000 units (4,000 a month). Holding inventory costs 25% of its value a year (capital, space, insurance, handling, obsolescence). Each delivery costs £60 to arrange, ship and receive. Average stock is roughly half the delivery quantity.

ScenarioDelivery sizeAverage stock (value)Annual holding costDeliveries a year × costTotal annual cost
A. Monthly deliveries4,0002,000 units (£10,000)£2,50012 × £60 = £720£3,220
B. Weekly deliveries, same delivery costabout 923about 462 units (£2,308)£57752 × £60 = £3,120£3,697
C. Weekly milk run shared with other suppliersabout 923about 462 units (£2,308)£57752 × £20 = £1,040£1,617
  • Scenario B is more expensive than A. Inventory fell by 77%, but delivery costs more than quadrupled. This is the classic economic order quantity trade-off.
  • Scenario C cuts the total by about half because the cost of each delivery fell. That is what JIT practices do: milk runs, standard containers, simple call-offs, supplier agreements and quicker receiving.
  • The hidden benefits are not in the table. Less stock also frees floor space, reduces damage and obsolescence, and exposes quality problems sooner. These are real but harder to price.
Tip

Before asking for more frequent deliveries, cut the cost of each one. Combine suppliers into milk runs, standardise containers and simplify the call-off. The same logic applies inside the plant: reduce set-up time before you reduce batch size.

Benefits of Just-in-Time

BenefitWhy it happens
Lower inventory and carrying costsLess raw material, work in progress and finished stock tied up in cash, space and insurance
Less waste and obsolescenceFewer parts expire, get damaged or become redundant after a design change
Problems surface quicklyWith little buffer, defects and breakdowns stop flow and must be fixed at the root
Shorter lead timesSmall batches move through the process faster; see cycle time vs lead time
Better responsivenessThe schedule follows real demand, so product mix changes are absorbed faster
Higher qualitySmall lots mean fewer bad parts made before a problem is spotted
More usable floor spaceSpace once used for storage can host new lines or cells
Stronger supplier relationshipsLong-term partnerships replace transactional, price-only buying

Lean practitioners often describe inventory as water in a river and problems as rocks. A high water level hides the rocks. Lowering the water (inventory) reveals them so they can be removed. This is why JIT is an improvement system as much as an inventory policy: each reduction in stock exposes the next problem to solve.

Disadvantages and risks of JIT

The same low buffers that expose problems also leave the system with little protection when something goes wrong.

RiskWhat happensHow to reduce it
Supplier failureA single late or failed delivery can stop production within hoursDual sourcing for critical parts, supplier development, strategic buffers
Demand spikesStock-outs and missed orders when demand jumps beyond buffer capacityLevelled scheduling, finished-goods buffer for volatile lines, capacity flex
Transport disruptionWeather, strikes, port congestion or border delays break frequent delivery schedulesNearer suppliers, alternative routes, buffer sized to the disruption you choose to cover
Quality failuresWith no spare stock, a defective batch halts the lineJidoka, supplier quality audits, poka-yoke (error-proofing)
Higher transaction costsMore orders, deliveries and handlingMilk runs, e-kanban, standard containers (see the worked example)
Supplier dependence and powerFew suppliers means less leverage and more exposureLong-term contracts, shared improvement, visibility of sub-tier suppliers
Implementation effortTakes years of process improvement and culture changePilot one value stream, train teams, measure and expand

What real disruptions have shown

  • The 1997 Aisin fire. A fire at Aisin Seiki, which made about 99% of Toyota's brake proportioning valves, threatened weeks of lost output. Published accounts describe suppliers improvising production of the valves, with Toyota losing about five days. It is often cited both as JIT's fragility and as the strength of its supplier network.
  • Semiconductors after 2011. Reuters reported in 2021 that after the 2011 earthquake Toyota adopted a business continuity plan requiring suppliers to hold two to six months of chip stock, depending on lead time. A Toyota spokesperson called it "a classic lean solution". The lesson is that JIT allows deliberate buffers where the risk justifies them.

For project and programme teams, these risks belong in the risk register. Our guides to project risk management and contingency plans show how to record and plan for them.

JIT vs just-in-case (and finding the balance)

Just-in-case (JIC) holds extra inventory to protect against uncertainty. JIT holds the minimum and relies on fast, reliable replenishment. Few organisations use either in pure form.

Just-in-TimeJust-in-case
Inventory levelMinimal, sized to replenishment timeHigh, sized to worst-case scenarios
Triggered byActual consumption (pull)Forecasts and safety targets (push)
Cost focusCarrying cost, waste, lead timeCost of stock-outs and lost sales
StrengthEfficiency, quality, cashResilience against disruption
WeaknessFragile when supply failsTies up cash and space, hides problems
Best forStable demand, reliable nearby supply, short lead timesVolatile demand, long or risky supply, critical items

A simple matrix to set your buffer policy

Classify each item by how likely its supply is to be disrupted and how serious a stock-out would be. Then apply a different policy to each group. This is our recommended working approach, not a formal standard.

Low impact of a stock-outHigh impact of a stock-out
Low supply riskPure JIT: kanban or two-bin, minimal safety stockJIT with a modest safety stock; monitor supplier performance
High supply riskJIT with a second source or quick substituteStrategic buffer (weeks or months), dual sourcing, visibility of sub-tier suppliers

Review the matrix at least yearly and after any major disruption. Items move between quadrants as suppliers, markets and designs change.

JIT vs Lean, Kanban, MRP and EOQ

JIT is often confused with the tools and approaches around it. This table sets out how they relate.

ConceptWhat it isRelationship to JIT
Lean manufacturingA whole management philosophy focused on value and removing wasteJIT is one of Lean's core pillars, with jidoka
KanbanThe signalling system (cards, bins or electronic signals)Kanban is how JIT is controlled day to day; JIT is the goal
MRP (material requirements planning)Calculates material needs from a master schedule and forecasts, then pushes ordersPush-based; many firms use MRP for long-term planning and kanban (JIT) for execution
EOQ (economic order quantity)A formula that balances ordering cost against holding costJIT attacks the ordering and set-up cost so the economic quantity becomes small
Theory of ConstraintsFocuses improvement on the bottleneck; uses drum-buffer-ropeComplementary: TOC places buffers deliberately in front of the constraint
Six SigmaStatistical reduction of variation and defectsLower variation makes JIT more reliable

Real-world examples of JIT

  • Toyota car assembly. The reference case: a levelled final assembly schedule, kanban-controlled supermarkets, frequent supplier deliveries and jidoka on every line, as documented in Toyota's own history.
  • Sequenced supply in vehicle assembly. Large, variant-specific parts such as seats are commonly delivered in the exact build sequence, shortly before they are fitted, so the plant holds hours rather than weeks of them.
  • Made-to-order food. Fast-food kitchens that assemble items when ordered, keeping only a small, timed buffer of components, are a widely used everyday illustration of pull.
  • Hospital consumables. Many hospitals restock wards with two-bin systems: when the front bin empties, it triggers replenishment while the back bin covers demand.
  • Construction sites. Contractors on congested city sites often schedule deliveries of steel, precast units or facade panels for specific days or hours because there is no space to store them.
  • Retail replenishment. Point-of-sale data triggers store replenishment from distribution centres, so shelves are refilled to match what was sold.

Notice that every example combines short, reliable replenishment with a deliberately small buffer. None of them runs at literally zero stock.

Is your organisation ready for JIT?

Cutting stock before the process is stable is the fastest way to discredit JIT. Score yourself honestly against these conditions before you start reducing inventory.

ConditionReadyNot ready yet
DemandSteady or can be levelled; mix is predictableLarge unpredictable spikes, highly seasonal, mostly one-off orders
QualityLow, stable defect rates; problems stopped at sourceFrequent rework; defects found late
EquipmentReliable, with planned maintenanceFrequent unplanned breakdowns
ChangeoversMinutes, or reducingHours, with no improvement plan
SuppliersReliable, nearby or with dependable logistics; willing to partnerLong lead times, poor on-time delivery, adversarial relationships
PeopleTrained, empowered to stop the line and suggest improvementsBlamed for stopping; no time for improvement
InformationAccurate stock records and simple signalsRecords unreliable; replenishment done by guesswork
Tip

If you have three or more "not ready yet" answers, start with stability work (quality, maintenance, set-up reduction and supplier performance) and treat inventory reduction as the result, not the starting point.

How to implement JIT in 8 steps

  1. Pick one value stream. Choose a product family with steady demand and a willing team. Map its current state with a value stream map, recording inventory, lead time and process time.
  2. Calculate takt time for that family and compare it with the cycle time of each step to find imbalances and the bottleneck.
  3. Stabilise the process. Fix the main quality problems, introduce standard work and planned maintenance, and make problems visible with visual management.
  4. Reduce changeover times on the equipment that forces large batches, so smaller lots become economical.
  5. Create flow by moving steps together into cells where possible and removing waiting between them.
  6. Introduce pull where flow is not possible: set up supermarkets and kanban loops, sized with the kanban formula, and level the final schedule.
  7. Bring suppliers in. Agree delivery frequency, container sizes, quality standards and call-off signals; consider milk runs. Our guide to project procurement management covers supplier agreements.
  8. Lower the water level gradually. Remove kanbans or reduce buffers one step at a time, fix each problem that appears, measure lead time, inventory turns and on-time delivery, then extend to the next value stream.

Treat the roll-out itself as a project, with a sponsor, milestones and a risk log. Our guide to manufacturing project management covers running improvement projects on a live production floor.

Just-in-Time thinking in project management

JIT was designed for repetitive production, but its central idea, doing work only when it is needed and in small amounts, transfers well to projects. This is part of Lean project management.

Just-in-time planning and backlog refinement

Detailed plans made months ahead become inventory: they age, need rework when circumstances change and hide the fact that nobody knows the answer yet. Agile teams plan at several levels, and detail is added only as work approaches.

  • Keep the long-range plan at the level of themes and milestones; see Agile release planning.
  • Refine backlog items just before they are needed, typically one or two iterations ahead; see backlog refinement.
  • Make irreversible decisions at the last responsible moment: the latest point at which you can still decide without losing options or causing delay.
  • Keep a short, ordered queue of ready work rather than a large pile of detailed specifications. A product backlog template helps keep it tidy.

JIT deliveries on construction and engineering projects

On constrained sites, materials are often called off to match the programme. The example below is illustrative and shows how a planner might set delivery windows and buffers for one floor of a building.

MaterialNeeded on siteSupplier lead timeDelivery windowBuffer held
Precast stair unitsWeek 12, day 26 weeksWeek 12, day 1, 07:00–09:00 (crane booked)None on site; next floor's units ready at the factory
PlasterboardWeeks 14–161 weekWeekly, Monday morningAbout 2 days' usage on the floor
Facade panelsWeeks 15–1810 weeksDaily, sequenced by elevation1 day's panels; factory holds 1 week
Fixings and consumablesThroughout2 daysTwo-bin replenishment from site storeSecond bin
  • Long-lead items still need early procurement: JIT is about the timing of *delivery*, not the timing of *ordering*.
  • Book logistics resources (cranes, hoists, loading bays) with the delivery, or the material will wait on the lorry.
  • Keep buffers where the risk is high, as the matrix above suggests: one day of facade panels protects the critical path from a single late lorry.
  • Our guide to construction project management covers logistics planning in more depth.

Pull and WIP limits in knowledge work

Kanban brought JIT's pull idea to knowledge work. Teams limit work in progress, start new items only when capacity frees up and measure how long work takes to flow through. WIP limits play the same role as a fixed number of kanban cards, and flow efficiency shows how much of an item's lead time is spent waiting. A Kanban board template is an easy way to start.

Common JIT mistakes

  • Cutting inventory first. Removing stock before fixing quality, breakdowns and set-ups simply causes stock-outs, and JIT gets the blame.
  • Pushing inventory onto suppliers. If suppliers simply hold the stock you no longer hold, total cost in the supply chain does not fall, and the price you pay eventually rises.
  • Ignoring transaction costs. More deliveries without cheaper deliveries raise total cost (see the worked example).
  • Treating JIT as a software feature. A system can generate signals, but flow, levelling and problem-solving happen on the floor.
  • One policy for every item. Critical, long-lead or single-source items need different buffers from cheap, local, fast-moving ones.
  • No levelling. Kanban loops sized for average demand break down when the final schedule swings wildly from day to day.
  • Not stopping to fix problems. Without jidoka, teams work around problems, and the low buffers make every workaround more painful.
  • Copying Toyota's tools without its culture. Kanban cards and takt boards do little without managers who support improvement and people who are trusted to stop the line.

JIT is one half of a larger system. To see how it fits with jidoka, standard work and kaizen, read Lean manufacturing.

Frequently asked questions

What is Just-in-Time in simple terms?

Just-in-Time means making or buying only what you need, when you need it and in the amount you need. Instead of building stock to a forecast, each step replaces only what the next step has used.

Who invented Just-in-Time?

JIT was developed at Toyota. Kiichiro Toyoda advocated the idea in the 1930s, and Taiichi Ohno built it into the Toyota Production System from the late 1940s onwards.

What are the main elements of JIT?

The Lean Enterprise Institute names three operating elements: the pull system, takt time and continuous flow, all built on level scheduling (heijunka). Kanban, small lots, quick changeovers, built-in quality and reliable suppliers make them work.

What is the difference between JIT and Kanban?

JIT is the goal: produce only what is needed when it is needed. Kanban is the signalling method, using cards, bins or electronic messages, that tells each process what to make or move. See kanban inventory management.

Is JIT the same as Lean?

No. Lean is the broader philosophy of maximising value and removing waste. JIT is one of its two core pillars, alongside jidoka (building quality in).

What is the difference between JIT and just-in-case?

Just-in-case holds extra stock to protect against uncertainty. JIT holds the minimum and relies on fast, reliable replenishment. Most organisations blend the two, using JIT for most items and buffers for critical ones.

What is the difference between JIT inventory and JIT manufacturing?

JIT manufacturing is the production approach in which each process makes only what the next has used. JIT inventory is the resulting stock policy: minimal raw material, work in progress and finished goods, replenished as consumed.

What are the biggest disadvantages of JIT?

Vulnerability to supplier failure, transport disruption and demand spikes, higher ordering and transport costs if deliveries are not made cheaper, dependence on a few suppliers, and the long effort needed to implement it properly.

Which businesses should not use JIT?

Pure JIT suits organisations poorly when demand is highly unpredictable, supply lead times are long or unreliable, items are critical and single-sourced, or quality is unstable. Such businesses can still apply JIT to the items where conditions are right.

Is JIT still used after recent supply chain disruptions?

Yes. Many companies have kept JIT for most items and added strategic buffers for critical or hard-to-source parts. Toyota itself, according to Reuters, asked suppliers to hold several months of chip stock after the 2011 earthquake.

Does JIT mean zero inventory?

No. JIT keeps small, deliberate stocks sized to the replenishment time, such as kanban containers and supermarkets. The aim is the minimum inventory needed for smooth flow, not none at all.

How do you calculate takt time?

Divide available working time by customer demand for the same period. For example, 51,600 seconds of working time a day ÷ 860 units demanded = a takt time of 60 seconds per unit.

Is Just-in-Time a pull system?

Yes. Production and delivery are triggered by actual downstream consumption rather than by a forecast. See pull vs push systems.

How does JIT apply to project management?

Project teams apply JIT by planning in detail only just before work starts, refining backlog items one or two iterations ahead, deciding at the last responsible moment, limiting work in progress and timing material deliveries to the programme.
