WIP Limits in Kanban: How to Set Them, With Examples

WIP limits cap how many work items can sit in a stage of your workflow, or in the whole workflow, at the same time, so the team finishes work before starting more. They are the practice that turns a Kanban board from a picture of work into a pull system, and they are the most direct way to shorten delivery times and expose bottlenecks. This guide explains why WIP limits work, how to set your first numbers step by step, and how to tune them, using worked examples throughout.

Key takeaways

  • A WIP limit is an agreed maximum number of items in a column, swimlane, person's queue or whole workflow. When it is reached, the team finishes or unblocks work instead of starting new items.
  • Little's Law explains why it works: average cycle time equals average WIP divided by average throughput, so less WIP at the same throughput means faster delivery.
  • Start a little below today's actual WIP, or around one to one and a half items per person in each active stage, and make the numbers visible on the board.
  • When a column is full, look right to left: help finish, review or unblock downstream work before pulling anything new.
  • Treat limits as experiments. Review them every few weeks using cycle time, throughput and blocked-item data, and change one limit at a time.

What is a WIP limit?

A WIP limit is the maximum number of work items allowed in a defined part of a workflow at one time, such as a board column, a swimlane or the whole system. Once the limit is reached, nobody pulls new work into that part until an item moves on, so the team finishes work before it starts more.

WIP stands for work in progress. The Kanban Guide (edition 2025.5, by John Coleman and Daniel Vacanti) defines it as the number of work items started but not finished, and requires Kanban teams to explicitly control it between their agreed start and finish points. Kanban University's glossary describes a WIP limit as a policy that constrains the amount of work allowed in a given part of the system. In other words, a limit is a team agreement that only works if everyone treats it as binding.

On a board, the limit usually appears in the column header, for example Test (3). A gap below the limit is a pull signal: someone in that stage has capacity and may take the next item from the column to the left. That is what separates a Kanban board from a shared to-do list: work is pulled in when there is room rather than pushed in whenever someone asks. Our guide to pull vs push systems explains the wider idea.

Limiting WIP is one of the core practices of the Kanban Method, alongside visualising work and managing flow. See what is Kanban for how the practices fit together.

Why WIP limits work

Limiting work feels counter-intuitive, but it speeds delivery for three reasons: queueing, task switching and hidden bottlenecks.

Little's Law

Little's Law is a result from queueing theory, proved by John D. C. Little in the journal Operations Research in 1961. Applied to a team's workflow it reads:

Average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput

Here cycle time is how long an item takes from start to finish, and throughput is how many items the team finishes per week. The formula shows that, if throughput stays roughly the same, the only way to make each item faster is to have fewer items in progress.

Illustrative example. A team has 24 items in progress and finishes about 6 items a week. Average cycle time is 24 ÷ 6 = 4 weeks. If the team lowers WIP to 12 and keeps finishing 6 a week, average cycle time falls to 12 ÷ 6 = 2 weeks. Nobody worked harder; the team simply stopped letting work queue.

Little's Law describes averages over a period and holds when the system is reasonably stable. Keep these conditions in mind before relying on it:

ConditionWhat it means for your board
Consistent unitsMeasure WIP in items, throughput in items per week and cycle time in weeks (or all in days).
Stable systemRoughly as many items finish as start over the period; WIP is not steadily climbing.
Items finishItems that are abandoned or left in progress indefinitely distort the averages; close or remove them explicitly.
Same boundariesCount WIP, cycle time and throughput between the same start and finish points on the board.

Less task switching

Every extra item a person holds adds context switching. Even ignoring that overhead, parallel work delays everything.

Illustrative example. You have three jobs, A, B and C, each needing five days of focused work. Working on them one after another, A is done on day 5, B on day 10 and C on day 15, an average of 10 days. Rotating a day at a time between them, A is done on day 13, B on day 14 and C on day 15, an average of 14 days, before you add any switching cost. Same effort, same finish date for the last job, but every customer waits longer.

ApproachA finishesB finishesC finishesAverage wait
One at a time (WIP 1)Day 5Day 10Day 1510 days
All three in parallel (WIP 3)Day 13Day 14Day 1514 days

Bottlenecks become visible

When every stage has a limit, a slow stage fills up and the stages before it stop being able to push work into it. Instead of a growing pile of half-finished items, you see a full column and idle hands upstream. That is uncomfortable, and useful: it shows exactly where to improve. The theory of constraints gives you a method for what to do next.

Other benefits

  • Quality: people finishing one item at a time make fewer mistakes and get feedback sooner, so defects are found while the work is still fresh.
  • Predictability: stable WIP gives a stable cycle time, which makes forecasting much easier; see Kanban planning.
  • Sustainable pace: limits give people a legitimate reason to say "not yet".
  • Software delivery performance: the DORA research programme lists WIP limits among the capabilities that help drive improvements in software delivery performance, particularly when combined with visual displays and feedback from monitoring.
Tip

"Stop starting, start finishing" is the Kanban slogan for this. A WIP limit turns the slogan into a rule the whole team can see.

Types of WIP limits

You can limit WIP at several levels. Most teams start with column limits and add others only to solve a specific problem.

TypeExampleWhen to use it
Per column (stage)Develop: max 6The default. Controls flow through each step and shows where work piles up.
Per column groupDevelop + Review combined: max 8When two stages are done by the same people and you want flexibility between them.
Per personMax 2 items eachWhen individuals are overloaded or juggling requests from several managers.
Per swimlane or class of serviceExpedite lane: max 1To protect capacity for urgent work and stop everything becoming urgent; see Kanban swimlanes.
Per work typeBugs: max 3 in progressTo keep a balanced mix of features, defects and maintenance.
Whole system (CONWIP)Max 15 items between Ready and DoneA simple first step for small teams, or for workflows where stages change often.
Upstream (Ready) limitReady: max 8To stop the Ready column becoming a second backlog and to make replenishment meaningful.

CONWIP stands for constant work in progress. Instead of limiting each stage, you limit the total number of items in the system: a new item can start only when one finishes. It is easy to introduce, but it will not show you which stage is the bottleneck, so most teams move to column limits once they are comfortable.

The Done column has no limit. It is the end of the flow, and limiting it would only stop work from finishing. Waiting states inside the flow, such as Ready for test or Develop: done, do need to be counted, because that is where most delay usually hides.

How to set WIP limits step by step

There is no formula that gives the perfect number. The aim is a sensible starting point that is low enough to change behaviour, then adjustment based on data. Follow these steps.

  1. Agree your start and finish points. Decide where work counts as started (often when it leaves Ready) and finished (often Done or Released). WIP is everything in between.
  2. Count today's WIP. For each column, count the items currently in it, including blocked and waiting items. If you can, take the average over the last two to four weeks rather than a single day.
  3. Note who works in each stage. Write down how many people actually do the work in each column. A column staffed by one specialist needs a much lower limit than one staffed by four people.
  4. Choose starting limits. A common starting point is a little below today's average WIP, or roughly one to one and a half items per person in each active stage. Avoid halving WIP overnight; a cut of 20 to 30% is usually enough to change behaviour without stalling the team.
  5. Agree the policies. Decide in advance whether blocked items count (we recommend yes), how expedite items are handled, and what happens if someone wants to break a limit.
  6. Make it visible. Put each limit in the column header, highlight columns that are at or over their limit, and write the policies on or beside the board.
  7. Run for two to four weeks, then review. Track cycle time, throughput, blocked items and every limit breach. Adjust one or two limits at your next retrospective or operations review.
Tip

Set limits as a team. Limits imposed by a manager tend to be quietly ignored; limits the team chose are defended by the team.

Worked example: setting limits on a team board

This example is illustrative. A six-person product team (one analyst, four developers and one tester) uses a board with the columns Ready, Analysis, Develop (split into Doing and Done), Test, Release and Done. They finish about 7 items a week. Today the board looks like this:

ColumnPeopleItems todayStarting limitReasoning
Readyn/a158About one week of throughput plus a small buffer; refilled at weekly replenishment
Analysis142One analyst; two items allows one to wait on a stakeholder reply
Develop: Doing411Shared limit of 8About 1.5 per developer, shared with Develop: Done
Develop: Donen/a6(within the 8)A waiting state; counting it stops finished code piling up untested
Test153One tester; three allows a small buffer so testing never runs dry
ReleaseShared23Releases go out twice a week, so a few items can wait
Donen/an/aNo limitEnd of the flow

Between Analysis and Release the team currently has 4 + 11 + 6 + 5 + 2 = 28 items in progress. By Little's Law, 28 ÷ 7 gives an average cycle time of about 4 weeks. The new limits allow at most 2 + 8 + 3 + 3 = 16 items. If throughput holds at 7 a week, average cycle time should fall towards 16 ÷ 7, or a little over 2 weeks.

The team does not throw away the extra work. They stop starting new items until each column drops below its limit, and move back to Ready anything nobody is actively working on.

Why Doing and Done sub-columns share a limit

Splitting Develop into Doing and Done shows which finished items are waiting for the next stage. With separate limits, developers could keep finishing work into Done while Test is overwhelmed. A shared limit means that when Develop: Done fills up, developers must help clear it, usually by helping the tester, before starting anything new.

What happened over four weeks

The team tracked a few numbers each week (illustrative figures):

WeekAverage WIPThroughput (items)WIP ÷ throughput (weeks)Observation
Before2874.0Long queues in Develop: Done and Test
12464.0Throughput dipped while developers learned to test
22072.9Test at its limit every day; developers paired with the tester
31772.4Two blockers waiting on another team escalated at the daily meeting
41682.0Automated regression checks freed tester time

Three lessons came out of the review. Test was the bottleneck, which nobody had seen while work sat in Develop: Done. Throughput dipped in the first week before recovering, which is common and worth warning stakeholders about. And the Analysis limit was never reached, so the team left it alone rather than changing it without evidence.

To see this kind of change over time, use a cumulative flow diagram: bands that stop widening are the visible sign that WIP limits are working.

What to do when a column hits its limit

A full column is not a failure; it tells you where to put your effort. Work from the right of the board to the left:

  1. Do not pull new work into the full column. Leave the gap for an item to move on first.
  2. Look downstream. Is there anything in the next stage you can help finish, review or release? Moving work out of later columns creates space all the way back.
  3. Unblock before you start. Check every blocked item in or after the full column and ask who can remove the blocker today.
  4. Swarm or pair. Join a colleague on the oldest item in the full column. Our guide to agile swarming explains how.
  5. Use spare time to improve the system. If you truly cannot help, work on automation, documentation, test data or fixing a recurring cause of blocked work.
  6. Raise it at the daily meeting. If the same column is full day after day, discuss it at the next review rather than quietly raising the limit.

Blocked items

Count blocked items towards the limit. If you do not, a column can fill with blocked work while new items keep arriving, and the limit stops doing its job. Counting them creates healthy pressure to resolve blockers. Mark blocked cards clearly, note the reason and date, and review patterns regularly; blocker clustering is a simple way to find the causes that recur.

Urgent work and breaching a limit

Rather than breaking limits ad hoc, give urgent work an expedite lane with a limit of one and agree who may use it. If a limit must be breached, make it visible (many teams turn the column header red), record the reason and discuss it at the next review. Frequent breaches mean the limit or the process needs to change.

Tuning WIP limits over time

Your first limits are a hypothesis. Review them every two to four weeks using board data rather than how busy people feel, changing one or two at a time.

What you seeWhat it probably meansWhat to try
A column never reaches its limitThe limit is too high to influence behaviourLower it by one and watch for a few weeks
A column is always full and the one before it is idleThat stage is the bottleneckSwarm on it, cut work entering it, then consider more capacity; do not just raise its limit
People are often idle waiting for othersLimits may be too tight for current variability, or the work is blocked by other teamsLook at blocker causes first; raise a limit by one only if idleness is structural
Cycle time keeps risingWIP is creeping up or items are getting biggerCheck total WIP against limits; split large items
Many breaches each weekLimits do not match reality, or urgent work is unmanagedAdd an expedite lane with its own limit; revisit intake
Items age quietly in waiting columnsWaiting states are not limited or not countedAdd waiting columns to the limit; watch the WIP aging chart

Lower limits gradually where queues form. Raise one only when people are idle for structural reasons, such as a stage gaining a person. If the team changes size, revisit every limit.

Three charts help you judge whether limits are working: the cumulative flow diagram (are the bands stable?), the cycle time scatterplot (is the spread shrinking?) and the WIP aging chart (which items are getting old right now?). See Kanban metrics for how to read them together.

Introducing WIP limits to a team and stakeholders

The mechanics are simple; the change in habits is not. Resistance usually comes from team members who worry about looking idle and stakeholders who feel their requests are being refused.

With the team

  • Frame limits as an experiment for a fixed period, such as four weeks, with an agreed review at the end.
  • Explain that the aim is to keep work moving, not every person busy; some slack outside the bottleneck is healthy.
  • Agree what people do when they cannot pull work: help downstream, review, pair or improve tooling.

With stakeholders

  • Show them the Little's Law arithmetic with your own numbers.
  • Give them a predictable way in: a regular replenishment meeting where they help choose what enters Ready; see Kanban meetings and cadences.
  • Explain the expedite policy so they know genuinely urgent work has a route.

WIP limits in Scrum, Scrumban and personal Kanban

WIP limits are not only for Kanban teams.

Scrum

The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention WIP limits, but a sprint is itself a form of limit: the team commits to a forecast amount of work for a fixed period. Inside the sprint, though, teams often start too many items and finish several on the last day. Column limits on the sprint board, such as three items in progress for five developers, help finish items earlier and reduce carry-over.

Scrumban

Scrumban teams keep some Scrum events but replace the sprint commitment with continuous pull governed by WIP limits. See Kanban vs Scrumban for how the hybrid works.

Personal Kanban

Jim Benson and Tonianne DeMaria Barry's Personal Kanban reduces the method to two rules: visualise your work and limit your work in progress. A personal limit of two or three items in Doing is a common starting point. Our personal Kanban guide shows how to set one up.

Setting WIP limits in project management tools

Most digital boards can display WIP limits, but they differ in how strictly they enforce them. Whatever the tool, a limit only works if the team treats it as a rule, so write the policy down too.

  • Jira: Kanban board columns support minimum and maximum constraints, and the board highlights a column that breaks its constraint.
  • Azure Boards: each Kanban column can be given a WIP limit, and columns can be split into Doing and Done.
  • ClickUp: Board view supports WIP limits per column and flags a column when it is full.
  • Trello: the base board has no built-in WIP limits, so teams rely on conventions or Power-Ups.
  • Wrike, monday.com and Asana: all offer board views with custom status columns. Where a limit is not enforced natively, put the number in the column name and track counts by status on a dashboard. Wrike, our top-rated tool, is a good fit when many teams share work, because request forms, custom workflows and cross-project dashboards make WIP visible across teams.

Common WIP limit mistakes

  • Limits so high they never bite. If no column ever reaches its limit, nothing changes. Lower them until the board starts prompting conversations.
  • Cutting too far, too fast. Halving WIP overnight can stall the team. Reduce in steps.
  • Raising a limit whenever it is hit. A full column is information about a bottleneck. Fix the flow before touching the number.
  • Not counting waiting or blocked items. Queues and blocked work are where delay hides; leaving them out makes limits meaningless.
  • The same limit for every column. Stages with one specialist and stages with four people need different numbers.
  • Breaking limits without discussion. Occasional, visible, recorded breaches are fine. Silent ones teach everyone that the limits are optional.
  • Ignoring work outside the board. Support tickets and "quick favours" consume capacity. Put them on the board or set lower limits to allow for them.
  • Using WIP limits to judge individuals. Limits manage the system, not people.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good WIP limit?

There is no universal number. A practical starting point is slightly below your current average WIP, or about one to one and a half items per person in each active stage, then adjust based on cycle time and where work queues.

How do you calculate a WIP limit?

There is no exact formula. Count your current WIP per column, note how many people work in each stage, and set the limit a little below today's level. Little's Law (average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput) helps you estimate the effect: if you finish 8 items a week and want an average cycle time of 2 weeks, aim for roughly 16 items in progress across the system.

What does WIP stand for?

WIP stands for work in progress (sometimes work in process): items that have been started but not yet finished.

What happens when a WIP limit is reached?

Nobody pulls new items into that stage. Team members help finish, review or unblock existing work, starting with the items furthest to the right of the board, until there is space.

Can a team break a WIP limit?

Occasionally, for a genuine emergency, but the breach should be visible, recorded and discussed at the next review. A better approach is an expedite lane with its own limit of one. Frequent breaches mean the limit or the process needs to change.

Should blocked items count towards the WIP limit?

Yes, in most cases. Counting blocked items creates pressure to resolve blockers and stops a column filling up with stuck work while new items keep arriving.

Should the Done column have a WIP limit?

No. Done is the end of the flow, so limiting it would only stop work finishing. Waiting columns inside the workflow, such as Develop: Done or Ready for test, should be counted.

Do WIP limits slow teams down?

They can feel slower for the first week or two while people adjust, and throughput may dip briefly. After that, each item usually finishes sooner and delivery becomes more predictable, because work is completed rather than left half-done.

What is CONWIP?

CONWIP means constant work in progress. Instead of limiting each column, you cap the total number of items in the whole workflow, starting a new item only when one finishes. It is a simple way to start, but column limits show bottlenecks more clearly.

Can you set WIP limits per person?

Yes. A personal limit, such as no more than two items each, helps when individuals are overloaded or take requests from several people. Column limits usually reflect team flow better, so many teams use both.

Does Scrum use WIP limits?

The Scrum Guide does not require them, but the sprint itself limits how much work the team takes on. Many Scrum teams add column limits to their sprint board to finish items earlier and reduce carry-over.

What is the difference between a WIP limit and capacity?

Capacity is how much work a team can do in a period, often in hours or items per week. A WIP limit is how many items may be in progress at once. Capacity planning asks how much fits; WIP limits control how much is started. See capacity planning.

How often should you change WIP limits?

Review them every two to four weeks, or when the team changes size. Change one or two limits at a time, by one item, so you can see the effect of each change.
