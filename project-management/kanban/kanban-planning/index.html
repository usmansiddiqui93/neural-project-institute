Kanban Planning: How to Plan and Forecast Without Sprints

Kanban planning is the continuous process a Kanban team uses to decide what to start next and to forecast when work will be finished, using its own flow data instead of sprint commitments. Kanban has no sprints, but it is far from unplanned: teams replenish their queue regularly, commit to work at a clear point, and answer "when will it be done?" with probabilities. This guide walks through each planning layer, with worked examples of a service level expectation, a throughput forecast and a Monte Carlo simulation.

Key takeaways

  • Kanban plans continuously at several levels: strategy and portfolio reviews set direction, replenishment decides what starts next, and delivery planning decides what ships when.
  • The commitment point separates options, which can be dropped, from work the team has committed to finish.
  • Most Kanban teams count right-sized items instead of estimating effort, then forecast from their own cycle time and throughput history.
  • A service level expectation, such as "85% of items finish within 11 days", answers when a single item will be done.
  • For many items, Monte Carlo simulation on past weekly throughput gives a date range with a confidence level for each date.

What is Kanban planning?

Kanban planning is the ongoing practice of choosing which work to start next, committing to it only when the team has capacity, and forecasting delivery from historical flow data. Instead of planning a fixed batch of work for a sprint, a Kanban team replenishes a small queue regularly and uses cycle time and throughput to give probabilistic dates.

It answers two questions that every stakeholder asks. What will you work on next? is answered by the backlog, replenishment and classes of service. When will it be done? is answered by service level expectations for single items and throughput forecasts for groups of items.

Kanban planning in knowledge work builds on the Kanban Method and the Kanban Guide. In manufacturing and ERP systems, "Kanban planning" usually means something different: calculating card quantities and replenishment signals for parts and materials. If that is what you need, see our guide to Kanban inventory management.

Kanban planning vs sprint planning

Scrum and Kanban both plan, but they plan at different moments and with different units. Scrum teams plan a sprint at a fixed point; Kanban teams plan whenever capacity frees up and the queue needs refilling.

AspectScrum sprint planningKanban planning
When it happensAt the start of each sprintContinuously, with a regular replenishment meeting
What is committedA Sprint Goal and a forecast of backlog items for the sprintEach item individually, when it crosses the commitment point
How much is taken onBased on capacity and past performanceLimited by WIP limits and the size of the Ready column
EstimationCommonly story points (not required by the Scrum Guide)Often none; items are right-sized and counted
Answering "when?"Velocity and release planningService level expectations, throughput and Monte Carlo forecasts
Changing prioritiesNew work normally waits for the next sprintNew work can be pulled in at the next replenishment, or expedited

Neither approach is better in general. Kanban planning suits work that arrives unpredictably, such as support, operations and marketing requests, and teams that want to release continuously. See sprint planning for the Scrum event, and Kanban vs Scrum for a full comparison.

The layers of Kanban planning

Kanban planning happens at several levels, each with its own regular meeting, called a cadence. Kanban University describes seven cadences; most teams start with the daily meeting and replenishment, then add the others as they need them.

LayerCadenceQuestion it answersTypical starting frequency
StrategyStrategy reviewWhich services and outcomes should we focus on?Quarterly
Portfolio and capacityOperations review; risk reviewHow should capacity be shared across teams and work types? What could hurt delivery?Monthly
Service performanceService delivery reviewAre we meeting customers' expectations? What should change?Every one to two weeks
What starts nextReplenishment (commitment) meetingWhich options should we commit to next?Weekly
What ships whenDelivery planning meetingWhat goes into the next release, and who needs to know?For each release
Today's flowDaily Kanban meetingWhat is blocked or ageing, and how do we finish it?Daily, 15 minutes

Treat the frequencies as starting points rather than rules. A team that receives requests daily may replenish twice a week; a small team with stable work may combine the service delivery review with replenishment. See Kanban meetings and cadences.

Kanban University separates replenishment from delivery planning deliberately. Committing to start a piece of work is a different promise from committing to a release date, and keeping them apart helps you only make promises you can keep.

Backlog, options and the commitment point

A Kanban board has two zones. Upstream of the commitment point, items are options: ideas and requests that the team might do. Downstream, items are commitments that the team will finish.

  • Backlog (options): a pool of possible work. The Open Guide to Kanban treats a backlog as optional and emergent; it does not need to be a long, fully ordered list. Discarding options here is normal and healthy.
  • Ready (replenishment queue): the small set of options selected at replenishment, waiting to be pulled. Give it a WIP limit so it does not become a second backlog.
  • Commitment point: Kanban University defines it as the point at which the decision is made to start activities to deliver a work item. On most boards it is the line between Ready and the first active column.
  • Delivery point: where the item reaches the customer or is released.

The clock for cycle time usually starts at the commitment point. Everything before it is planning; everything after it is delivery, governed by WIP limits. Our free product backlog template helps you keep options tidy, and a work request template gives stakeholders a consistent way to submit new ones.

How to run a replenishment meeting

Replenishment is the heart of day-to-day Kanban planning. It is short, regular, and decides which options the team commits to next.

Who attends and what to bring

  • Attendees: the people who decide priorities (product owner, service manager or key stakeholders) and a few team members who understand the work.
  • Inputs: the current board, the number of free slots in Ready, the team's service level expectation, any fixed-date items, and the shortlist of options.

Agenda

  1. Check capacity. Count free slots in the Ready column (its WIP limit minus items already there). This is the maximum number of items you can select.
  2. Review what is coming due. List any fixed-date items whose latest start date falls before the next replenishment.
  3. Shortlist options. Stakeholders bring their top candidates. Discard anything that no longer matters.
  4. Prioritise. Order the shortlist using cost of delay and class of service, described below.
  5. Check size. For each candidate, ask whether it can finish within the SLE. Split anything that cannot.
  6. Confirm readiness. Make sure each selected item has enough information to start.
  7. Commit and communicate. Move the selected items into Ready and tell stakeholders what was chosen and what was not.

Keep it to 30 minutes or less; if it runs longer, the shortlist is too long or items are arriving unprepared. The outputs are a refilled Ready column and a clear record of what was deferred.

Worked example: one replenishment meeting

Illustrative example. A marketing operations team has a Ready limit of 8 and three items already waiting, so there are five free slots. Its SLE is 11 days, and it replenishes every Monday.

OptionClass of serviceFits the SLE?Decision
Landing page for a product launch on the 30thFixed dateYesSelected first: latest start is the 19th, before the next replenishment
Fix broken sign-up form trackingStandard (high cost of delay)YesSelected
Quarterly newsletterStandardYesSelected
Rebuild the whole resources sectionStandardNoSplit; the first slice (new navigation) selected
Two blog post requestsStandardYesOne selected, one deferred
Upgrade the email template libraryIntangibleYesDeferred; the improvement slot is already in use
Social campaign idea with no briefStandardUnknownReturned to the requester for a brief

Five items enter Ready, one large request is split, and the requesters of the deferred items hear why the same day.

Prioritising with cost of delay and classes of service

Cost of delay is the value lost by doing something later rather than sooner. Kanban groups work into classes of service according to the shape of that cost, and each class gets its own policy. Kanban University describes four archetypes:

Class of serviceImpact of delayPlanning policy
ExpediteHigh and immediatePulled straight away, outside replenishment; limit of one at a time
Fixed dateOccurs at a specific dateStart by a latest start date worked out from the SLE
StandardTypical of current conditionsSelected in order at replenishment
IntangibleUnknown or not yet feltKept flowing in a small share of capacity, such as upgrades and improvements

Classes of service are often shown as Kanban swimlanes so everyone can see how capacity is split.

Right-sizing instead of estimating

Many Kanban teams skip effort estimation. Instead of asking "how many points is this?", they ask a simpler question at replenishment: is this item small enough to finish within our SLE? If yes, it goes into Ready. If not, the team splits it.

This works because, once items are right-sized, counting them gives forecasts that are usually as useful as summing estimates, and it costs far less meeting time. Items will still vary in size, but that variation is already captured in the team's cycle time and throughput history.

  • Split by user outcome or workflow step rather than by technical layer, so each piece delivers something usable.
  • Watch out for items that are much bigger than the rest; they distort forecasts and age badly on the board.
  • Track how often large items split, because that tells you how much scope grows after commitment.

Story points can still help if your organisation needs them, or if items vary greatly in size. See story points for how they work.

Service level expectations: planning single items

A service level expectation (SLE) tells stakeholders how long a single item is likely to take once started. The Kanban Guide describes it as having two parts, a period of elapsed time and a probability, based on historical cycle time.

How to calculate an SLE

  1. Collect the cycle times of your most recent finished items, from the commitment point to the finish point. Twenty to thirty items is a reasonable sample.
  2. Sort them from shortest to longest.
  3. Pick a confidence level. The 85th percentile is a popular choice because it covers most items without being stretched by rare outliers.
  4. Find the value at that percentile. That is your SLE.
  5. Show it on the board and recalculate every month or so.

Worked example

Illustrative data. A team's last 20 finished items took these numbers of days: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15 and 21. The average is about 7.6 days, but the average hides the long tail, so the team uses percentiles instead.

PercentileCycle timeHow to say it
50th6 daysHalf our items finish within 6 days
70th8 daysSeven in ten finish within 8 days
85th11 days85% of items finish within 11 days (our SLE)
95th15 daysAlmost all items finish within 15 days

The team now tells stakeholders: "Once we start an item, 85% of the time it is done within 11 days."

Using the SLE day to day

The SLE is also a daily planning tool. Compare each in-progress item's age with it: an item at day 8 is approaching the SLE and needs attention before it becomes late. A WIP aging chart shows this at a glance.

Forecasting many items with throughput

When a stakeholder asks "when will these 40 items be done?", the SLE is not enough, because items flow in parallel. Use throughput, the number of items finished per week.

Illustrative data. Over the last 10 weeks a team finished 6, 9, 7, 4, 8, 10, 7, 5, 8 and 6 items, an average of 7 a week. The remaining scope is 40 items.

Method 1: average and range

MethodCalculationForecast
Average40 ÷ 7 items per weekAbout 5.7 weeks, so 6 weeks
Optimistic40 ÷ 10 (best week)4 weeks
Pessimistic40 ÷ 4 (worst week)10 weeks

The average gives roughly a 50/50 date, which is not a promise you want to make. The range is honest but very wide, because it assumes every week is as good or as bad as the extreme.

Method 2: Monte Carlo simulation

A Monte Carlo simulation produces something more useful: a probability for each possible date. The steps are:

  1. Take your recent weekly throughput history (here, the 10 weeks above).
  2. Simulate a future week by picking one of those weekly values at random. Repeat week by week, adding up the items, until the total reaches 40.
  3. Record how many weeks that took.
  4. Repeat the whole simulation thousands of times (we ran 10,000).
  5. Count how often each outcome occurred and turn the counts into cumulative percentages.
Finished withinProbability (40 items)Probability (48 items, allowing 20% growth)
5 weeksAbout 12%Close to 0%
6 weeksAbout 72%About 10%
7 weeksAbout 98%About 62%
8 weeksAlmost 100%About 96%

The team can now say: "We are about 72% likely to finish all 40 items within 6 weeks and very likely within 7." The third column shows why you should allow for scope growth. If past items have tended to split or attract extra work, a forecast on the original count will be optimistic.

Method 3: how many items by a date

Monte Carlo can answer the opposite question too. Simulating six weeks of throughput 10,000 times, the same team finished at least 42 items in half the runs, at least 38 items in 85% of runs and at least 35 items in 95% of runs. So for a deadline six weeks away, the team can commit with high confidence to the 35 to 38 most important items and treat the rest as stretch.

Tip

Give forecasts as a date and a confidence level, such as "85% likely by week 7", and update them every week as new throughput data arrives. A forecast that changes with evidence is a feature, not a failure.

Two cautions. Forecasts are only as reliable as the system is stable: if WIP is rising or the work mix has changed, the past is a weaker guide. And you need some history; ProKanban.org suggests you can begin with about ten completed items, accepting that early forecasts will be wide. For wider forecasting techniques, see our guide to project forecasting.

Planning around fixed deadlines

Kanban handles deadlines well, provided you plan backwards from them.

  1. Classify the item as fixed date. Mark it clearly on the board so it is visible at every daily meeting.
  2. Work out a latest start date. Subtract your SLE from the deadline. With an 85th-percentile SLE of 11 days, an item due on the 30th should start by the 19th; to be 95% confident, use the 95th percentile (15 days) and start by the 15th.
  3. For a group of items, forecast the scope. Use Monte Carlo to see how many items you can finish by the date, as in the example above.
  4. If the forecast misses, change something now. Reduce or reorder scope, protect capacity by pausing standard work, or agree a later date. Making the trade-off early is the point of forecasting.
  5. Track item age against the latest start and finish dates. Escalate at the daily meeting as soon as an item looks likely to miss.

Do not turn every item into a fixed-date item. If most work carries a deadline, the deadlines lose meaning and the team loses the ability to protect the genuinely time-critical ones.

Long-term planning: roadmaps and capacity allocation

Kanban does not replace roadmaps. Roadmaps sit above the board as themes, goals or large initiatives, reviewed at strategy and operations reviews. The board then shows how the pieces of those initiatives flow through delivery.

Capacity allocation

A practical way to connect strategy to the board is to split WIP across work types. Illustrative example: a team with a total WIP limit of 10 agrees the following allocation at its quarterly strategy review.

Work typeShare of capacityWIP slots
Roadmap features60%6
Defects and support20%2
Improvements and technical work10%1
Expedite10%1

Changing the allocation is a strategic decision: shifting one slot from features to improvements is visible, deliberate and easy to reverse. Throughput per work type then tells you how fast each roadmap theme is likely to move.

Portfolio Kanban

At portfolio level, the same ideas apply to larger items: initiatives flow through stages such as Proposed, Analysing, Approved, In progress and Done, with WIP limits on how many run at once. Our guides to the agile roadmap and agile portfolio management cover this level, and the product roadmap template gives you a starting structure. If several teams share a portfolio, a work management tool with cross-project dashboards and request forms, such as Wrike, makes the link between roadmap items and team boards easier to see.

Setting up Kanban planning step by step

If your team is new to Kanban planning, introduce it in this order:

  1. Map your workflow and mark the commitment point and the finish point on the board.
  2. Add a Ready column with a WIP limit, sized to roughly one replenishment interval of throughput.
  3. Set WIP limits on active columns so work is pulled, not pushed.
  4. Start recording dates: when each item crosses the commitment point and when it finishes.
  5. Hold a weekly replenishment meeting with the people who set priorities.
  6. Agree classes of service and a policy for expedite and fixed-date work.
  7. After 20 or more finished items, calculate an SLE and show it on the board.
  8. Start forecasting with throughput and review forecasts every week at the daily or service delivery meeting.

Common Kanban planning mistakes

  • Treating "no sprints" as "no planning". Without replenishment and forecasting, Kanban becomes a to-do list and stakeholders lose trust.
  • Letting Ready become a second backlog. An unlimited Ready column hides priorities; limit it.
  • Committing too early. Pulling items past the commitment point before there is capacity inflates WIP and lengthens cycle time.
  • Promising single dates. An average-based date is a coin toss. Give a date with a confidence level.
  • Forecasting an unstable system. If WIP is climbing, fix flow first; a cumulative flow diagram shows whether it is stable.
  • Ignoring scope growth. Allow for items splitting after commitment, as in the 48-item forecast above.
  • Making everything urgent. Too many expedite and fixed-date items destroy predictability for all other work.

Frequently asked questions

How does Kanban planning work without sprints?

The team replenishes a small Ready column at regular meetings, commits to each item when it crosses the commitment point, and limits WIP so work is pulled as capacity frees up. Dates come from historical cycle time and throughput rather than a sprint commitment.

Does Kanban have sprint planning?

No. Kanban has no sprints, so there is no sprint planning. Its closest equivalents are the replenishment meeting, which decides what starts next, and the delivery planning meeting, which decides what ships in each release.

What is a replenishment meeting in Kanban?

A short, regular meeting where the people who set priorities and the team choose which options to move into the Ready column, based on free capacity, cost of delay and class of service.

How often should a Kanban team replenish?

Weekly is a common starting point. Replenish often enough that the Ready column never runs dry but not so often that the meeting becomes a distraction; teams with fast-moving demand may replenish twice a week.

What is a commitment point in Kanban?

The point on the board at which the team decides to start delivering an item, usually the line between Ready and the first active column. Before it, items are options that can be dropped; after it, the team commits to finishing them.

Does Kanban planning involve estimating?

Often not in effort. Many teams right-size items so each can finish within the service level expectation, then count items and forecast from throughput. Some teams still estimate when items vary greatly in size.

Are story points used in Kanban?

They are optional. Kanban does not require them, and many teams find counting right-sized items simpler and just as useful for forecasting.

What is a service level expectation in Kanban?

A forecast of how long a single item should take once started, made of a time period and a probability, such as "85% of items finish within 11 days". It is calculated from historical cycle times.

How do you forecast delivery dates in Kanban?

Use the SLE for single items. For many items, divide the remaining count by average weekly throughput for a rough figure, or run a Monte Carlo simulation on past weekly throughput to get a probability for each date.

What is Monte Carlo forecasting in Kanban?

A simulation that randomly samples your past weekly throughput thousands of times to model possible futures. It gives answers such as "72% likely within 6 weeks, 98% likely within 7".

How much data do you need to forecast in Kanban?

You can start with about ten finished items, accepting wide ranges. Forecasts improve as you collect more weeks of throughput, as long as the workflow stays reasonably stable.

Can Kanban handle fixed deadlines?

Yes. Use a fixed-date class of service, work out a latest start date by subtracting your SLE from the deadline, and use throughput forecasts to check that a larger scope can fit before the date.

What is the difference between the backlog and the Ready column?

The backlog holds options that may or may not be done. The Ready column holds the small, limited set selected at replenishment, waiting to be pulled when capacity frees up.

How does Kanban planning handle long-term roadmaps?

Roadmap themes and initiatives sit above the board and are reviewed at strategy and operations reviews. Capacity allocation across work types links them to day-to-day flow, and throughput shows how fast each theme is moving.
