Kanban vs Agile: Differences, Overlap and When to Use Each

Kanban vs Agile is not a choice between two rival methods: Agile is a set of values and principles for delivering work in small increments and adapting to change, while Kanban is a flow-based method that many teams use to work in an Agile way. The real decision is between Kanban's continuous flow and time-boxed Agile frameworks such as Scrum. This guide explains how the two relate, compares boards, planning and metrics, works through a real-world style example and gives you a step-by-step process for choosing.

Key takeaways

  • Agile is a philosophy set out in the 2001 Agile Manifesto; Kanban is a method for managing and improving the flow of work. They sit at different levels, so they are not alternatives.
  • Kanban comes from Lean and the Toyota Production System, not from the Agile software movement, but it fits the Manifesto's values closely and is widely counted as an Agile approach.
  • There are two main definitions of Kanban today: the Kanban Method (Kanban University) and The Kanban Guide (Vacanti and Coleman, edition 2025.5). Both centre on visualising work, limiting work in progress and improving flow.
  • The practical comparison is flow vs time-box: Kanban has no sprints or prescribed roles and measures cycle time and throughput, while Scrum uses fixed Sprints, three accountabilities and Sprint Goals.
  • Kanban suits work that arrives unpredictably or continuously; time-boxed frameworks suit a dedicated team building a product towards planned goals. Many teams combine the two.

Kanban vs Agile: the short answer

Agile is a mindset for delivering value in small, frequent increments, collaborating closely with customers and adapting the plan as you learn. Kanban is a method for visualising work, limiting how much is in progress and improving the flow of items from request to delivery. Agile tells you what to value; Kanban gives you one practical way to act on those values.

AgileKanban
What it isA set of values and principlesA method for managing and improving flow
Defined byThe Agile Manifesto (2001): 4 values, 12 principlesThe Kanban Method (Kanban University) and The Kanban Guide (edition 2025.5)

Tells youWhat to value and whyHow to see, limit and improve the work in your system
RelationshipThe umbrellaOne of the approaches used under it

So the real question is not Agile or Kanban, but continuous flow, fixed Sprints, or a mix.

What is Agile?

Agile is an approach to product and project work that delivers in short cycles, uses feedback to steer and treats the plan as something to update rather than defend. It was named in February 2001, when 17 software practitioners wrote the Agile Manifesto. The Manifesto values individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over following a plan.

Twelve principles add detail. Among them: satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery; welcome changing requirements, even late in development; deliver working software frequently, preferring shorter timescales; maintain a sustainable pace indefinitely; and, at regular intervals, reflect on how to become more effective and adjust.

Just as important is what Agile does not contain. It prescribes no roles, meetings, iteration length, board or metric. Those come from the frameworks and methods that put Agile into practice, including Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming (XP), Lean software development, Scrumban and scaled frameworks such as SAFe.

What is Kanban?

"Kanban" is Japanese for signboard or visual signal. At Toyota, from the late 1940s onwards, cards travelled with parts and signalled when an upstream process should make more, so that production followed real consumption rather than a forecast. That is the origin of the pull system, explained in our guide to pull vs push systems.

Applied to knowledge work, Kanban grew from David J. Anderson's work with software teams, starting with a project at Microsoft in 2004 and continuing at Corbis, and was described in his 2010 book *Kanban: Successful Evolutionary Change for Your Technology Business*. Today two well-known sources define it.

The Kanban Method (Kanban University)

Kanban University's official guide describes Kanban as a method for defining, managing and improving services that deliver knowledge work. It sets out three change management principles (start with what you do now; agree to pursue improvement through evolutionary change; encourage acts of leadership at all levels), three service delivery principles (understand and focus on customer needs and expectations; manage the work and let people self-organise around it; regularly review the network of services and its policies) and six general practices:

  1. Visualise the work and the workflow.
  2. Limit work in progress (WIP).
  3. Manage flow.
  4. Make policies explicit.
  5. Implement feedback loops, such as a daily team Kanban meeting and regular replenishment and retrospectives.
  6. Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally.

The Kanban Guide (Vacanti and Coleman)

The Kanban Guide, written by Daniel Vacanti and John Coleman (current edition 2025.5, May 2025), defines Kanban as a strategy for optimising the flow of value through a process. It has three practices: defining and visualising a workflow, actively managing items in a workflow, and improving a workflow. It requires four flow measures: WIP (items started but not finished), throughput (items finished per unit of time), work item age (time since an unfinished item started) and cycle time (time from start to finish).

The two differ in emphasis but agree on the core: make work visible, limit WIP so that work is pulled rather than pushed, and use data to improve. Neither prescribes roles, Sprints or estimation. For the principles in more detail, see Kanban principles and practices, or start with what is Kanban.

Is Kanban Agile?

In practice, yes: Kanban is almost always listed among Agile approaches, and most Agile teams use a Kanban board at some level. The nuance is that Kanban was not created from the Agile Manifesto. Its roots are Lean manufacturing, and The Kanban Guide does not mention Agile at all; it draws on Lean principles, systems thinking and the Toyota Production System. The Kanban Method describes itself as a way to manage any professional service, Agile or not.

It is accurate to say Kanban is a Lean method that is fully compatible with, and widely used for, Agile ways of working.

How Kanban puts the 12 Agile principles into practice

A useful way to judge whether a method is Agile is to check it against the twelve principles. The table below is our own mapping, not part of either official document. The third column shows where Kanban says little and you need to add a practice of your own.

Tip

Two principles, face-to-face conversation and technical excellence, are where Kanban teams most often fall short. Kanban is a flow method, not an engineering method, so software teams usually pair it with practices such as automated testing and continuous integration.

Kanban vs time-boxed Agile frameworks: key differences

Because Agile itself prescribes no practices, the meaningful comparison is between Kanban and the time-boxed frameworks people usually mean by "Agile", most often Scrum. The Scrum column below follows the 2020 Scrum Guide.

Agile (Scrum) board vs Kanban board

Both boards show cards moving left to right, which is why they are often confused. The difference is in the rules, not the columns.

  • Lifespan: a Scrum board usually holds the current Sprint Backlog and is cleared at the end of each Sprint. A Kanban board is persistent: items stay until they are done, however long that takes.
  • WIP limits: a Kanban board shows a limit on columns or on the whole system. A Scrum board normally has none, because the Sprint itself caps the work.
  • Commitment point: a Kanban board marks the point where an option becomes a commitment (often between "Options" and "Ready"). On a Scrum board the commitment happens at Sprint Planning.
  • Explicit policies: Kanban boards often show entry and exit criteria for each column, such as "Ready = acceptance criteria agreed".
  • Classes of service: Kanban teams may add an expedite lane or colour code for fixed-date items. See Kanban swimlanes.

Agile metrics vs Kanban metrics

Time-boxed teams tend to measure how much they finish per Sprint; Kanban teams measure how long items take and how many finish per week. Both are useful, and many teams track both.

MetricTypical inWhat it tells youWatch out for
VelocityScrumStory points completed per Sprint, used for forecastingNot comparable between teams; easy to inflate
Sprint burndownScrumWork remaining in the SprintSays nothing about flow after the Sprint
WIPKanbanHow many items are started but not finishedCount items, not people
ThroughputKanbanItems finished per weekNeeds items of broadly similar size
Cycle timeKanbanTime from start to finish for each itemUse percentiles, not only the average
Work item ageKanbanHow long unfinished items have been in progressOld items are your early warning

Worked example: Little's Law

Little's Law, first proved by John Little in 1961, links three of the Kanban measures: average cycle time = average WIP / average throughput. Suppose your team usually has 12 items in progress and finishes 4 a week. On average an item takes 12 / 4 = 3 weeks from start to finish. If you cap WIP at 6 and throughput stays at 4 a week, average cycle time falls to 6 / 4 = 1.5 weeks. Nobody worked faster; items simply spent less time waiting.

The law holds over the long run only when the system is stable (roughly as many items start as finish, and items are not abandoned), so use it to understand your system rather than to promise dates. Compare with the Agile velocity chart and cycle time.

Worked example: one team, two Agile ways

The following example is illustrative: the team and figures are invented to show the mechanics, not taken from a study.

A five-person internal digital team at a regional housing association looks after the tenant website and portal. Its work is a mix: about 60% planned improvements (a new repairs-booking journey, accessibility fixes) and about 40% unplanned requests (content changes, urgent bugs, requests from the call centre).

Version A: two-week Sprints

  • Sprint Planning every other Monday picks enough planned work to fill about 80% of capacity, leaving a buffer for urgent requests.
  • Urgent bugs interrupt the Sprint two or three times a week. The team often misses its Sprint Goal and the buffer is either too big or too small.
  • Content requests from the call centre wait up to two weeks for the next Sprint unless someone treats them as urgent.
  • The repairs-booking journey gets focused attention, and the Sprint Review gives tenants' representatives a regular slot to see progress.

Version B: Kanban

  • Board columns: Options, Ready (limit 5), Build (limit 3), Test (limit 2), Done.
  • Two classes of service: Expedite (one item at a time, may exceed limits) for portal outages, and Standard for everything else.
  • Cadences: 15-minute daily meeting at the board; weekly replenishment with the service manager to choose what moves into Ready; monthly service delivery review with call-centre and housing leads; monthly retrospective.
  • Planned and unplanned work share one board, so the trade-off between them is visible and decided at replenishment rather than in the middle of a Sprint.

What six weeks of data showed

MeasureSprints (weeks 1 to 6)Kanban (weeks 7 to 12)
Average WIP11 items6 items
Throughput5 items a week5.5 items a week
Average cycle timeabout 2.2 weeksabout 1.1 weeks
Content requests: typical waitup to 2 weeks2 to 4 days

The lesson is not that Kanban "won". Kanban suited this team's mixed, interrupt-driven work, but it needed an explicit policy (reserving Build capacity) to protect the planned project, something Sprints had provided for free. A team whose work was almost all planned product development would probably have kept Sprints. Plan replenishment and reviews using our guide to Kanban ceremonies.

None of these is a rule, which is why the next section gives you a process rather than a verdict.

How to choose: a step-by-step decision process

  1. Describe your work. Mark the last 30 items your team finished as planned or unplanned, and note how urgent each was on arrival.
  2. Check how predictable arrivals are. If more than about a third were unplanned and needed doing within days, Sprints will be interrupted often.
  3. Check whether you need a shared goal per cycle. One product heading for a release benefits from a Sprint Goal; a team serving many requesters often does not.
  4. Look at your stakeholders. Do they want regular review dates or quick turnaround on individual requests?
  5. Consider how much change you can absorb. Scrum adds new accountabilities and events; Kanban starts with what you do now.
  6. Score the options below, choose, and agree a date (say, eight weeks out) to review the data and adjust.

Decision scorecard

Score each statement from 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree). A higher left-hand total points to Kanban, a higher right-hand total to a time-boxed framework; close scores suggest Scrumban.

How to introduce Kanban in an Agile team

  1. Map your current workflow. Write down the real stages work goes through today, including waiting stages such as "awaiting approval".
  2. Build the board. One column per stage, plus Options and Done. Put every item currently in progress on it, including the ones nobody likes to admit to.
  3. Agree the commitment point and Definition of Done. Decide where an idea becomes a commitment and what "done" means for your customers.
  4. Set initial WIP limits. A common starting point is around the number of people working in a stage, or slightly lower. Treat the first limits as an experiment.
  5. Make policies explicit. Write entry and exit criteria for columns, how to handle expedites, and how blocked items are shown.
  6. Start the cadences. A short daily meeting at the board, a replenishment meeting, and a regular review and retrospective.
  7. Measure from day one. Record start and finish dates for every item so that WIP, throughput, cycle time and work item age are available after a few weeks.
  8. Improve one thing at a time. Use the data to find the biggest queue or most frequent blocker, change one policy, and check the effect.

Combining Kanban with other Agile frameworks

Kanban is designed to be applied to an existing process, so it combines readily with other approaches.

  • Scrum with Kanban practices: a Scrum team keeps its Sprints, roles and events but adds WIP limits, flow metrics and explicit policies to the Sprint Backlog. This is permitted by the Scrum Guide, which leaves the team free to choose its own practices.
  • Scrumban: a hybrid that started as a way to move from Scrum to Kanban (described by Corey Ladas in 2008) and is now often used as a lasting mix of planning cadence and flow. See Kanban vs Scrumban.
  • Scaled frameworks: SAFe uses Kanban systems to manage the flow of epics at portfolio level, alongside time-boxed iterations for teams. See SAFe.

For the full family of approaches, read types of Agile methodologies, and for the Scrum side specifically, Agile vs Scrum.

Common misconceptions and mistakes

  • "We have a board, so we do Kanban." A board without WIP limits, explicit policies and flow measurement is a visual to-do list. Useful, but not Kanban.
  • "Kanban means no planning." Kanban plans continuously through replenishment and uses throughput data to forecast. It simply does not tie planning to a fixed time-box.
  • "Kanban means no deadlines." Fixed-date work is handled as a class of service and prioritised so that it finishes in time.
  • Setting WIP limits and then ignoring them. If the limit is always exceeded, either change it deliberately or treat the breach as a signal to swarm on blocked work.
  • Measuring people instead of flow. Cycle time and throughput describe the system. Using them to rank individuals encourages gaming and hides problems.
  • Dropping reflection. Without Sprints forcing a retrospective, some Kanban teams stop improving. Put a retrospective in the calendar.

Frequently asked questions

Is Kanban Agile?

Yes, in practice. Kanban is usually listed among Agile approaches because it delivers value continuously, welcomes changing priorities and builds in regular reflection. Strictly, it comes from Lean manufacturing rather than the Agile Manifesto, so it is best described as a Lean method that fits Agile values.

What is the difference between Kanban, Agile and Scrum?

Agile is the philosophy: four values and twelve principles. Scrum and Kanban are two ways to apply it. Scrum uses fixed-length Sprints, three accountabilities and five events; Kanban uses continuous flow, WIP limits and flow metrics, without prescribed roles or Sprints.

Does Kanban have sprints?

No. Kanban uses continuous flow: items are pulled when there is capacity and released when they are done. Teams still hold regular meetings, such as replenishment and reviews, but they are not tied to a delivery time-box. If you want Sprints with Kanban practices, look at Scrumban.

Which is better, Agile or Kanban?

Neither, because they are not alternatives. Kanban is one way of being Agile. The useful question is whether continuous flow (Kanban) or fixed time-boxes (such as Scrum) suit your work better; the decision process above helps you answer it.

Is Kanban Agile or Lean?

Both. Kanban's origins are in the Toyota Production System and Lean thinking, and The Kanban Guide does not mention Agile. Because it shares Agile's focus on customer value, feedback and improvement, it is widely used and taught as an Agile approach too.

Is Kanban a methodology or a framework?

It is usually called a method: the Kanban Method. It provides principles and practices that you apply to your existing process, rather than a complete framework of roles and events. The Kanban Guide calls it a strategy for optimising the flow of value.

Does Kanban have roles?

Neither the Kanban Method nor The Kanban Guide requires roles. Some teams use optional roles such as a service request manager (who orders incoming work) and a service delivery manager (who looks after flow), but you can keep your existing job titles.

Can you use Kanban without Scrum?

Yes. Many teams practise Agile using Kanban alone. Kanban needs a visual workflow, WIP limits, explicit policies and feedback loops, none of which depends on Scrum.

Is a Kanban board in Jira or Trello automatically Agile?

No. A tool's board view is a way of displaying work. It becomes Kanban when you add WIP limits, explicit policies and flow measurement, and it supports Agile when the team uses it to deliver small increments and adapt based on feedback.

Is Kanban iterative?

Kanban is incremental and continuous rather than iterative in fixed cycles. Each item is delivered when finished, and the process is improved through regular feedback loops.

Can non-software teams use Kanban as their Agile approach?

Yes. Marketing, HR, finance, legal, operations and construction teams often use Kanban as their entry point to Agile because it starts with their current process and needs no new roles or Sprints.
