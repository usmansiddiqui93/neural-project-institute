Kanban Inventory Management: Two-Bin System and Formula

Kanban inventory management is a replenishment method in which a signal (a card, an empty bin or an electronic message) triggers restocking only when stock has actually been used. It keeps inventory low and matched to real demand without complex forecasting. This guide explains two-bin and card systems, works through the kanban formula step by step and shows you how to set up, run and improve a kanban loop.

Key takeaways

  • Kanban inventory replaces exactly what was used: a fixed quantity is reordered whenever a container empties, so timing varies with demand while quantities stay the same.
  • The two-bin system is the simplest form. Size each bin to cover demand during the replenishment lead time plus a safety allowance.
  • Number of kanbans = daily demand × lead time (days) × (1 + safety factor) ÷ container quantity, rounded up.
  • Replenishment lead time includes waiting for the card to be collected, ordering, making or supplying, transport and put-away. Underestimating it is the most common sizing error.
  • Kanban suits items with steady, repeating demand. Test variability (an XYZ check) before you convert an item.
  • Shorter lead times reduce inventory far more than smaller containers. Review card counts regularly and remove cards to drive improvement.

What is kanban inventory management?

Kanban inventory management is a pull-based way of controlling stock in which each container of parts or materials carries a signal. When the container is emptied, the signal goes back to the supplier or producing process as an authorisation to replace that container, and nothing more. Inventory is therefore driven by actual consumption rather than forecasts, and stays within a fixed, visible maximum.

"Kanban" is Japanese for signboard or visual card. The method comes from the Toyota Production System, where it is the day-to-day control mechanism for Just-in-Time (JIT) production. Toyota's history records that it introduced a "supermarket method" in 1954, modelled on the way supermarkets restock only what customers take; once a production instruction was added, this became the kanban system.

The same ideas, visual signals and a fixed limit on the amount of work in the system, were later adapted to knowledge work as Kanban for projects. This guide covers the original, physical-inventory version.

How a kanban replenishment loop works

Every kanban system is made up of loops. A loop links one consuming point (a workstation, a ward, a shelf) with one supplying point (a store, an upstream process, an external supplier) through a fixed number of containers, each with its own signal.

  1. Containers are filled to a fixed quantity and each carries a kanban card or label.
  2. The consuming point uses parts from one container at a time.
  3. When a container is empty, its card is removed and placed in a collection post, or the empty bin itself is returned, or a barcode is scanned.
  4. The signal reaches the supplying point on a regular collection route or electronically.
  5. The supplier makes or picks exactly one container's quantity, attaches the card and sends it back.
  6. The full container returns to its location before the consuming point runs out.

The defining feature is fixed quantity, variable timing. The quantity per container never changes between reviews; what changes is how often containers come back. When demand rises, cards cycle faster; when it falls, they cycle slower, and the system adjusts without anyone re-planning.

Toyota's rules for kanban

Toyota's rules for using kanban are widely quoted, usually in a form close to this:

  1. The downstream process withdraws only the items and quantities shown on the kanban.
  2. The upstream process produces only the items, quantities and sequence shown on the kanban.
  3. Nothing is made or moved without a kanban.
  4. A kanban always stays attached to its goods.
  5. Defective items are never sent to the next process.
  6. Reducing the number of kanbans, carefully, exposes problems and drives improvement.

Rules 3 and 4 are the ones most often broken in practice. If people order "a few extra" without a card, or cards are separated from their containers, the loop's inventory limit stops meaning anything.

The two-bin system

The two-bin system is the simplest kanban, and often the best place to start. Each item is held in two containers, usually one in front of the other or one on top of the other.

  1. Use stock from the front (or top) bin only.
  2. When it is empty, move the empty bin, or its card, to the reorder point: a shelf, a box, a board or a scanner.
  3. Pull the back bin forward and keep working from it.
  4. The empty bin is refilled to its fixed quantity and returned behind the one in use.
  5. If the second bin empties before the first returns, that is a signal that the bin size, lead time or demand has changed and the loop needs review.

Sizing a two-bin system: worked example

A hospital ward uses an average of 12 boxes of examination gloves a day. The materials team collects empty bins once a day and delivers refills the following day, so the worst-case time from "bin empty" to "bin back on the shelf" is 2 days. The ward manager wants 1 extra day of cover for busy periods.

  • Bin quantity = daily usage × (replenishment lead time + safety days)
  • Bin quantity = 12 × (2 + 1) = 36 boxes per bin.
  • Check: when the first bin empties, the second holds 36 boxes. In the worst case, 2 days pass before the refill returns, using 24 boxes and leaving 12, which is the 1-day safety allowance.
  • Maximum on the shelf is 72 boxes (both bins full); typical stock is well below that.

Two-bin systems work best for low-cost, high-use items with short lead times: fasteners, consumables, packaging, office and cleaning supplies, and medical consumables. Many hospitals and workshops use them because they need almost no training and no software.

Tip

Track express kanbans. If you need them often, the loop is undersized or the process is unstable, and the root cause should go onto your improvement list.

A good inventory kanban card answers every question the person refilling the container might have, so nobody has to look anything up. A typical card includes:

Laminated cards or durable labels survive best. Number every card ("3 of 6") so you can audit the loop: if only five cards can be found, one is lost or a container is hiding somewhere. For the project-board equivalent, see our guide to Kanban cards.

How to calculate the number of kanbans

The number of kanbans in a loop sets the maximum inventory the loop can hold. Too few and you run out; too many and you carry stock you do not need.

The kanban formula

A widely used formula, associated with Toyota and found in most operations textbooks, is:

N = (D × L × (1 + α)) ÷ C

  • N = number of kanbans (cards or containers) in the loop, rounded up.
  • D = average demand per period, for example units per day.
  • L = replenishment lead time in the same period, for example days.
  • α (alpha) = safety factor, as a decimal: 0.1 means 10% extra.
  • C = quantity per container.

Some organisations express safety as days of cover instead: N = D × (L + safety days) ÷ C. Both are equivalent ways of adding a buffer. More detailed methods, such as the one set out by AllAboutLean, add extra kanbans for lot-size formation, batch orders and demand peaks. Whichever you use, treat the answer as a starting estimate to be adjusted by observation.

Break down the replenishment lead time

L is not the supplier's quoted lead time. It is the full time from the moment a container empties to the moment it is back, full, at the point of use. In this example, an assembly line runs a 16-hour day and is supplied by a machining cell.

Lead time elementHoursNotes
Waiting for the card to be collected2.0Cards are collected every 2 hours, so allow the worst case
Queue at the machining cell3.0Other kanbans ahead of it in the queue
Production of one container (40 parts at 90 seconds each)1.0Including a short set-up
Transport to the supermarket and put-away1.0Fixed material-handling route
Total replenishment lead time7.07 ÷ 16 = 0.4375 days

Worked example

  1. Demand (D): assembly uses 480 parts a day.
  2. Lead time (L): 0.4375 days, from the table above.
  3. Safety factor (α): 20% (0.2), because the cell's queue time varies.
  4. Container quantity (C): 40 parts.
  5. Calculate: N = (480 × 0.4375 × 1.2) ÷ 40 = 252 ÷ 40 = 6.3.
  6. Round up: use 7 kanbans. The loop can never hold more than 7 × 40 = 280 parts.

What really drives the number of cards

Using the same demand and safety factor, here is how the number of kanbans (N) and the maximum loop inventory (N × C) change with lead time and container size.

Lead timeContainer 20Container 40Container 80
0.25 days (4 hours)8 cards, max 160 parts4 cards, max 160 parts2 cards, max 160 parts
0.4375 days (7 hours)13 cards, max 260 parts7 cards, max 280 parts4 cards, max 320 parts
1 day (16 hours)29 cards, max 580 parts15 cards, max 600 parts8 cards, max 640 parts
  • Lead time dominates. Cutting lead time from 1 day to 4 hours reduces maximum inventory by roughly 70 to 75%, whatever the container size.
  • Container size mainly affects rounding. Smaller containers trim the maximum a little and smooth the flow, but they also mean more cards, trips and handling.
  • So attack lead time first: collect cards more often, cut queue time with WIP limits, reduce set-ups and shorten transport routes. Our guide to cycle time vs lead time explains how to measure each.
Tip

Always round up, never down, and use at least two kanbans per loop: one being used, one being replenished. Record why you chose your safety factor so the next review can challenge it.

Which items suit kanban? (with a worked XYZ check)

Kanban works best for items with steady, repeating demand. A quick way to test that is an XYZ analysis, which classifies items by how variable their demand is, using the coefficient of variation (CV) = standard deviation ÷ average. It is often combined with ABC analysis, which classifies items by annual value.

Here are five weeks of demand for three items. In practice, use at least 12 weeks of data; five weeks are used here only to keep the arithmetic short.

ItemWeekly demand (5 weeks)AverageStandard deviationCVClass
M8 bolts100, 96, 104, 102, 981002.83%X (steady)
Shipping cartons80, 40, 120, 60, 1008028.335%Y (variable)
Spare motor0, 40, 5, 120, 103544.7128%Z (erratic)

Thresholds vary between organisations. In this guide we use X below 25%, Y from 25% to 75% and Z above 75%. Then match the policy to the class:

ClassSuggested policy
X: steady demandKanban or two-bin; small safety factor
Y: variable demandKanban with a larger safety factor, more frequent card-count reviews, or a seasonal card adjustment
Z: erratic or one-off demandOrder to requirement (MRP or make-to-order), or a minimal stock with a manual review; not usually kanban

Also exclude, or treat separately, items with very long or unreliable supply lead times, short shelf lives, very high unit costs or upcoming design changes. For those, decide the buffer deliberately; our JIT guide includes a supply-risk matrix for this.

Kanban vs reorder point, min-max and MRP

Kanban is one of several replenishment methods. They often sit side by side in the same organisation.

MethodWhat triggers an orderOrder quantityBest forWatch out for
Kanban / two-binA container empties (physical or scanned signal)Fixed: one containerSteady, repeating items; visual control at the point of useLost cards, unofficial extra orders, demand changes not reflected in card counts
Reorder point (ROP)Recorded stock falls to a set levelFixed (often EOQ)Stocked items managed in an inventory systemDepends on accurate stock records
Min-maxStock falls to or below the minimumVariable: up to the maximumRetail, spares and maintenance storesPeriodic reviews can miss fast consumption
MRPPlanned requirements from a master schedule and bill of materialsCalculated per requirementComplex, variable or one-off demand; long lead timesForecast errors; frequent re-planning ("nervousness")

A two-bin system is effectively a reorder point system in which the reorder point is physical: the second bin is the reorder level. That is why it works without accurate stock records. Many manufacturers use MRP for planning and long-lead purchases and kanban for day-to-day replenishment, which is a practical blend of push and pull.

How to set up kanban inventory in 10 steps

  1. Choose a pilot area with a manageable number of steady items, such as one production line, ward or store room.
  2. Map the flow of materials and information from supplier to point of use; a simple value stream map is ideal.
  3. Select items using ABC and XYZ analysis; start with high-use, low-variability, low-cost items.
  4. Collect data: average daily usage, its variability and every element of replenishment lead time.
  5. Choose containers and standard quantities that suit handling, space and supplier pack sizes.
  6. Calculate the number of kanbans for each item with the formula, rounding up.
  7. Design and print cards or labels, number them, and mark locations clearly using visual management (labels, colours, shelf markings).
  8. Agree the rules with suppliers and the team: collection times, response times, what to do if a bin runs out and how express kanbans work. Put external supplier commitments in writing, for example in a service level agreement or contract; an RFQ template helps when you go out to suppliers.
  9. Train everyone and go live, keeping the old method available as a fallback for the first few weeks.
  10. Review and improve: monitor stock-outs, express kanbans and maximum stock; recalculate card counts monthly or when demand changes; remove a card when the loop is consistently over-supplied.

Treat the pilot as a small project with a clear owner, a start and end date and success measures, then roll out in waves. Our guide to manufacturing project management covers running improvement projects on a live site.

Running a kanban inventory board

A board makes the replenishment pipeline visible, which is especially useful for bought-in items where a card disappears to purchasing for days. A simple purchasing board has these columns:

ColumnWhat it meansOwner
Signal receivedCards or scans waiting to be processedPurchasing
OrderedOrder placed with the supplierPurchasing
In transitSupplier has confirmed dispatchSupplier / logistics
ReceivedGoods booked in, awaiting put-awayStores
Back in useContainer returned to its location (cards then recycle)Stores
  • Add a swimlane per supplier or item category so a single late supplier stands out; see Kanban swimlanes.
  • Flag cards that exceed their expected lead time, for example with a red dot, and review them daily.
  • Track a few simple measures: stock-outs, express kanbans used, average replenishment time and on-time supplier delivery. Our guide to Kanban metrics explains how lead time and throughput are measured on a board.

You can run the board on a whiteboard, in a spreadsheet or with our free Kanban board template.

E-kanban and software

Electronic kanban (e-kanban) keeps the same logic but sends signals digitally. Typically, someone scans the barcode or QR code on an empty container, and the system raises a replenishment request, notifies the supplier or creates a purchase order, often through an ERP system.

  • Benefits: no lost cards, instant signals to distant suppliers, accurate data on lead time and usage, and easier recalculation of card counts.
  • Costs: scanners, software and integration effort, plus the risk that people trust the screen and stop looking at the shelf.
  • Good fit: multi-site operations, many external suppliers, regulated environments that need traceability, and loops with long physical distances.

Options range from kanban modules inside ERP and inventory systems, through specialist e-kanban platforms, to general work-management tools used as a purchasing board. Tools such as Wrike or Trello can host a clear replenishment board with columns, owners and due dates, while scanning, stock levels and automatic purchase orders are better handled by inventory or ERP software. Many small operations simply start with cards and a board, and add scanning once the loops are stable.

Common kanban inventory mistakes to avoid

  • Using the supplier's quoted lead time instead of the full replenishment time, including waiting for cards to be collected.
  • Setting card counts once and never revisiting them. Demand, suppliers and container sizes change.
  • Including unsuitable items (erratic, long-lead or expensive) and then blaming kanban for stock-outs.
  • Allowing "just in case" orders outside the loop, which quietly inflates inventory.
  • Not numbering cards, so lost cards go unnoticed until a stock-out.
  • No rule for empty bins. Decide in advance who acts and how quickly when a second bin starts to run low.
  • Over-engineering the first pilot with software before the physical loop works.
  • Ignoring the bottleneck. If an upstream process simply cannot keep up, no number of cards will fix it; see the Theory of Constraints.

From inventory kanban to project Kanban

Project and software teams borrowed kanban's core ideas. A Kanban board's columns are the stages of a replenishment loop, its cards are the signals, and its WIP limits play the same role as a fixed number of kanbans: nothing new starts until something finishes. Individuals use the same principle in personal Kanban.

If you manage the procurement side of a project, kanban can also run your site or workshop stores, while your procurement plan handles long-lead and one-off purchases.

To understand the production philosophy kanban was designed to support, read Just-in-Time (JIT).

For the wider system of waste reduction it belongs to, see Lean manufacturing.

Frequently asked questions

What is kanban inventory management?

Kanban inventory management uses signals, such as cards, empty bins or barcode scans, to trigger replenishment only when stock has been used. A fixed quantity is replaced each time, keeping inventory low and matched to real demand.

How does a two-bin kanban system work?

Stock is split into two bins. You use the first; when it is empty it becomes the reorder signal, and the second bin covers demand until the refilled first bin returns. Each bin should cover demand during the replenishment lead time plus a safety allowance.

How do you calculate the number of kanbans?

Number of kanbans = daily demand × replenishment lead time in days × (1 + safety factor) ÷ container quantity, rounded up. For example, (480 × 0.4375 × 1.2) ÷ 40 = 6.3, so use 7 kanbans.

What information goes on a kanban card?

Part number and description, quantity per container, container type, point of use, supplying location or supplier, lead time, a card number such as "3 of 6", and usually a barcode or QR code.

Is kanban inventory the same as Just-in-Time?

Not quite. Just-in-Time is the goal of producing and delivering only what is needed when it is needed; kanban is the signalling method that makes JIT inventory work day to day. See Just-in-Time (JIT).

What is the difference between a production kanban and a withdrawal kanban?

A production kanban authorises a process to make a container of parts. A withdrawal, or conveyance, kanban authorises moving a container from a store or supermarket to the point of use.

What is e-kanban?

Electronic kanban sends replenishment signals digitally, usually when someone scans a barcode or QR code on an empty container, often creating a request or purchase order in an ERP or inventory system.

What is a three-bin kanban system?

A loop with three containers: one at the point of use, one in the store and one at the supplier being refilled. It is common for bought-in parts where the supplier takes part in the loop.

Which items should not be managed with kanban?

Items with erratic or one-off demand, very long or unreliable lead times, short shelf lives, very high unit costs or imminent design changes are usually better ordered to requirement or given a deliberate buffer.

What is the difference between kanban and a reorder point?

Both reorder when stock reaches a set level. A reorder point relies on recorded stock figures in a system; kanban makes the trigger physical or scanned at the point of use, so it works without accurate stock records.

How often should you review the number of kanbans?

Review monthly for most items, and whenever demand, lead time, supplier or container size changes. Recalculate the card count, and remove cards when a loop is consistently over-supplied.

Can small businesses use kanban inventory?

Yes. A two-bin system with simple cards is cheap and effective for consumables, packaging, spare parts and fast-selling stock, and it needs no software.

What are the main disadvantages of kanban inventory?

It needs steady demand and reliable suppliers, card counts go stale without regular review, lost cards or unofficial orders undermine it, and it suits one-off or erratic items poorly.

How is inventory kanban related to Kanban for projects?

Both use visual signals and pull. Project Kanban boards adapted the idea to knowledge work, with WIP limits playing the role of a fixed number of kanban cards.
