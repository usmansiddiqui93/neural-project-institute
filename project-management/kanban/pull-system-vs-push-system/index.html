Pull System vs Push System: Differences and Examples

The difference between a pull system and a push system is what starts the work: a push system releases work according to a forecast, plan or schedule, whether or not the next step is ready, while a pull system starts work only when there is real demand and free capacity downstream. The clearest test is whether the amount of work in progress is explicitly capped. Kanban is a pull system. This guide compares the two with tables, a manufacturing and a knowledge-work worked example, and a step-by-step route from push to pull.

Key takeaways

  • Push releases work from forecasts and schedules; pull releases work when a downstream signal shows demand and spare capacity.
  • A widely cited definition from Hopp and Spearman (2004) says a pull system is one that explicitly limits work in process. If nothing caps WIP, it is push, even if you build to order.
  • Capping WIP shortens lead times: by Little's Law, average cycle time equals average WIP divided by throughput.
  • Push still makes sense for stable demand, long supply lead times and work that benefits from batching. Most organisations use a hybrid with a push-pull boundary.
  • In knowledge work, pull means people take the next item when they finish one, controlled by WIP limits on a Kanban board, instead of a manager assigning work to full queues.

Pull system vs push system: the short answer

A push system starts work because a plan says so: a forecast, a production schedule or a manager's allocation pushes items into the process. A pull system starts work only when a downstream step or customer signals that it needs more and capacity is free, so the amount of work in progress stays capped. Push optimises for keeping everyone busy; pull optimises for finishing work quickly.

Push systemPull system
What starts workForecast, schedule or planA signal of real demand and free capacity
Work in progressNot explicitly limited; tends to growExplicitly capped
Information flowsTop down, ahead of demandUpstream, from the point of consumption
Typical toolsMRP, master production schedules, task assignmentKanban cards, WIP limits, two-bin systems, CONWIP
Main riskOverproduction, overload, long queuesShortages or delays if demand spikes or processes are unreliable

What is a push system?

In a push system, work is released into a process based on what is expected to be needed. A planning step (often software such as material requirements planning, MRP) turns a demand forecast into a schedule, and each stage produces its quota and passes the output on. Nothing in the system stops a stage producing more because the next one is already full.

Examples of push

  • A clothing manufacturer produces its autumn range in spring to a sales forecast and stores it until the season starts.
  • A bakery bakes 200 loaves every morning based on last month's average sales.
  • A factory releases work orders every Monday from the master schedule, regardless of how much is already on the shop floor.
  • A manager splits a project plan into tasks and assigns five to each person on Monday, whatever they are already working on.
  • A marketing department commissions next quarter's content in one batch at the planning meeting.

Push is not a mistake in itself. It works well when demand is stable and predictable, when supply lead times are longer than customers are willing to wait, or when large batches bring real economies of scale. Its weakness is that forecasts are always wrong to some degree, and the error shows up as excess stock, queues and overloaded people.

What is a pull system?

In a pull system, work is triggered by consumption. When a customer takes a product, or a downstream step finishes an item and has room for another, a signal goes upstream to replace or supply it. Kanban University's glossary defines a pull system as one for delivering work only when both demand exists and delivery capacity is available.

The idea came from Taiichi Ohno at Toyota. According to the Lean Enterprise Institute, he based the supermarket concept on American stores, where shelves are restocked as customers take goods, and Toyota installed its first supermarket in 1953. Cards called kanban (Japanese for signboard) travelled with parts and authorised production of exactly what had been used.

Examples of pull

  • A supermarket restocks a shelf when the gap appears, and the store reorders from the warehouse based on what was sold.
  • A two-bin system on a workbench: when the first bin of screws is empty, it is sent to stores for refilling while the second bin is used.
  • An assembly line where each station has a fixed number of kanban cards, so it can only build what the next station has used.
  • A café that makes each coffee when it is ordered, with a limit on how many orders the barista accepts at once.
  • A software team whose board allows three items in Build; a developer pulls a new item from Ready only when one leaves Build.

In knowledge work, the cards are usually virtual: a WIP limit on a board column is the pull signal. The Kanban Guide makes the link directly, noting that an effect of controlling WIP is that it should create a pull system. Learn more in WIP limits and what is Kanban.

The WIP-cap test: a clearer way to tell push from pull

Many articles say push is "forecast-driven" and pull is "demand-driven". That is a useful rule of thumb, but it breaks down quickly. Wallace Hopp and Mark Spearman, in their 2004 paper "To Pull or Not to Pull: What Is the Question?" (*Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*), proposed a sharper definition: a pull production system is one that explicitly limits the amount of work in process that can be in the system. A push system has no such limit.

This test clears up three common misconceptions:

  • "Make-to-order is pull." Not necessarily. A tailor who only makes suits to order but accepts and starts every order immediately has no WIP cap, so queues and lead times grow without limit. That behaves like push.
  • "Make-to-stock is push." Not necessarily. Toyota builds some cars without a specific customer order, but within a system of capped buffers and kanban, which is pull.
  • "MRP is push and kanban is pull." Usually true, but Hopp and Spearman note that MRP with a WIP constraint becomes a pull system. The planning tool matters less than whether releases are capped.

Pull systems still use forecasts. You need an estimate of demand to size buffers and WIP limits. The difference is that the forecast sets the capacity of the system, while actual consumption decides what happens day to day.

Worked example: sizing a kanban pull loop in a workshop

The figures in this example are illustrative. A metal workshop makes a bracket used by its own assembly cell. Assembly uses about 120 brackets a day. It takes the bracket line 1.5 days to replenish a container once it is signalled (including queueing, machining and moving). Brackets travel in containers of 30, and the team adds a 20% safety factor for variation.

A commonly used formula for the number of kanban cards is:

Number of cards = (daily demand x replenishment lead time x (1 + safety factor)) / container quantity

  1. Demand during the lead time: 120 x 1.5 = 180 brackets.
  2. Add the safety factor: 180 x 1.2 = 216 brackets.
  3. Divide by the container size: 216 / 30 = 7.2.
  4. Round up to 8 cards. The most brackets that can ever be in the loop is 8 x 30 = 240.

What the same workshop looks like under push

Under its old push schedule, the bracket line produced 700 a week (140 a day) because that was the forecast. Real demand was 600 a week. In four weeks the workshop built up 400 surplus brackets, which took up floor space and cash, and when the design changed in week five, 400 brackets had to be reworked or scrapped. With the pull loop, at most 240 brackets existed at any time and the design change affected only those.

Tip

Review the number of cards whenever demand or lead time changes noticeably. Many pull systems slowly turn back into push because the cards were sized once and never revisited; Toyota's practice of steadily reducing cards to expose problems is the opposite habit.

Worked example: from assigning work to pulling it in a design team

This example is also illustrative. A four-person in-house design team receives requests from marketing, product and sales.

Before: push

  • Every Monday the team lead assigns new requests so that each designer has about four on the go.
  • Average WIP is 16 items. The team finishes about 8 a week.
  • By Little's Law, average cycle time is 16 / 8 = 2 weeks, even though most requests take less than a day of actual design effort.
  • Designers switch between jobs all day, requesters chase constantly, and urgent items jump the queue by escalation.

After: pull

  1. The team maps its workflow onto a board: Requests, Ready (limit 4), Designing (limit 4), Review (limit 3), Done.
  2. The lead stops assigning. Instead, she runs a 20-minute replenishment twice a week with the requesting teams to agree which requests move into Ready.
  3. A designer who finishes an item pulls the top card from Ready. If Review is full, they help clear Review first.
  4. A policy allows one expedite item at a time, agreed by the lead.
  5. The team records start and finish dates for every card.

Results after six weeks

MeasurePush (before)Pull (after)
Average WIP16 items7 items
Throughput8 items a week9 items a week
Average cycle time (WIP / throughput)2 weeksabout 0.8 weeks (4 working days)
Escalations from requestersseveral a weekrare; requesters see the queue at replenishment

Why limiting WIP shortens lead time (Little's Law)

Little's Law, first proved by John Little in 1961, states that in a stable system average cycle time = average WIP / average throughput. If you keep throughput the same and halve the amount of work in progress, the average time each item spends in the system halves too.

Push systems tend to increase WIP, because work is released whether or not it can be processed, so cycle time grows. Pull systems cap WIP, so cycle time is bounded. Pull often raises throughput a little as well, because fewer items compete for attention and problems are fixed sooner.

The law describes long-run averages and assumes a stable system, where roughly as many items start as finish and items are not abandoned part-way. Use it to understand and explain your system, not to promise a date for a single item. For forecasting individual items, use the distribution of past cycle times and throughput.

When push is the better choice

Pull is not always superior. Push, or a push-heavy hybrid, often makes more sense when:

  • Demand is stable and well understood, so forecasts are accurate and stock rarely goes to waste.
  • Supply lead times are long, such as components shipped from overseas, and customers will not wait that long.
  • Demand is seasonal, so capacity must be used in quiet months to build stock for the peak.
  • Batching brings real savings, for example a long, expensive machine set-up or a bulk purchase discount.
  • Work must be committed far ahead, such as a fixed event date, a regulatory deadline or a construction sequence that needs materials on site in advance.
  • Processes are not yet reliable, so removing buffers would expose customers to every breakdown. Stabilise first, then move towards pull.

Push-pull hybrids and the push-pull boundary

Most real systems combine both. The push-pull boundary (sometimes called the decoupling point) is the place in the chain where work stops being driven by forecast and starts being driven by actual demand. Upstream of it you push; downstream you pull.

SettingPushed (to forecast)Pulled (to demand)
Build-to-order computersComponents bought and stocked to a forecastFinal assembly once a customer orders
Bespoke furnitureCommon frames and materials prepared in advanceUpholstery and finishing for each order
RestaurantIngredients bought and prepared to expected coversDishes cooked when ordered
Project portfolioAnnual plan and budget allocate funding to initiativesTeams pull the next item when they have capacity
Kanban teamOptions are gathered and refined in the backlogItems cross the commitment point and are pulled into delivery

In Kanban the boundary has a name: the commitment point. Before it, items are options that can be reordered or dropped. After it, the team has committed and pulls work at the pace its WIP limits allow. Moving the boundary further upstream increases responsiveness but needs a more reliable process; moving it downstream reduces lead time for the customer but needs more stock or more preparation. Plan replenishment in Kanban planning.

How to move from push to pull: step by step

  1. Map the flow. Draw the steps that work goes through and where it waits. A value stream map shows queues and stock between steps.
  2. Measure current WIP and lead time. Count items in progress at each step and how long items take end to end. This is your baseline.
  3. Stabilise the process. Fix the most frequent breakdowns, rework loops and missing information first. Pull exposes instability; it does not cure it.
  4. Choose the pull mechanism. Replenishment cards for standard items, sequential or CONWIP for made-to-order work, column WIP limits for teams.
  5. Size the limits. Use the card formula for stock, or start knowledge-work limits around the number of people in a stage. Make them slightly tight so queues become visible.
  6. Stop releasing work by schedule. This is the hard step. Planners and managers must hold new work until a signal says there is room.
  7. Agree what happens when a limit is hit. People swarm on the blocked step, help downstream, or improve the process; they do not start something new.
  8. Review and adjust. Track WIP, throughput and cycle time weekly, reduce limits gradually, and resize buffers when demand changes.

Pull systems in Agile and project work

Pull is the core idea behind Kanban and appears throughout Agile practice.

  • Kanban: WIP limits on the board turn every free slot into a pull signal. Replenishment meetings decide what is ready to be pulled; the team decides when. See Kanban vs Agile for how this fits Agile values.
  • Scrum: the team pulls work into the Sprint during Sprint Planning, choosing how much it can complete towards the Sprint Goal. Within the Sprint, Developers typically pull tasks rather than having them assigned.
  • Resource management: a push-style approach assigns people to several projects at once to keep utilisation high. A pull-style approach limits the number of active projects and starts a new one only when capacity frees up, which shortens delivery across the portfolio. See capacity planning, resource allocation and managing multiple projects.
  • Flow measurement: a cumulative flow diagram shows whether work is being pushed in faster than it leaves, and flow efficiency shows how much of the lead time is waiting.

For the broader Lean thinking behind this, read our guide to Lean project management.

Common mistakes when introducing pull

  • Setting WIP limits but still releasing work on a schedule. The board shows limits, but new work keeps arriving in people's queues by email. Pull has to apply at the point where work is released.
  • Removing buffers before the process is stable. Breakdowns that buffers used to hide now stop customers being served, and pull gets the blame.
  • Treating idle time as waste. Someone waiting for a slot should help clear a bottleneck or improve the process, not start a new item.
  • Sizing cards or limits once. Demand, lead times and team size change; the limits must change with them.
  • Expediting everything. If most items are "urgent", the expedite lane becomes a push channel. Limit it to one item at a time.
  • Ignoring the upstream side. Pull at team level does not help if the portfolio keeps approving more projects than teams can start.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between a push system and a pull system?

What starts the work. A push system releases work according to a forecast or schedule; a pull system releases work only when a downstream signal shows demand and spare capacity. As a result, pull systems cap work in progress and push systems do not.

Is Kanban a push or pull system?

Kanban is a pull system. A fixed number of cards, or a WIP limit on a board column, means new work can only start when an item has been used or finished. Kanban University defines a pull system as delivering work only when demand exists and capacity is available.

Is MRP push or pull?

Traditional MRP is a push system: it releases orders from a master schedule regardless of how much work is already in the system. Hopp and Spearman point out that MRP combined with an explicit WIP limit behaves as a pull system.

What is an example of a pull system?

A supermarket restocking a shelf when it empties, a two-bin system for workshop consumables, or a team that pulls the next request from a Ready column only when an In progress slot is free.

What is an example of a push system?

A clothing brand producing a whole season's stock to a sales forecast, a factory releasing work orders every week from a fixed schedule, or a manager assigning tasks to people regardless of their current workload.

Which is better, push or pull?

Neither in every case. Pull usually gives shorter lead times, less stock and less overload, especially when demand is uncertain. Push can be better for stable or seasonal demand, long supply lead times and work that benefits from large batches. Most organisations combine both.

Is make-to-order the same as a pull system?

No. Make-to-order describes when production starts relative to the customer order. Pull describes whether work in progress is capped. A make-to-order business that starts every order at once, with no limit, has the queue problems of a push system.

What is a push-pull system?

A hybrid in which earlier stages are pushed to a forecast and later stages are pulled by actual demand. The point where one changes to the other is the push-pull boundary, or decoupling point. Build-to-order assembly from stocked components is a common example.

Is Scrum a push or pull system?

Scrum is a pull system at Sprint level: the team pulls the amount of work it can complete into the Sprint at Sprint Planning. Within the Sprint, Developers usually pull tasks themselves rather than having them assigned.

What are the disadvantages of a pull system?

It needs reliable processes and suppliers, can struggle with sudden demand spikes, requires discipline to respect limits, and its buffers and limits must be sized and reviewed regularly.

What is CONWIP?

CONWIP (constant work in progress) is a pull system in which a fixed number of cards circulates for a whole production line rather than for each part. When a job finishes, its card returns and releases the next most urgent job. It suits make-to-order and mixed products.

How do WIP limits create a pull system?

When a column or stage is at its limit, nothing new can enter until an item leaves. The free slot acts as the signal to pull the next item, so work is released by downstream capacity rather than pushed in by a plan.

How do you calculate the number of kanban cards?

A common formula is daily demand multiplied by replenishment lead time and by (1 plus a safety factor), divided by the container quantity, rounded up. Review the result whenever demand or lead time changes.
