Flow Efficiency: How Much of Lead Time Is Real Work?

Flow efficiency measures how much of the time an item spends in your process is spent actively working on it, rather than waiting. It often reveals that the biggest delays come from queues, not from slow work.

By Neural Project InstitutePublished 5 October 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • Flow efficiency is active time divided by total time in process.
  • Most delay usually comes from waiting, not working.
  • Lower WIP and fewer handoffs improve it.
Table of contents

Formula

Flow efficiency = active work time ÷ total time in process × 100%

Tip

Example: an item spends 10 days in progress but is actively worked on for 2 of them. Flow efficiency is 2 ÷ 10 = 20%.

Why it is often low

In many knowledge-work processes, items spend most of their time waiting: for review, for approval, for another team or for someone to have capacity. Flow efficiency well below 50% is common. That's good news, because reducing waiting is often easier than making people work faster.

How to measure it

  • Split board columns into "doing" and "done/waiting" sub-columns.
  • Mark blocked time on cards.
  • Compare time in active columns with total cycle time.
  • Sample a set of recent items if your tool can't calculate it automatically.

How to improve flow efficiency

  • Lower WIP limits so items wait less in queues.
  • Reduce handoffs between people and teams.
  • Agree fast-turnaround policies for reviews and approvals.
  • Remove recurring blockers; see blocker clustering.
  • Map the end-to-end process with value stream mapping.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good flow efficiency?

There is no universal target. Measure your baseline and aim to improve it; many teams find large gains simply by reducing waiting.

Is flow efficiency the same as resource utilisation?

No. Utilisation measures how busy people are; flow efficiency measures how continuously work moves. High utilisation often lowers flow efficiency.
