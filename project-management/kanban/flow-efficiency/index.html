Flow Efficiency: Formula, Examples and How to Improve It

Flow efficiency is the percentage of an item's total time in your process that is spent actively working on it, rather than waiting in queues, for approvals or behind blockers. When teams measure it for the first time, they usually find that most of the delay comes from waiting, not from slow work, which means the quickest gains come from managing queues rather than asking people to work harder. This guide gives you the formula, two worked calculations, a step-by-step way to measure it on a Kanban board and the practical levers that raise it.

Key takeaways

  • Flow efficiency = active work time ÷ total time in process × 100%. If an item takes 20 days and someone works on it for 6 of them, flow efficiency is 30%.
  • It measures how continuously work moves, not how busy people are. Pushing resource utilisation towards 100% usually lowers flow efficiency because queues grow.
  • To measure it, split your board into active and waiting columns, agree what counts as active, track blocked time and calculate it from card timestamps.
  • There is no validated industry benchmark. Commonly quoted ranges are anecdotal, so set your own baseline and track the trend and the spread.
  • The biggest levers are lower WIP limits, faster pick-up of reviews and approvals, fewer handoffs, smaller items and removing recurring blockers.
  • Use flow efficiency to find where work waits and improve the system, never to rate individuals or compare unlike teams.

What is flow efficiency?

Flow efficiency is a flow metric that compares the time a work item is actively being worked on with the total time it spends in the process from start to finish. It is expressed as a percentage. A low figure means items spend most of their life waiting in queues, for decisions or behind blockers rather than being progressed.

Picture it by watching the work item, not the people. Follow one feature, contract or design request from start to done and note when anyone is actually touching it. Everything else is waiting.

The idea comes from Lean thinking, where the same ratio is often called the value-added ratio or, in Lean Six Sigma, process cycle efficiency. Niklas Modig and Pär Åhlström's book *This Is Lean: Resolving the Efficiency Paradox* (2012) made the contrast between flow efficiency and resource efficiency widely known, and Kanban practitioners adopted flow efficiency as a way to show how much time work spends in queues.

In Kanban it is a supporting metric rather than a core one. The Kanban Guide (2025 edition) names four measures every Kanban system should track: work in progress, throughput, work item age and cycle time. Flow efficiency sits alongside them, as our guide to Kanban metrics explains, and is most useful for diagnosing where time is lost.

The flow efficiency formula

The calculation is simple:

Flow efficiency = active work time ÷ total time in process × 100%

Total time in process is the sum of active time and everything that is not active: waiting time and blocked time. So you can also write it as active time ÷ (active time + wait time + blocked time).

TermMeaningExamples
Active time (touch time)Someone is working on the itemWriting, designing, coding, testing, reviewing
Wait timeExpected time in a queueReady for review, awaiting a scheduled release
Blocked timeUnexpected time the item cannot progressWaiting for client content or an absent approver
Total time in processElapsed time from agreed start to agreed endUsually cycle time; sometimes lead time

Cycle time or lead time as the denominator?

Most Kanban teams use cycle time (start to finish), the part of the process the team controls. Value stream analyses often use lead time (request to delivery), which adds backlog waiting, so the result is lower and closer to the customer's experience. Both are valid; choose one and stay consistent. Our guide to cycle time vs lead time explains where each one starts and stops.

Item-level vs aggregate flow efficiency

You can calculate flow efficiency for a single item, or for a set of items completed in a period. For a set, there are two methods:

  • Aggregate: add up all the active time and divide by all the total time. This weights long-running items more heavily, which reflects where most of the elapsed time actually went.
  • Average of percentages: calculate each item's percentage and take the mean. This treats every item equally, so a few long, slow items have less influence.

The two can differ noticeably, as the team example below shows. The aggregate is generally the better guide to the system; whichever you choose, use it every time.

Worked example: calculating flow efficiency for one item

This illustrative example follows one card, a landing page update, across a marketing team's board. The request sat in the backlog for 5 working days before anyone started it. The table shows how the next 20 working days were spent.

Stage on the boardWorking daysType
Analyse: in progress1Active
Analysed, waiting for design2Wait
Design: in progress2Active
Design: blocked, waiting for brand assets3Blocked
Designed, waiting for build2Wait
Build: in progress2Active
Ready for review4Wait
Review: in progress1Active
Approved, waiting for scheduled release3Wait
Total206 active, 11 waiting, 3 blocked

Work through the calculation step by step:

  1. Add up the active days: 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 6 days.
  2. Add up all days from start to finish to get the cycle time: 20 days.
  3. Divide active time by cycle time: 6 ÷ 20 = 0.30, so flow efficiency is 30% on a cycle-time basis.
  4. For lead time, add the 5 days the request waited in the backlog: 5 + 20 = 25 days. Flow efficiency on a lead-time basis is 6 ÷ 25 = 24%.
  5. Rank the waits from longest to shortest: Ready for review (4 days), Design blocked (3), waiting for release (3), then two 2-day handoffs.

Step 5 matters more than the percentage. If everyone worked twice as fast, the item would still take 17 days (3 active plus 14 waiting or blocked). Cutting the review queue to one day and the release wait to zero would bring cycle time to 13 days with no extra effort, lifting flow efficiency to about 46%.

Tip

Working days or calendar days? Either works, as long as you use the same unit for active and total time. Working days are easier for teams to reason about; calendar days match what customers experience.

Worked example: flow efficiency for a team

Now take five items the same team finished in a month (illustrative figures, cycle-time basis):

ItemActive daysCycle time (days)Item flow efficiency
A21020%
B61250%
C1205%
D3837.5%
E43013.3%
Total1680
  • Aggregate flow efficiency: 16 ÷ 80 = 20%.
  • Average of item percentages: (20 + 50 + 5 + 37.5 + 13.3) ÷ 5 = 25.2%.

The aggregate is lower because items C and E took 50 of the 80 days and were mostly waiting. That is the discussion to have: why did C, one day of work, take four weeks? Usually the answer is a long queue, a dependency on another team or a blocker nobody owned. The spread of individual values, not one summary number, is what turns flow efficiency into action.

Flow efficiency vs resource efficiency

Resource efficiency (often called utilisation) measures how much of the available time people or machines are busy. Flow efficiency measures how much of the work item's time is spent being progressed. The two pull in opposite directions. If every person must be busy all the time, work has to queue in front of them so that nobody runs out of something to do, and queued work is waiting work.

Modig and Åhlström describe this as the efficiency paradox and summarise the trade-off in an efficiency matrix:

StateResource efficiencyFlow efficiencyWhat it looks like
Efficient islandsHighLowEvery team is busy, but items wait between them and take a long time overall
Efficient oceanLowerHighSome spare capacity is kept so work moves quickly when it arrives
WastelandLowLowPeople are not busy and work still waits, often because of poor coordination
Perfect stateHighHighOnly reachable with highly predictable demand and work, so rarely achievable in practice

Queueing theory explains why. In a simple single-server queueing model with random arrivals and work times, the average time an item spends waiting relative to the time it takes to do grows sharply as utilisation rises. The table below uses that simplified model to show the effect at one step:

Utilisation of the person or stepAverage wait as a multiple of work timeFlow efficiency at that step
50%1×50%
70%About 2.3×30%
80%4×20%
90%9×10%
95%19×5%

Real processes are messier, but the shape holds. That is why the theory of constraints deliberately keeps spare capacity at non-bottleneck steps, and why a pull system with WIP limits usually beats pushing work in as fast as it arrives. Flow efficiency vs cycle time, lead time and other metrics

Flow efficiency explains other flow metrics rather than replacing them. Use it alongside them:

MetricQuestion it answersLink to flow efficiency
Cycle timeHow long does an item take once started?Usually the denominator
Lead timeHow long does the customer wait?Alternative denominator, including backlog waiting
ThroughputHow much do we deliver?Better flow usually shortens cycle time rather than raising throughput
Work in progress (WIP)Are we overloaded?Too much WIP is the most common cause of low flow efficiency
Work item ageWhat is stuck right now?The live warning; flow efficiency is the after-the-fact view

The link to cycle time comes through Little's Law: average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput. If WIP falls while throughput holds, cycle time falls, and because the active time per item has not changed, flow efficiency rises. Our guides to throughput and the WIP aging chart show how to read the related measures.

What is a good flow efficiency?

There is no validated industry benchmark, so be wary of any article that gives one as fact. Figures are widely quoted in the Kanban community, such as single-digit percentages being common in knowledge work and around 40% being very good, but these are usually attributed to practitioners' experience (often to Kanban Method pioneer David J. Anderson) rather than to published studies.

One of the few published data sets comes from an Agile coach at the online retailer ASOS, who reported in 2023 that across 63 teams in his organisation, team-level flow efficiency averaged 35%, with values from 9% to 68%. That is one organisation measured one way, not a benchmark, but it shows how widely results vary with how you define active and waiting states.

In practice, a useful target has three parts:

  1. A baseline, measured over a few weeks of completed items with a clearly written definition of active time.
  2. A direction, such as raising aggregate flow efficiency by cutting the two longest waits, rather than a fixed percentage.
  3. A check on the spread, so the slowest items, not just the average, are getting better.
Tip

If your first measurement comes out above 60% or 70%, check your board before celebrating. A process with few or no waiting columns will look highly efficient simply because waiting is hidden inside active columns.

How to measure flow efficiency step by step

You can measure flow efficiency with a digital board or a physical one. These steps work for both:

  1. Agree the boundaries. Decide where the clock starts (usually the first in-progress column) and stops (done or delivered), and whether you use cycle time or lead time.
  2. Split each stage into doing and done. A "done" or "ready for next" column makes finished-but-waiting work visible as waiting.
  3. Write a policy for what counts as active, for example "someone is working on it today", and put it on the board.
  4. Track blocked time separately with a blocker flag. Blocked time is non-active even if the card sits in a doing column.
  5. Capture timestamps. Digital tools record column moves; on a physical board, date the card each time it moves.
  6. Calculate per item, then for the period, using the aggregate method.
  7. Find the longest waits. List the stages with the most waiting time and pick one to improve.
  8. Review regularly, every few weeks, alongside cycle time and throughput.

Measuring without a digital tool

If your tool cannot calculate flow efficiency, sample instead. Take 15 to 30 recently finished items, reconstruct their dates from cards, comments or emails, and ask the people involved roughly how many days of hands-on work each needed. Approximate figures are enough to find the biggest queues. A lighter option is to add a dot to a card at the end of each day it was actively worked on: dots ÷ days in progress gives a quick figure.

Flow efficiency in a value stream map

A value stream map records process time and wait time for every step from request to delivery, so total process time ÷ total lead time gives flow efficiency across teams and departments. You can sketch the steps first with our free workflow template.

Why flow efficiency is often low

Low flow efficiency is normal in knowledge work, and the causes are usually structural rather than individual. The table lists the common ones:

CauseWhat you see on the boardTypical fix
Too much work in progressMany cards in every column; people switching between itemsLower WIP limits; stop starting, start finishing
Queues before reviews and approvals"Ready for review" or "Awaiting sign-off" columns fullReview pick-up policies; more reviewers; smaller review batches
Handoffs between specialistsCards wait in a done column for the next personCross-skilling; pairing; fewer stages
Dependencies on other teamsCards blocked waiting for another team's workAgree service levels with the other team; restructure ownership
Batched releasesFinished items pile up before a scheduled releaseRelease more often; automate deployment
Recurring blockersSame blocker reasons appear again and againAnalyse and remove root causes

Most of these are policy choices, and changing them is usually cheaper than hiring or asking people to work faster.

How to improve flow efficiency

Start with the longest waits you found when measuring, then work through these levers in roughly this order.

1. Limit work in progress

With fewer items in the system, each spends less time queued behind others. Lower WIP limits a step at a time and watch cycle time and blocked items.

2. Shrink the queues before reviews and approvals

These are often the largest waits. Agree explicit policies, such as "pick up reviews before starting new work" or "approvals within one working day", give the queue its own WIP limit, and delegate approval of low-risk items.

3. Reduce handoffs and dependencies

Every handoff creates a queue. Bring the needed skills into the team, pair specialists and merge stages one person could carry through. For other teams, agree turnaround times and track breaches as blockers.

4. Make work items smaller

Small items move faster, get reviewed sooner and are blocked less often. Split work into slices that can each be finished on their own; our guide to user stories shows how to slice work for software and non-software teams.

5. Remove recurring blockers

Record why items are blocked, group the reasons and tackle the most frequent or most costly cause first. Blocker clustering is a simple workshop technique for this, and a root cause analysis template helps you dig into the biggest cluster.

6. Swarm on the oldest items

When an item is ageing or blocked, have people with spare capacity help finish it rather than start something new. Swarming trades some individual utilisation for much faster flow, which is exactly the trade flow efficiency rewards.

7. Pace intake to the bottleneck

Work always queues in front of the slowest stage, so release new work at the pace it can absorb and improve it first. The cumulative flow diagram shows this as a band that keeps widening.

Worked example: improving a team's flow efficiency

This illustrative example follows a six-person product team that finishes about 6 items a week. When they start measuring, there are about 24 items in progress and each item needs around 3 days of active work.

  1. Baseline. Using Little's Law, average cycle time = 24 ÷ 6 = 4 weeks, or 20 working days. Flow efficiency = 3 ÷ 20 = 15%.
  2. Diagnosis. Measuring waiting by column shows the longest queue is "Ready for review" (about 6 days on average), followed by items blocked waiting for another team's data feed.
  3. Change 1: WIP limits. The team sets WIP limits that bring total WIP down to about 12 items over three weeks, by finishing work before pulling more.
  4. Change 2: review policy. Developers agree to pick up review requests before starting anything new, and the review column gets a WIP limit of 3.
  5. Change 3: dependency. The team agrees a two-day turnaround with the data team and flags anything older as blocked in the daily meeting.
  6. Result. Throughput stays at about 6 items a week, so cycle time = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 weeks, or 10 working days. Active time is still about 3 days, so flow efficiency = 3 ÷ 10 = 30%.
MeasureBeforeAfter
Work in progressAbout 24 itemsAbout 12 items
ThroughputAbout 6 items/weekAbout 6 items/week
Average cycle time4 weeks (20 working days)2 weeks (10 working days)
Active time per itemAbout 3 daysAbout 3 days
Flow efficiency15%30%

Nobody worked faster. The team started less, so items waited less and customers got their work in half the time.

Limitations of flow efficiency

Flow efficiency is a useful diagnostic, but it has real weaknesses that you should be honest about when you report it:

  • It depends on your board. A workflow with no waiting columns always shows 100%. The figure measures how you model the process as much as the process itself.
  • Active time is fuzzy, and items often sit blocked in a doing column, inflating active time unless blockers are flagged.
  • Averages hide the story. A single percentage can mask the handful of very slow items that matter most.
  • It does not compare well across teams. Different definitions, work types and board designs make cross-team comparisons misleading.
  • It can be gamed. Removing waiting columns, moving cards late or splitting items artificially can all raise the number without improving delivery.

Treat flow efficiency as a conversation starter about where work waits, confirm with cycle time and blocker data, and never make it an individual target.

Common flow efficiency mistakes

  • Treating it as a productivity measure for people. It measures the system. Using it to judge individuals encourages people to look busy, which lowers flow.
  • Chasing utilisation at the same time. You cannot keep everyone 100% busy and have work flow quickly. Accept some slack.
  • Changing definitions mid-stream, which breaks the trend.
  • Fixing the work instead of the waits. Training people to work faster rarely helps when most of the time is queueing.
  • Comparing teams on a league table. Use each team's own trend instead.

Tools and templates for tracking flow efficiency

Any board that records when cards change column can supply the data. Look for separate active and waiting stages, blocker flags and time-in-status reporting.

  • Wrike (our top-rated tool) builds boards on custom statuses, so you can create separate doing and waiting stages for each step, and its dashboards show where work is piling up across projects. See our Wrike review.
  • Jira is popular with software teams and includes cumulative flow diagrams and control charts for cycle time, which help you spot the stages where items wait. See our Jira review.
  • Dedicated Kanban analytics tools, such as Businessmap, include ready-made flow efficiency charts if you want the calculation done for you.

Frequently asked questions

What is flow efficiency?

Flow efficiency is the share of an item's total time in process that is spent actively working on it, rather than waiting in queues, for approvals or behind blockers. It is expressed as a percentage.

How do you calculate flow efficiency?

Divide active work time by total time in process and multiply by 100. For example, 6 active days in a 20-day cycle time gives 6 ÷ 20 = 30%.

What is a good flow efficiency?

There is no validated benchmark. Figures such as 40% being very good are widely quoted but anecdotal. Measure your own baseline, then aim to improve the trend and reduce the slowest items.

Is flow efficiency the same as resource utilisation?

No. Utilisation measures how busy people are; flow efficiency measures how continuously work moves. Pushing utilisation close to 100% usually lowers flow efficiency because queues grow.

What is the difference between flow efficiency and cycle time?

Cycle time is the total elapsed time to finish an item once started. Flow efficiency shows what proportion of that time was active work, so it explains why cycle time is long.

Should blocked time count as active time?

No. Blocked time is time the item cannot progress, so count it as non-active, even if the card sits in a doing column, and record the blocker's cause.

Is flow efficiency an official Kanban metric?

It is widely used by Kanban practitioners, but the Kanban Guide's four required measures are WIP, throughput, work item age and cycle time. Flow efficiency is a supporting, diagnostic metric.

What is the difference between flow efficiency and process cycle efficiency?

They measure the same ratio of value-adding or active time to total time. Process cycle efficiency is the Lean Six Sigma name; flow efficiency is the term most Kanban teams use.

How do you improve flow efficiency?

Lower WIP, shrink review and approval queues, reduce handoffs and dependencies, split work into smaller items, remove recurring blockers and swarm on ageing items. Start with the longest wait.

Can you compare flow efficiency between teams?

Not reliably. Teams define active and waiting states differently and do different work. Compare each team with its own baseline instead.

Does value stream mapping measure flow efficiency?

Yes. A value stream map records process time and wait time at each step, and total process time divided by total lead time gives flow efficiency across the whole value stream.
