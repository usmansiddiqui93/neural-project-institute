Kanban Principles and Practices: A Practical Guide

Kanban principles are the guiding rules of the Kanban Method: start with what you do now, agree to pursue evolutionary change, respect current roles and titles, and encourage leadership at every level. Alongside them sit three service delivery principles and six general practices that turn those ideas into daily habits. This guide explains each one, shows how the lists differ between Kanban University and the Kanban Guide, and walks through worked examples you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • Kanban University's principles page lists four foundational principles; its newer Official Guide groups three change management principles with three service delivery principles.
  • The six general practices are visualise, limit WIP, manage flow, make policies explicit, implement feedback loops, and improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally.
  • The Kanban Guide (2025.5) condenses Kanban into three practices and says improvements need not be small or incremental, a real difference from the Kanban Method.
  • Principles explain why you change gently; practices are what you actually do at the board each day.
  • WIP limits and explicit policies do most of the work: they expose bottlenecks and make improvement a matter of evidence, not opinion.
  • STATIK gives a step-by-step way to apply the principles when you introduce Kanban to a team.

What are Kanban principles?

Kanban principles are the beliefs that shape how the Kanban Method introduces and evolves change. They say you begin with your current process, improve it through small agreed steps, keep existing roles and titles, and let anyone lead an improvement. The six Kanban practices are the concrete activities, such as limiting work in progress, that put those principles into action.

The principles are the constitution; the practices are the working rules. A team can follow the practices mechanically and still fail if it ignores the principles, for example by forcing a new board on people who were never asked.

Here is the whole picture on one table. Each item is explained in detail below.

Service delivery principlesUnderstand and focus on customer needs and expectations; manage the work, let people self-organise around it; regularly review the network of services and its policiesKanban University Official Guide to the Kanban Method
General practicesVisualise; limit WIP; manage flow; make policies explicit; implement feedback loops; improve collaboratively, evolve experimentallyKanban University

If you are new to Kanban itself, start with our overview of what Kanban is, which covers its history, the board and the core flow metrics. This page goes deeper into the principles and practices.

Which list is right? 4 principles, 6 principles or 3 practices

Different websites quote different numbers, and each is correct for the source it follows.

SourcePrinciplesPracticesWhat to know
Kanban University principles page (first published 2014)4 foundational principles6 general practicesThe list most search results and quiz questions use
David J. Anderson, "Revisiting the Principles and General Practices" (2021) and the Official Guide to the Kanban Method (2022)3 change management + 3 service delivery6 general practicesRespect for roles and titles is folded into "start with what you do now"
The Kanban Guide 2025.5 (Coleman and Vacanti, May 2025)No numbered principles3 practices plus a Definition of Workflow and 4 flow metricsA minimal, flow-focused definition maintained separately from Kanban University

Small wording differences also appear. The 2014 page says "agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change", while the Official Guide says "agree to pursue improvement through evolutionary change". The meaning is the same: the team agrees, in advance, to change gradually rather than through a big redesign.

Tip

Studying for an exam? Check which body of knowledge it follows. Kanban University courses use the change management and service delivery principles with six general practices; ProKanban.org's courses use the Kanban Guide's three practices. Our own advice in this guide draws on both.

The four foundational Kanban principles

These principles describe how Kanban changes an organisation: from the inside, with consent, one step at a time. Kanban University's principles page lists them in this order.

1. Start with what you do now

Kanban is applied to the process you already have, not to an ideal process drawn on a whiteboard. You map how work really flows today, including the awkward parts such as waiting for sign-off, and put it on a board. The method does not prescribe a process of its own.

  • How to apply it: interview the people doing the work, follow three or four recent items from request to delivery, and draw the states they actually passed through.
  • Example: a finance team believes invoices go Received, Approved, Paid. Tracing ten invoices shows a hidden "Waiting for purchase order" state where most of them sat for days. That state becomes a column.
  • Warning sign: the board has columns nobody recognises, or people keep a private list because the board does not match reality.

2. Agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change

Everyone affected agrees up front that the process will improve through a series of small changes, each kept or reversed on the evidence. Agreement matters because Kanban relies on the team owning the change; without it, the initiative has no support when the first WIP limit feels uncomfortable.

  • How to apply it: say explicitly at kick-off that nothing is final, that each change will be reviewed, and that any change can be rolled back.
  • Example: instead of reorganising a support desk into tiers, the team first adds a single policy, "blocked tickets are raised at the daily meeting", and reviews the effect after a month.
  • Warning sign: a manager announces a new workflow, new tool and new roles in the same week.

3. Respect the current process, roles, responsibilities and titles

Kanban does not start by changing who does what. Keeping existing roles reduces fear, which Kanban University describes as a way to build broader support for change. If roles do need to change later, the evidence from the board will show why, and the team can agree it.

  • How to apply it: keep job titles, reporting lines and approval responsibilities as they are on day one; put them on the board as states or policies if they affect flow.
  • Example: a legal reviewer is a bottleneck. Rather than removing the review, the team limits how much reaches it at once and agrees which contract types genuinely need it.
  • Warning sign: the first conversation about Kanban is about new roles.

4. Encourage acts of leadership at all levels

Improvement ideas often come from the people closest to the work. Kanban asks for an environment where anyone, from a new starter to a director, can spot a problem and propose a well-reasoned change.

  • How to apply it: keep a visible improvement column or list, and let whoever raises an idea run the experiment with the team's agreement.
  • Example: a junior developer notices that most blocked cards wait on one shared test environment, gathers a month of blocker data and proposes a booking policy.
  • Warning sign: only the team lead changes the board or its policies.

These four principles explain why Kanban meets less resistance than many transformations. For how they fit a wider organisational change, read our guide to Agile transformation.

The service delivery principles

Kanban University adds three principles that treat a team's output as a service delivered to customers.

Understand and focus on customer needs and expectations

A service is judged by its customers. David J. Anderson describes this as being "fit for purpose" in the customer's eyes, and suggests using customers' own criteria, such as how quickly and how predictably they get what they asked for, as the measures you improve.

Example: an internal IT team discovers that requesters care less about the average fix time than about knowing when a fix will arrive. The team starts publishing a service level expectation, such as 85% of standard requests within five working days.

Manage the work; let people self-organise around it

Work items can be counted, sized, limited and tracked; people's time and effort are much harder to predict in knowledge work. Kanban therefore manages the flow of work and trusts the team to organise itself around it. Managers ask "what is blocked?" rather than "what is everyone doing?".

Example: the daily meeting walks the board from right to left, card by card, instead of going round the room for individual status updates. Our guide to Kanban ceremonies gives an agenda.

Regularly review the network of services and its policies

Most teams depend on others, and others depend on them. Policies, such as classes of service and WIP limits, determine how the whole network behaves, so they should be reviewed regularly and improved to get better outcomes for customers and the business.

Example: a product team and a data team find that every release waits on the same data migration step. A joint monthly review agrees a shared policy for scheduling migrations and an explicit limit on how many can run at once.

The three Kanban agendas and nine values

Kanban Method writers also describe three agendas, the purposes an organisation might adopt Kanban for, each linked to three values. Mike Burrows set these out in a 2014 series of InfoQ articles, and they appear in Kanban University training. They are useful for explaining why a team is adopting Kanban, but they are less often quoted than the principles and practices.

AgendaWhat it is aboutAssociated values
SustainabilityRelieving overburdened individuals and teams by balancing demand with capabilityTransparency, balance, collaboration
Service orientationLooking outward at customers and the flow of services that meet their needsCustomer focus, flow, leadership
SurvivabilityBuilding an adaptable organisation that can keep evolving as its environment changesUnderstanding, agreement, respect

Most teams begin on the sustainability agenda ("we are overloaded") and move to service orientation as they publish expectations to customers.

The six general practices of the Kanban Method

The practices are where principles become habits. They are listed in roughly the order teams adopt them, but they reinforce each other: visualisation shows where to limit WIP, WIP limits make flow problems visible, and explicit policies make improvement possible.

1. Visualise the work, workflow and business risks

Make invisible work visible. Every item is a card, every state is a column, and risks are shown with colours, markers or swimlanes. Kanban University's wording includes business risks, which many teams forget: a card's type, due date or cost of delay should be visible at a glance.

  1. List every type of work the team does, including unplanned and internal work.
  2. Draw the states from your traced items as columns, splitting busy states into "doing" and "done" where work waits.
  3. Put every current item on a Kanban card with its start date.
  4. Add risk markers and use swimlanes only where they help a decision, such as an expedite lane.

2. Limit work in progress

A WIP limit caps how many items can be in a state, a group of states or the whole system. It creates a pull system: new work starts only when capacity frees up. Kanban University calls the limits more important than the exact mechanism you use to pull.

Worked example: setting first WIP limits. A six-person software team has one analyst, four developers and one tester. Today the board shows 3 items in Analysis, 9 in Development and 6 waiting for test.

StatePeopleItems nowStarting WIP limitReasoning
Analysis132One active plus one ready to hand over
Development495About one item per developer plus one for pairing or a blocked item
Ready for test (queue)-63A small buffer in front of the constraint
Test1-2The tester is the constraint; protect their focus

With Ready for test full, developers stop starting new items and help test or automate checks. This is the Theory of Constraints idea of subordinating everything to the bottleneck; see our guide to the Theory of Constraints.

Why it works, in numbers. Little's Law says average cycle time is roughly average WIP divided by average throughput in a stable system. If the team finishes 3 items a week and holds 15 in progress, the average item takes about 15 ÷ 3 = 5 weeks. Holding 8 in progress at the same throughput points to about 8 ÷ 3 ≈ 2.7 weeks. Nobody works harder; items simply wait less.

3. Manage flow

Managing flow means watching how work moves and acting when it does not: clearing blockers, finishing ageing items and smoothing queues. The aim, in Kanban University's words, is work that flows fast, smoothly and predictably.

  1. Each day, look for the oldest items in progress and the items marked blocked.
  2. Ask what it would take to move each one, starting from the right of the board.
  3. Swarm on items close to breaching your expectation; see Agile swarming.
  4. Record start and finish dates so you can measure cycle time and throughput.
  5. Watch a WIP aging chart and a cumulative flow diagram for growing queues.

Example: a card has been in Review for six days while most items clear Review in two. At the daily meeting the team learns the reviewer is waiting for a test account. One person requests it immediately; the card is done the next day, and a policy is added for requesting accounts at the commitment point.

4. Make policies explicit

Policies are the rules the team works by. Written down and visible, they make discussions about improvement rational and evidence-based instead of emotional and anecdotal, which is how Kanban University puts it. The Official Guide gives pull criteria, WIP limits and classes of service as typical examples.

PolicyExample wordingWhy it helps
Entry to Ready (commitment point)Has a named requester, acceptance criteria and a size of small or mediumStops vague work entering the system
Blocked itemsTag red, add the reason and date; raise at the next daily meetingMakes blockers visible and measurable
Expedite class of serviceOne card at a time; may exceed WIP limits; used only for production incidentsLets urgent work through without wrecking flow
Exit from DoneMeets the definition of done and the requester has been toldAgrees what finished means

Keep policies short, put them on the board next to the column they govern, and change them only by agreement. Classes of service and replenishment rules are covered in our guide to Kanban planning.

5. Implement feedback loops

Feedback loops are regular meetings and reviews, often called cadences, where the team compares what it expected with what happened and adjusts. The Official Guide to the Kanban Method suggests these team-level cadences as a starting point:

CadenceSuggested frequencyPractice it mainly serves
Team Kanban meetingDailyManage flow: blockers, ageing items, what can we finish today?
Internal team replenishment meetingWeekly or as neededLimit WIP and make policies explicit: what enters Ready, and why
Team retrospectiveEvery two weeks or monthlyImprove collaboratively: review data and agree experiments

Service-oriented cadences, such as service delivery, operations, risk and strategy reviews, are added when several teams or services are involved. Reuse meetings you already hold where you can; that respects the current process.

6. Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally

Change is made together and treated as an experiment. Kanban University suggests using models, such as the Theory of Constraints, the Theory of Profound Knowledge and the Lean Economic Model, to predict the effect of a change, then comparing the prediction with what actually happens. Run safe-to-fail experiments, one at a time.

Example experiment card:

  • Observation: the 85th percentile cycle time is 12 days, and cards wait an average of three days in Ready for review.
  • Hypothesis: if we limit Review to 2 and pair on reviews, the 85th percentile will fall to about 9 days within six weeks, with throughput unchanged.
  • Measure: cycle time percentiles and weekly throughput from the board.
  • Review date and rollback: retrospective in six weeks; if throughput drops by more than one item a week, revert.

Data from blocker clustering and flow efficiency are good sources of experiments.

The Kanban Guide's three practices compared

The Kanban Guide, written by John Coleman and Daniel Vacanti, is a separate, shorter definition. Its 2025.5 edition (May 2025) defines Kanban as a strategy for optimising the flow of value through a process and describes three practices: defining and visualising a workflow, actively managing items in a workflow, and improving a workflow. Each team records the first in a Definition of Workflow, which must cover work items, start and finish points, states, how WIP is controlled, explicit policies and a service level expectation.

Kanban Guide practiceClosest Kanban Method practicesWhat the Kanban Guide adds
Defining and visualising a workflowVisualise; make policies explicitA written Definition of Workflow with an SLE
Actively managing items in a workflowLimit WIP; manage flowExplicit focus on controlling WIP, preventing unnecessary ageing and unblocking work
Improving a workflowImplement feedback loops; improve collaboratively, evolve experimentallyImprovement can happen just-in-time, with no required meetings

Two differences are worth knowing. First, the Kanban Guide has no change management principles and no required roles or meetings. Second, it says improvements need not be small or incremental, whereas the Kanban Method asks teams to agree to evolutionary change. In our experience, small changes are still the safer default, but the Kanban Guide's view is a fair reminder that an obviously broken policy can be fixed immediately.

The Kanban Guide also requires four flow metrics: WIP, throughput, work item age and cycle time. Our Kanban metrics guide shows how to calculate and chart them.

How to apply Kanban principles step by step with STATIK

Kanban University recommends STATIK, the Systems Thinking Approach to Introducing Kanban, for designing a Kanban system that respects the principles. Its Official Guide lists six steps. Do them with the team, not for it: the guide specifically advises against a project manager, team lead or coach doing STATIK alone.

  1. Identify sources of dissatisfaction. Ask the team and its customers what frustrates them, such as late delivery, overload or unpredictability. Output: a short list of problems in their words.
  2. Analyse demand. List the types of work that arrive, where they come from and roughly how often. Output: work item types with arrival rates.
  3. Analyse system capabilities. Look at how much the team delivers and how long items take today. Output: baseline throughput and cycle time, even if rough.
  4. Model the workflow. Map the states each work type passes through, including queues. Output: the board's columns and the commitment and delivery points.
  5. Identify classes of service. Agree how different kinds of urgency are treated, for example expedite, fixed date, standard and intangible. Output: classes of service with policies.
  6. Design the Kanban system. Set WIP limits, policies, cadences, the board layout and the metrics you will track. Output: a working board and a calendar of meetings.

Steps 1 to 3 are where "start with what you do now" happens in practice. Steps 4 to 6 produce the practices. To map flow in more detail before step 4, a value stream map helps, and our free workflow template gives you a place to record states and owners.

Worked example: an HR recruitment team

A five-person recruitment team supports hiring managers across a company. Hiring managers complain that they never know when a role will be filled, and recruiters feel overloaded. Here is how the team applied the principles and practices over twelve weeks.

WeeksPrinciple or practiceWhat the team didResult
1Start with what you do nowTraced eight recent hires and mapped Requested, Job ad, Sourcing, Screening, Interviews, Offer, AcceptedFound a hidden wait for interview panel availability
2Visualise; respect current rolesBuilt a board with every open role; kept recruiter and coordinator roles unchanged; showed panel scheduling as its own state27 roles open, 11 of them waiting on panels
3Limit WIPAgreed a limit of 4 active roles per recruiter in Sourcing and Screening, and 6 roles in Interviews across the teamTwo new requests waited in Requested for a week instead of starting
4Make policies explicitWrote entry criteria for Job ad (approved budget and job description) and a 48-hour panel-booking policy agreed with hiring managersFewer roles stalled after starting
5–8Manage flow; feedback loopsDaily 15-minute board walk; weekly replenishment with HR business partners; flagged any role in Interviews for more than ten working daysPanel waits became the main blocker discussed
9–12Improve collaboratively; focus on customersRan one experiment: pooled interviewers for common roles. Published an expectation to hiring managers based on recent cycle timesHiring managers received a realistic time to offer for standard roles
Tip

The numbers in this example are illustrative, not data from a real company. The pattern is what matters: trace, visualise, limit, write policies, manage flow, then improve one thing at a time.

Notice what the team did not do. It did not rename roles, buy a new applicant tracking system or redesign the hiring process. The board and its data showed the problem, and the team agreed the fix.

Frequently asked questions

What are the 4 principles of Kanban?

Kanban University's principles page lists: start with what you do now; agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change; respect the current process, roles, responsibilities and titles; and encourage acts of leadership at all levels.

What are the 6 practices of Kanban?

Visualise; limit work in progress; manage flow; make policies explicit; implement feedback loops; and improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally.

Why do some sources list six Kanban principles?

Kanban University's Official Guide groups three change management principles (start with what you do now, agree to pursue improvement through evolutionary change, encourage leadership at all levels) with three service delivery principles. Respect for current roles is folded into the first.

What are the Kanban service delivery principles?

Understand and focus on customer needs and expectations; manage the work and let people self-organise around it; and regularly review the network of services and its policies to improve outcomes.

What is the most important Kanban principle?

Kanban University gives no ranking. In practice, starting with what you do now is the foundation, and limiting WIP is the practice that usually makes the biggest difference to flow.

What does "start with what you do now" mean in Kanban?

It means applying Kanban to your existing process. You map how work really flows today and improve it step by step, rather than designing a new process first.

What are feedback loops in Kanban?

Regular meetings and reviews where the team compares expected and actual results and adjusts. Kanban University suggests a daily Kanban meeting, replenishment weekly or as needed, and a retrospective every two weeks or monthly.

What are the Kanban Guide's three practices?

The Kanban Guide 2025.5 lists defining and visualising a workflow, actively managing items in a workflow, and improving a workflow.

What are the three agendas of Kanban?

Sustainability, service orientation and survivability. Each is linked to three values: transparency, balance and collaboration; customer focus, flow and leadership; understanding, agreement and respect.

How are Kanban principles different from Scrum values?

Scrum's five values describe how a Scrum Team behaves inside a defined framework. Kanban principles describe how to evolve an existing process gradually without imposing a new one. See Kanban vs Scrum.
