What Is Kanban? Definition, Principles and Examples

Kanban is a method for managing work by making it visible on a board, limiting how much is in progress at once and continuously improving how smoothly work flows from request to delivery. It began on Toyota's factory floors and is now used by software, IT, marketing, HR and operations teams. This guide explains what Kanban means, how a Kanban system works, its principles, practices and metrics, and how to set one up step by step, with worked examples throughout.

Key takeaways

  • Kanban is a pull-based way of managing work: you visualise it, limit work in progress (WIP) and improve flow in small, evidence-based steps.
  • The Kanban Guide (2025.5 edition) defines Kanban as a strategy for optimising the flow of value through a process, built on three practices and four flow metrics.
  • The Kanban Method from Kanban University adds four principles and six general practices, starting with what you do now.
  • WIP limits are what turn a task board into a Kanban system: they stop the team starting too much and shorten the time each item takes.
  • Measure WIP, throughput, cycle time and work item age, and use percentiles of past cycle times to set a service level expectation.
  • You can start in a day with a whiteboard or a free template; no new roles, sprints or reorganisation are required.

What is Kanban?

Kanban is a method for managing and improving work that flows through a process. The team shows every work item as a card on a board, divides the board into the stages the work passes through, limits how many items can be in progress at once, and pulls new work only when capacity frees up. The aim is faster, more predictable delivery.

The Kanban Guide, the short official guide written by John Coleman and Daniel Vacanti, puts it more briefly: Kanban is a strategy for optimising the flow of value through a process. Its latest edition (2025.5, May 2025) describes three practices that work together, defining and visualising the workflow, actively managing items in the workflow and improving the workflow, and calls their combined use a Kanban system.

In simple terms, Kanban answers three everyday questions for a team:

  • What are we working on, and where is it? The board shows every item and its stage.
  • Should we start something new? Only if the WIP limit for that stage leaves room.
  • How do we get faster and more reliable? Watch where work waits, change one thing, and check the data.

Kanban does not prescribe sprints, roles or fixed meetings. You apply it to the process you already have, which makes it one of the easiest Agile approaches to start with. For how it sits among other approaches, see our overview of types of Agile methodologies.

What does Kanban mean? Origins and history

Kanban (看板) is Japanese for signboard or visual card. In manufacturing, a kanban was a physical card attached to a container of parts. When a downstream process used the parts, the card went back upstream as a signal to make or deliver more. Nothing was produced without a card, so production was pulled by actual demand rather than pushed by forecasts.

The system grew out of the Toyota Production System under industrial engineer Taiichi Ohno. Toyota studied how supermarkets restock shelves, replacing only what customers have taken, from the late 1940s, and applied the idea in its main plant machine shop in 1953. Kanban became a core tool of Just-in-Time production and the wider Lean movement.

In the 2000s, David J. Anderson adapted these ideas to knowledge work, first with a software maintenance team at Microsoft and later at Corbis. His 2010 book, *Kanban: Successful Evolutionary Change for Your Technology Business*, set out what became known as the Kanban Method. Kanban University now maintains that body of knowledge, while the separate Kanban Guide, first published in 2020, offers a minimal, flow-focused definition.

The core idea has never changed: work is pulled by demand, and limiting what is in progress makes problems visible. For the manufacturing roots, read our guide to pull vs push systems.

The Kanban Method and the Kanban Guide agree on the essentials, so most knowledge-work teams borrow from both.

How Kanban works

A Kanban system has a handful of moving parts.

A pull system

In a push system, work is assigned as soon as it arrives. In a pull system, people take the next item only when they have finished something and the next stage has room, which keeps the amount of work in the system under control.

The Kanban board

The Kanban board is a visual model of your workflow. Columns represent stages (for example Ready, In progress, Review, Done) and horizontal swimlanes can separate types of work or classes of service.

Kanban cards

Each work item is a Kanban card showing at least a title, an owner and the date it started. Many teams add a type, due date, blocker flag and class of service. Cards move left to right as work progresses.

WIP limits

A WIP limit caps how many cards can sit in a column, a group of columns or the whole board. When a column is full, the team stops starting and helps finish. WIP limits are the feature that turns a task board into a Kanban system.

Commitment and delivery points

The commitment point is where the team agrees to do an item; the clock for cycle time usually starts here. The delivery point is where the item is finished or handed to the customer. The Kanban Guide asks every team to define these start and finish points explicitly.

Explicit policies

Policies are the team's agreed rules: what must be true before a card can enter a column, who can pull from Ready, how blocked items are handled, how urgent work jumps the queue. Writing them down makes improvement easier, because you can change a rule and see the effect.

Here is an example board for a small IT support team, showing WIP limits and policies on one screen:

ColumnWIP limitEntry policy (example)Cards now
BacklogNoneAny request logged with a requester and summary14
Ready (commitment point)6Triaged, sized as small or medium, priority agreed at replenishment5
In progress4One person owns it; started date written on the card4 (full)
Waiting on customer3Question sent; follow up after 2 working days2
Review2Fix tested by someone other than the owner1
Done (delivery point)NoneRequester confirms or ticket auto-closes after 3 days23 this month

In progress is at its limit, so nobody starts a new ticket. Instead, the free person helps the reviewer or chases the oldest item waiting on a customer. Repeated daily, that decision is how Kanban improves flow.

Kanban principles and practices

The Kanban Method describes four principles and six general practices. They guide how to introduce Kanban without disrupting the team.

The four Kanban principles

Kanban University lists these principles:

  1. Start with what you do now. Map the current process as it is, rather than redesigning it first.
  2. Agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change. Make small, safe changes and keep the ones that work.
  3. Respect the current process, roles, responsibilities and titles. Avoid a reorganisation that creates fear and resistance.
  4. Encourage acts of leadership at all levels. Anyone who sees a problem can propose an improvement.

The six general practices

PracticeWhat it meansHow it looks in practice
VisualiseMake the work, the workflow and business risks visibleEvery item on the board, blocked work flagged in red
Limit WIPCap work started but not finishedColumn limits; stop starting, start finishing
Manage flowWatch and smooth how work movesReview cycle time, throughput and ageing items
Make policies explicitAgree and display the rulesEntry and exit criteria for each column
Implement feedback loopsReview regularly at several levelsDaily Kanban meeting, service delivery review
Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentallyUse models and data to guide changeRun one experiment at a time and compare the metrics

The Kanban Guide's three practices

The Kanban Guide condenses this into three practices: defining and visualising the workflow, actively managing items in the workflow (controlling WIP, preventing items ageing unnecessarily and unblocking work) and improving the workflow continuously rather than on a fixed schedule. It asks each team to write a Definition of Workflow covering, at minimum:

  • the work items that flow through the process;
  • where items start and finish;
  • the states between start and finish;
  • how WIP is controlled;
  • explicit policies for moving items through the states;
  • a service level expectation (SLE).

Kanban metrics: measuring flow

Kanban relies on a few simple measures rather than estimates and velocity. The Kanban Guide names four required flow metrics:

MetricDefinition (Kanban Guide)Question it answers
Work in progress (WIP)The number of items started but not finishedHow much are we juggling right now?
ThroughputThe number of items finished per unit of timeHow much do we deliver each week?
Work item ageElapsed time from start to now, for unfinished itemsWhich items are at risk of being late?
Cycle timeElapsed time from start to finishHow long does an item usually take?

Teams often visualise these with a cumulative flow diagram, a cycle time scatterplot and a WIP aging chart. Our Kanban metrics guide explains each chart.

Worked example: ten finished items

A five-person content team finished 10 items over the last two weeks (14 calendar days). Their cycle times, in calendar days and sorted from shortest to longest, were: 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9, 14.

MeasureCalculationResult
Throughput10 items ÷ 2 weeks5 items per week (about 0.71 per day)
Average cycle time59 days ÷ 10 items5.9 days
50th percentile (median)5th of 10 sorted values5 days
85th percentile0.85 × 10 = 8.5, round up to the 9th value9 days
Average WIP implied by Little's Law0.71 items per day × 5.9 daysAbout 4 items

Notice that one 14-day item pulls the average above the median. Cycle time data is usually skewed like this, which is why Kanban practitioners prefer percentiles to averages.

Little's Law links the three measures: average cycle time ≈ average WIP ÷ average throughput, as long as the system is reasonably stable. Suppose the board actually holds 8 items in progress while throughput stays at about 0.71 per day. Then average cycle time will drift towards 8 ÷ 0.71 ≈ 11 days. Halving WIP to 4 points back towards 5 to 6 days, with no one working harder. This is the arithmetic behind WIP limits.

Service level expectation and work item age

A service level expectation (SLE) has two parts, an elapsed time and a probability, such as 85% of items finish within 9 days. The Kanban Guide says it should be based on historical cycle time; with no history, a best guess is acceptable until data builds up. With only 10 data points, the content team treats its SLE as provisional and revisits it monthly.

The SLE becomes useful day to day when you compare it with work item age. If a card has been in progress for 7 days and the SLE is 9 days, it is close to becoming an outlier, so the team swarms on it, splits it or removes the blocker. Watching age prevents late items instead of reporting them afterwards.

A worked example: Kanban for a marketing team

Before Kanban. The team kept a shared task list. Requests arrived by email and chat, each writer had six to eight pieces on the go, and nobody could say when a new request would be done.

Week 1: visualise. The team mapped its real process: Requested, Ready, Drafting, Editing, Design, Approval, Published. Every open piece became a card. The board showed 31 items started and only 2 in Approval, which made the problem obvious: too much started, too little finished.

Week 2: limit WIP and set policies. They agreed limits of 5 for Drafting, 3 for Editing, 2 for Design and 3 for Approval, and two policies: no new draft starts while Editing is full, and any card blocked for more than two days is raised at the daily meeting. A weekly replenishment meeting with the head of marketing replaced ad hoc requests.

Weeks 3–6: manage flow. Cards piled up in Approval, so the team agreed a 48-hour approval policy with stakeholders and added an Expedite swimlane limited to one card for genuine emergencies. They started recording start and finish dates to measure cycle time and throughput.

Week 8: improve. With two months of data, they set an SLE and could answer the question stakeholders cared about most: if we commit to your article today, it will usually be live within a known number of days.

Tip

The numbers in this example are illustrative. Your own data will differ; the point is the sequence: visualise, limit, manage flow, measure, then improve one thing at a time.

How to implement Kanban step by step

These steps follow the Kanban principle of starting with what you do now.

  1. Choose the scope. Pick one team or one service with a clear group of customers, such as IT support requests or the content pipeline.
  2. Map the current workflow. List the stages work really goes through today, including waiting states such as Waiting for approval.
  3. Define your work items. Agree what a card represents (a request, a feature, a deliverable) and roughly how big it should be.
  4. Build the board and load it. Create one column per stage, mark the commitment and delivery points, and put every current item on a card with its start date.
  5. Set initial WIP limits. A common starting point is about one to one and a half items per person in each active stage, or simply the current count minus one. Expect to adjust them.
  6. Write explicit policies. Add entry criteria for each column, a rule for blocked items and a rule for urgent work.
  7. Start a daily Kanban meeting. Fifteen minutes at the board, walking it right to left: what is blocked, what is ageing, what can we finish today?
  8. Agree replenishment. Decide how and how often new items enter Ready, and who prioritises them.
  9. Measure and review. Track WIP, throughput, cycle time and work item age. After two to four weeks, hold a review, pick one bottleneck and run one experiment.

A realistic first month looks like this:

WeekFocusOutput
1Map and visualiseBoard with every current item, commitment and delivery points marked
2Limit WIP and agree policiesWIP limits on active columns, written entry criteria, daily meeting running
3Manage flowBlockers flagged and cleared daily, start and finish dates recorded
4First reviewBaseline cycle time and throughput, one improvement experiment chosen

Kanban meetings and cadences

The Kanban Guide does not mandate any meetings; it asks you to manage and improve the workflow regularly. The Kanban Method suggests a set of feedback loops, often called cadences, that teams adopt as they need them. Typical frequencies are a starting point, not a rule.

CadencePurposeTypical frequency
Kanban meeting (stand-up)Review the board, unblock work, focus on finishingDaily, about 15 minutes
Replenishment meetingSelect and commit to the next items for ReadyWeekly, or when Ready runs low
Delivery planning meetingPlan and coordinate releases to customersPer delivery
Service delivery reviewCompare delivery against customer expectations and SLEsEvery two weeks
Operations reviewLook across services for shared bottlenecksMonthly
Risk reviewAnalyse blockers and recurring risksMonthly
Strategy reviewCheck services still match business directionQuarterly

Most teams begin with the daily meeting and replenishment, then add a monthly review using blocker clustering. Our Kanban ceremonies guide gives agendas for each.

Benefits of Kanban

  • Visibility. Everyone sees what is in progress, what is waiting and what is blocked, without status meetings.
  • Shorter, more predictable delivery. Limiting WIP reduces cycle time, and percentile-based SLEs give honest forecasts.
  • Flexibility. Priorities in the backlog can change at any time; you only commit when an item is pulled into Ready.
  • Less overload and context switching. People finish work before starting more, at a sustainable pace.
  • Easy adoption. No new roles, sprints or reorganisation; you start from the current process.
  • Continuous improvement. Data and visible bottlenecks show where to improve next.
  • Works beyond software. Any process with stages, from recruitment to legal review, can use a board.

Limitations of Kanban and how to handle them

Kanban's lightness is also its weakness if a team is not disciplined.

LimitationWhy it happensHow to handle it
No built-in time boxesWork flows continuously, so deadlines can feel vagueUse SLEs, fixed-date classes of service and a regular delivery cadence
Boards can become clutteredEvery item is visible, including stale onesArchive Done weekly; review cards older than your SLE
Easy to skip WIP limitsLimits feel restrictive when demand is highMake limits a written policy and discuss every breach
Less guidance than ScrumFew prescribed events or rolesAdopt the cadences you need and write them down
Long-range planning is not built inThe focus is on flow, not release plansUse throughput data for forecasts and a simple roadmap

When to use Kanban

Kanban fits best where work arrives continuously and priorities change often.

Kanban vs Scrum and other approaches

Both Kanban and Scrum are Agile, but they organise work differently.

AspectKanbanScrum
CadenceContinuous flowFixed-length Sprints of one month or less
RolesNone requiredProduct Owner, Scrum Master, Developers
CommitmentItem by item, when pulled into ReadyA Sprint Goal and selected items each Sprint
Changing prioritiesAny time, for work not yet startedSprint scope is protected; the backlog can change
Limiting workExplicit WIP limitsLimited by the Sprint and the team's capacity
Key metricsWIP, throughput, cycle time, work item ageCommonly Sprint progress and velocity, though not required
BoardPersistent board, never resetTypically reset each Sprint

Many teams combine them. Scrum teams often add WIP limits and flow metrics, and Scrumban blends Scrum's events with Kanban's pull system.

Common Kanban mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
Using a board without WIP limitsIt becomes a to-do list; work piles up and cycle time growsSet limits on active columns, even generous ones, and lower them over time
Designing an ideal process firstThe board does not match reality, so people stop updating itMap how work really flows today
Hiding waiting statesWaiting time is invisible, yet it is often most of cycle timeAdd queue columns such as Ready for review; see flow efficiency
Cards that are too bigItems sit in one column for weeks and hide progressSplit work into items that finish within your SLE
Ignoring blocked cardsBlockers age quietly until a deadline is missedFlag blockers visibly and discuss them first every day
Reporting averages onlyOutliers distort the average and forecastsUse percentiles of cycle time
Changing many things at onceYou cannot tell which change helpedRun one experiment at a time and compare the data

Kanban tools: physical boards and software

A whiteboard and sticky notes are enough to start. Digital tools help when the team is remote, when you want automatic flow charts, or when several teams need to see each other's work.

  • Trello is a simple, approachable board tool, popular for small teams and personal Kanban.
  • Jira offers Kanban boards with column constraints and flow reports, well suited to software teams.
  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, combines board views with workflows, dashboards and cross-team reporting, a good fit when Kanban spans several departments.

Before choosing, check that the tool supports WIP limits, custom columns, blocked flags and start and finish dates.

Frequently asked questions

What is Kanban in simple terms?

Kanban is a way of managing work by putting every item on a visual board, limiting how much is in progress at once and steadily improving how work flows to completion.

What does Kanban mean in Japanese?

Kanban (看板) means signboard or visual card. At Toyota, kanban cards signalled when a process needed more parts.

Is Kanban Agile or Lean?

Both. Kanban comes from Lean manufacturing and is widely used as an Agile approach for knowledge work, focused on flow and continuous improvement rather than fixed iterations.

What are the 4 principles of Kanban?

Kanban University lists: start with what you do now; agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change; respect the current process, roles, responsibilities and titles; and encourage acts of leadership at all levels.

What are the 6 practices of Kanban?

Visualise, limit WIP, manage flow, make policies explicit, implement feedback loops, and improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally. The Kanban Guide condenses these into three practices.

Is Kanban a methodology or a framework?

The Kanban Guide calls Kanban a strategy for optimising flow, and Kanban University calls it a method. It is lighter than Scrum, with no required roles or events.

Does Kanban use sprints?

No. Kanban manages a continuous flow of work, pulling items in as capacity frees up. Teams that want a time-boxed rhythm often use Scrumban.

Does Kanban have roles?

No roles are required. Some organisations add a Service Request Manager and a Service Delivery Manager as their Kanban practice matures.

What is a WIP limit in Kanban?

A WIP limit caps how many items can be in a stage or on the board at one time. It stops the team starting too much and shortens cycle time.

What are the main Kanban metrics?

The Kanban Guide requires four flow metrics: work in progress, throughput, work item age and cycle time.

What is an example of Kanban?

A support team puts every ticket on a board with columns Ready, In progress, Review and Done, limits In progress to four tickets, and pulls a new ticket only when one is finished.

What are the disadvantages of Kanban?

Without time boxes, deadlines can feel vague, boards can become cluttered and teams may skip WIP limits. SLEs, explicit policies and regular reviews address these.

Do you need software to use Kanban?

No. A whiteboard and sticky notes are enough to start. Software helps remote teams and calculates flow metrics automatically.

Who created the Kanban Method?

David J. Anderson developed the Kanban Method for knowledge work in the 2000s, building on the kanban system of the Toyota Production System led by Taiichi Ohno.

How long does it take to implement Kanban?

You can set up a board in a day. Expect two to four weeks before you have enough data to tune WIP limits, and a few months before an SLE is reliable.
