WIP Aging Chart: How to Read and Use It, With Examples

A WIP aging chart plots every unfinished work item as a dot, placed in its current workflow stage at the height of its work item age, with lines showing how long past items took to finish. It tells you which items are at risk of missing your service level expectation while there is still time to act, which cycle time charts cannot do because they only show finished work. This guide explains how work item age is calculated, how to read the dots and percentile lines, and what to do about ageing items, with a fully worked example and spreadsheet instructions.

Key takeaways

  • A WIP aging chart shows work in progress only: one dot per started-but-unfinished item, with workflow stages on the horizontal axis and work item age on the vertical axis.
  • Work item age is the elapsed time since an item started. Calculate it the same way as cycle time, for example today − start date + 1 in calendar days.
  • Horizontal lines show percentiles of historical cycle time (commonly 50th, 70th, 85th and 95th). A dot above the 85th percentile line is already older than 85% of past items were when they finished.
  • Read age against stage: an item that is old in an early stage has most of its work still ahead and is at greater risk than an item of the same age near the end.
  • Review the chart daily, oldest and most at-risk items first, and respond by unblocking, swarming, splitting or escalating before starting new work.

What is a WIP aging chart?

A WIP aging chart (also called an aging work in progress chart) is a scatter chart of the items currently in progress. Each dot is one unfinished item, placed in its workflow stage on the horizontal axis and at its work item age on the vertical axis. Lines from historical cycle time percentiles show which items are ageing beyond what is normal.

Its purpose is to make the age of unfinished work visible every day. Most Kanban charts look backwards: a cycle time scatterplot shows how long finished items took, and a throughput chart counts what was completed. Those are lagging measures: by the time an item appears on them, it is too late to change its outcome. Work item age is a leading measure, because it describes items that can still be helped.

The chart is closely associated with Daniel Vacanti, co-author of the Kanban Guide, whose book *Actionable Agile Metrics for Predictability* devotes two chapters to it. The Kanban Guide (edition 2025.5) does not require any particular chart, but it does make work item age one of four mandatory flow metrics and lists "ensuring work items do not age unnecessarily, using the SLE as a reference" as part of actively managing work. The aging chart is the most direct way to do that.

Work item age: the measure behind the chart

The Kanban Guide defines work item age as the elapsed time between when a work item started and the current date. It applies only to items that have started and not finished; once an item finishes, its age becomes its cycle time.

Work item age = today's date − start date + 1

  • Start date: the date the item crossed your agreed start point, usually when it is pulled into the first in-progress column. Use exactly the same start point as for cycle time, or the two cannot be compared.
  • Today's date: the date you look at the chart.
  • + 1: a counting convention, so that an item started today has an age of 1 day rather than 0. Vacanti's book uses it, and our cycle time vs lead time guide uses the same convention for cycle time. Whichever convention you choose, apply it to both measures.
  • Unit: calendar days are usual, because customers wait through weekends and holidays too. Working days are acceptable if cycle time is also counted in working days.

For example, an item started on Monday 16 November and still unfinished on Wednesday 25 November has an age of 25 − 16 + 1 = 10 days.

Total age or time in the current stage?

Tools differ on what the vertical axis shows. ActionableAgile's Aging Work in Progress chart plots total age since the item started. Businessmap's Aging WIP chart describes age as the days an item has spent in its current stage. Both are useful, but they answer different questions:

MeasureQuestion it answersBest used for
Total work item ageIs this item at risk of missing our service level expectation?Daily prioritisation; matches the Kanban Guide's definition and compares directly with cycle time percentiles
Time in current stageHas this item stalled where it is now?Diagnosing a bottleneck or a stuck hand-off within one stage

Use total age as the primary measure. An item that has spent only one day in Testing may still be 20 days old overall, and its customer experiences the 20 days, not the one.

Anatomy of a WIP aging chart

Whichever tool draws it, the chart has the same parts:

ElementWhat it showsWhere the data comes from
Horizontal axisWorkflow stages in board order, from the first started stage to the last stage before doneYour Kanban board columns
Vertical axisWork item age in daysStart dates of unfinished items
DotsOne per item in progress; a number on a dot means several items share the same stage and ageCurrent board state
Percentile linesThe age by which a given share of past items had finished, for example 50%, 70%, 85% and 95%Cycle times of finished items over a recent period
Pace bands (optional)Colour zones per stage showing how old past items typically were when they left that stageStage exit dates of finished items
SLE marker (optional)Your service level expectation, often the 85th percentile lineAgreed SLE

A service level expectation (SLE) has two parts, according to the Kanban Guide: a period of elapsed time and a probability, such as "85% of items finish within 14 days". It should be based on historical cycle time. On the aging chart it becomes a threshold: an item crossing it has not necessarily failed, but it now belongs to the slowest 15% of the team's work. See our Kanban planning guide for how to calculate an SLE and keep it up to date.

Percentile lines change slowly as new items finish. Pace bands add information that the overall lines cannot: whether an item is behind for its stage. ActionableAgile, for example, colours each column by applying percentiles of past items' ages to that column, and its documentation says at least 10 completed items are needed for the calculation.

Worked example: reading one team's WIP aging chart

Illustrative example. A five-person website development team uses a Kanban board with four in-progress stages: Analysis, Development, Review and Testing. An item starts when it is pulled into Analysis and finishes when it leaves Testing. The team counts calendar days and uses the + 1 convention.

Step 1: calculate the percentile lines from finished work

Over the last six weeks the team finished 30 items. Their cycle times, sorted, in days: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10, 10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 21, 26.

With the nearest-rank method, the rank for percentile p is p ÷ 100 × n, rounded up, where n is the number of items:

PercentileRank (n = 30)Cycle time (days)Meaning
50th0.50 × 30 = 158Half of past items finished within 8 days
70th0.70 × 30 = 211070% finished within 10 days
85th0.85 × 30 = 25.5 → 261485% finished within 14 days
95th0.95 × 30 = 28.5 → 292195% finished within 21 days

The team's SLE is therefore 85% of items finish within 14 days. As a check, 26 of the 30 items (87%) took 14 days or less; with nearest rank, the chosen value always covers at least the stated share.

Step 2: calculate the age of every item in progress

On Wednesday 25 November 2026, nine items are in progress. Age = 25 November − start date + 1. The fifth column counts how many of the 30 past items finished in fewer days than the item's current age, which shows how unusual that age already is.

ItemStageStartedAge (days)Past items that finished soonerPosition against the lines
AAnalysisTue 24 Nov20 of 30 (0%)Below 50th
BAnalysisTue 17 Nov917 of 30 (57%)Between 50th and 70th
CDevelopmentMon 23 Nov31 of 30 (3%)Below 50th
DDevelopmentThu 19 Nov711 of 30 (37%)Below 50th
EDevelopmentThu 12 Nov1425 of 30 (83%)On the 85th (SLE) line; blocked by a third-party API
FReviewFri 20 Nov68 of 30 (27%)Below 50th
GReviewMon 16 Nov1019 of 30 (63%)On the 70th line
HTestingWed 18 Nov814 of 30 (47%)On the 50th line
ITestingMon 9 Nov1727 of 30 (90%)Above the 85th; SLE already missed

Step 3: read age against stage

Against the overall lines alone, item B (9 days) and item H (8 days) look similar: both sit close to the 50th percentile. But H is in Testing, the last stage, while B is still in Analysis, with Development, Review and Testing ahead of it. The team's tool also reports pace: the age by which 85% of past items had left each stage (illustrative figures for the same 30 items).

Stage85% of past items had left this stage by age (days)Items now in the stageReading
Analysis3A (2), B (9)A on pace; B three times the stage's 85th percentile
Development8C (3), D (7), E (14)D near the limit; E well behind
Review11F (6), G (10)G close to the limit
Testing14H (8), I (17)H on pace; I behind

The pace view moves B from "middle of the pack" to one of the three most urgent items. This is the most common insight an aging chart gives that a simple list of ages does not: the same age means different risk in different stages.

Step 4: estimate each item's chance of meeting the SLE

Historical data can also answer a sharper question: of past items that reached this age, how many still finished within 14 days? This is a conditional share: items with a cycle time between the current age and 14 days, divided by all items with a cycle time of at least the current age.

  • Item A (age 2): all 30 past items reached age 2, and 26 of them finished within 14 days, so 26 ÷ 30 = 87%.
  • Item B (age 9): 13 past items reached age 9; 9 of them finished within 14 days, so 9 ÷ 13 = 69%, and lower in practice because B is still in Analysis.
  • Item G (age 10): 11 past items reached age 10; 7 finished within 14 days, so 7 ÷ 11 = 64%.
  • Item E (age 14): 5 past items reached age 14 and only 1 finished on that day, so 1 in 5 (20%). Since E is blocked in Development, it will almost certainly miss the SLE.
  • Item I (age 17): the SLE is already missed. Of the 3 past items that reached age 17, 2 finished within 21 days (the 95th percentile).

The pattern is the reason ageing matters: the older an item gets, the lower its chance of finishing within the SLE, because the items that remain unfinished at a given age are disproportionately the slow ones. These shares assume the future resembles the past 30 items and ignore stage, so treat them as a guide to priority rather than a forecast.

Step 5: decide what to do

At the daily meeting the team works through the items from most to least at risk:

  1. Item I (17 days, Testing): past the SLE. Two people pair on the remaining tests today, and the delivery lead tells the requester to expect it within the next few days rather than leaving them to discover the delay.
  2. Item E (14 days, blocked): escalate the third-party API issue the same day and record the blocked time, so the cause can be analysed later with blocker clustering.
  3. Item B (9 days, Analysis): ask why. The analyst reports that the item has grown to cover three separate page templates. The team splits it into three smaller items, and the first enters Development tomorrow.
  4. Item G (10 days, Review): a developer reviews it before starting anything new.
  5. No new item starts today while four items are at risk.

Step 6: check the cause with Little's Law

Thirty items in six weeks is a throughput of 5 items a week, or 30 ÷ 42 ≈ 0.71 items per calendar day. Little's Law says average cycle time ≈ average WIP ÷ average throughput. Over the six weeks the mean cycle time was 275 ÷ 30 ≈ 9.2 days, which implies average WIP of about 9.2 × 0.71 ≈ 6.5 items. Today there are 9 items in progress. If throughput stays at 30 items per 42 days, 9 ÷ (30 ÷ 42) = 12.6 days average cycle time, about three days longer than before.

The aging chart is showing a symptom; the cause is that the team has started more work than it finishes. Its next action is to review its WIP limits. Little's Law describes long-run averages in a stable system, so this is a direction of travel, not a precise prediction.

Patterns to look for and what they mean

After a few weeks of daily use, most teams recognise a handful of recurring shapes on the chart:

Pattern on the chartLikely meaningTypical response
Old dots in early stagesItems started before they were understood, or too largeSplit, clarify or, if the work is no longer needed, cancel it
Many dots stacked in one stageA bottleneck or a full queue waiting for the next stageSwarm on the downstream stage; check the WIP limit for that column
All dots rising together day after dayLittle or nothing is finishingStop starting; finish or unblock the oldest items
A few dots far above the 95th lineForgotten, parked or blocked items still counted as in progressDecide explicitly: resume, split or cancel and remove
Dots pile up in the last stage before doneItems waiting for release, sign-off or deploymentLook at the release or approval policy rather than the team's work
Most dots above the 85th lineWIP has grown, or the SLE is out of dateReduce WIP, then recalculate percentiles from recent data

Some tools offer a replay that shows how the chart looked on earlier days. Comparing the same items a week apart shows whether dots are moving right (progressing) or only upwards (ageing in place).

Using the chart in the daily Kanban meeting

The aging chart is designed for the daily meeting, where the team focuses on the work rather than on individual status updates. A sequence that works for most teams:

  1. Start from the right of the chart and work leftwards, because items closest to done need the least effort to finish.
  2. Within each stage, start with the oldest dot, and look first at anything above the 85th percentile or behind its stage pace.
  3. Ask what the item needs to move today: a reviewer, a decision, a test environment, an answer from a supplier.
  4. Agree an action: swarm, pair, unblock, escalate, split, or cancel if the item no longer has value.
  5. Check before pulling new work: if items are at risk, finishing them takes priority over starting more.

Fifteen minutes is usually enough. The Kanban meetings and cadences guide explains how the daily meeting fits with replenishment and service delivery reviews. Scrum teams can use the same chart during the Daily Scrum to inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal; the Scrum Guide does not require it, but nothing in Scrum prevents it.

Tip

Mark blocked items differently on the chart (a different colour or shape). A blocked dot keeps ageing, and seeing several blocked items in a row on the chart is a prompt to look for a common cause.

How to build a WIP aging chart in a spreadsheet

You do not need a specialist tool. The Cards tab in the advanced and expert versions of our free Kanban board template already records Column (D), Started (E) and Finished (F) dates, with headers on row 5 and cards from row 6, and its Cycle time column (G) uses finish date − start date + 1. It does not include an aging chart, but you can add one in about fifteen minutes:

  1. Extend the card list so that it holds at least 20 to 30 finished items plus everything currently in progress, and copy the Cycle time formula, =IF(F6="","",F6-E6+1), down column G for every row.
  2. Add an Age column (column I). For a card with Started in column E and Finished in column F on row 6: =IF(AND(E6<>"",F6=""),TODAY()-E6+1,""). Finished cards and cards not yet started stay blank.
  3. Number the stages. List your in-progress stages in order in a spare range, for example L2:L5, and add a Stage number column (column J): =IFERROR(MATCH(D6,$L$2:$L$5,0),"").
  4. Spread overlapping dots. Add a Plot X column (column K) so that items in the same stage do not sit on top of each other: =IF(J6="","",J6+(COUNTIF($J$6:J6,J6)-1)*0.08), where J holds the stage number.
  5. Calculate the percentile lines from the Cycle time column G with the nearest-rank method, for example the 85th: =SMALL($G$6:$G$40,ROUNDUP(0.85*COUNT($G$6:$G$40),0)). Repeat with 0.5, 0.7 and 0.95.
  6. Insert a scatter chart with Plot X on the horizontal axis and Age on the vertical axis. Label the axis positions 1 to 4 with your stage names.
  7. Add each percentile as a two-point line series, from x = 0.5 to x = 4.5 at the percentile's value, so it runs across the whole chart.

Excel's PERCENTILE.INC and Google Sheets' PERCENTILE functions interpolate between values, so they can give slightly different results from nearest rank. Either is acceptable if you use the same method every time and say which one you use.

WIP aging chart vs other Kanban charts

Each standard Kanban chart answers a different question, and they work best together. Our Kanban metrics guide covers the full set.

ChartShowsItems includedBest for
WIP aging chartAge of each unfinished item by stageIn progress onlyDaily decisions on what to finish or unblock
Cycle time scatterplotCycle time of each finished item by finish dateFinished onlySetting and reviewing SLEs; spotting trends
Cumulative flow diagramCount of items in each stage over timeAll, as countsSeeing WIP growth and bottlenecks over weeks
Throughput run chartItems finished per day or weekFinished only, as countsForecasting with throughput and Monte Carlo

When an item finishes, its dot leaves the aging chart and becomes a dot on the cycle time scatterplot at the same age. The two charts are two halves of the same picture.

Limitations of the WIP aging chart

  • Age says nothing about remaining effort. A 3-day-old item may have weeks of work left; per-stage pace helps, but only the team can judge size. Right-sized items make the chart far more reliable.
  • Percentiles need enough history. With fewer than about 20 to 30 finished items, one unusual item moves the lines noticeably. Use a best guess at first, as the Kanban Guide allows, and recalculate as data accumulates.
  • Mixed work types blur the lines. If bugs typically take 3 days and features 15, one set of percentiles misleads for both. Draw separate lines per work type or class of service, or filter the chart; swimlanes on the board often map to these groups.
  • Stale history misleads. After a large process change, percentiles from before the change no longer describe the system. Use a rolling window, such as the last 8 to 12 weeks.
  • The chart depends on honest start dates. Moving an item back to the backlog to reset its age, or starting the clock late, hides exactly the information the chart exists to show.

Common WIP aging chart mistakes

  • Looking at it only in retrospectives. Its value is in daily action; a weekly glance turns a leading indicator into a lagging one.
  • Treating dots above the line as individual failures. Age reflects the system: WIP levels, dependencies and policies. Using the chart to judge people encourages gaming of start dates.
  • Using different start points for age and cycle time. If cycle time starts at Development but age starts at Analysis, every item looks older than it is.
  • Leaving parked items on the chart. Work that is genuinely on hold should be explicitly cancelled or moved out of the workflow by a team decision, not left to age silently and distort the picture.
  • Starting new work to look busy while old items wait. That raises WIP and, through Little's Law, lengthens cycle time for everything.
  • Ignoring the stage. Ranking items by age alone misses the early-stage items that are furthest behind.

Work item age is most useful alongside a service level expectation. See our guide to cycle time for how percentiles, scatterplots and SLEs are calculated from finished work.

Frequently asked questions

What is a WIP aging chart?

A scatter chart of the items currently in progress: each dot is one unfinished item, placed in its workflow stage and plotted at its work item age, with lines showing historical cycle time percentiles.

What is work item age?

The elapsed time between when a work item started and the current date. The Kanban Guide makes it one of four mandatory flow metrics, alongside WIP, throughput and cycle time.

How is work item age calculated?

Age = today's date − start date + 1, using the same start point and day-counting convention as for cycle time. An item started on 16 November is 10 days old on 25 November.

How do you read the percentile lines on a WIP aging chart?

Each line is a percentile of historical cycle time. A dot above the 85th percentile line is already older than 85% of past items were when they finished, so it is at high risk of missing an SLE based on that percentile.

What is the difference between a WIP aging chart and a cycle time scatterplot?

The aging chart shows unfinished items and their current age; the scatterplot shows finished items and their final cycle time. When an item finishes, it moves from the first chart to the second.

Why is work item age a leading indicator?

Because it describes items that are still in progress, it warns you about delays while you can still act. Cycle time is only known after an item has finished.

Should the chart show total age or time in the current stage?

Use total age as the main measure, because it compares directly with cycle time and the SLE. Time in the current stage is a useful secondary view for finding where an item has stalled.

What should a team do when an item is ageing?

Discuss it at the daily meeting, then swarm on it, remove the blocker, escalate a dependency or split the item. Avoid starting new work until at-risk items are moving.

How much history do you need for the percentile lines?

Aim for 20 to 30 finished items of similar type. With less data, use a best-guess SLE and recalculate as items finish.

Can Scrum teams use a WIP aging chart?

Yes. The Scrum Guide does not require it, but many teams use work item age during the Daily Scrum to decide which Sprint Backlog items need help first.

How often should the WIP aging chart be reviewed?

Daily, usually at the daily Kanban meeting or Daily Scrum. Recalculate the percentile lines every few weeks, or after a significant process change.

Which tools produce a WIP aging chart?

Specialist flow analytics tools such as ActionableAgile and Kanban tools such as Businessmap include one, and you can build a simple version in Excel or Google Sheets from start dates and stages.
