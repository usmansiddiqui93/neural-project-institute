Lean Manufacturing: Principles, Wastes, Tools and Examples

Lean manufacturing is a way of producing goods that delivers what the customer values with the least possible waste, by making work flow smoothly, producing only what is pulled by demand and improving the process continuously. It grew out of the Toyota Production System and now shapes operations, healthcare, software and project management. This guide covers the principles, the eight wastes and the main tools, then works through value stream and takt time examples and a step-by-step way to start.

Key takeaways

  • Lean manufacturing maximises customer value by removing waste (muda), unevenness (mura) and overburden (muri) from the way work flows.
  • The five lean principles are value, value stream, flow, pull and perfection, set out by James Womack and Daniel Jones in Lean Thinking (1996).
  • The eight wastes (TIMWOODS) are transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects and unused skills.
  • Core tools include Just-in-Time, kanban, jidoka, 5S, poka-yoke, SMED, standard work, value stream mapping and kaizen, but tools only work inside a culture of problem-solving.
  • Start with one value stream: map it, stabilise it, introduce flow and pull, then improve in small, measured cycles.
  • The same ideas apply to projects: limit work in progress, make problems visible and let the people doing the work improve it.

What is lean manufacturing?

Lean manufacturing is a production approach that aims to deliver exactly what customers value, when they need it, using the fewest resources. It does this by identifying every step in the process, removing steps that add no value, making the remaining steps flow without delay and improving the system continuously with the people who do the work.

The core idea is to look at every activity from the customer's point of view and sort it into one of three groups:

Type of activityWhat it meansExample in a furniture workshop
Value-addingPhysically changes the product in a way the customer would pay forCutting, sanding and assembling a table
Necessary but non-value-addingAdds no value but cannot be removed yet because of current technology, rules or contractsSafety inspection, regulatory labelling
Pure wasteAdds no value and could be removedMoving parts to a store and back, waiting for a forklift, reworking a scratched top

The value-adding share is often small, so lean focuses first on time spent waiting, travelling and being reworked, not on making people work faster.

Lean also targets two problems that cause waste: mura (unevenness in workload or demand) and muri (overburden on people or machines). These are explained in the section on the eight wastes below.

Lean manufacturing vs mass production

AspectTraditional mass productionLean manufacturing
SchedulingPush: make to forecast and store the outputPull: make what the next step or customer has used
Batch sizeLarge batches to spread set-up costSmall batches, made affordable by quick changeovers
InventoryBuffers everywhere to protect each stepKept deliberately low so problems surface
QualityInspected at the endBuilt in at each step; the line stops when a defect appears
ProblemsWorked around to protect outputExposed, analysed and fixed at the root cause

Where lean manufacturing came from

Lean grew out of the Toyota Production System (TPS), developed in Japan after the Second World War, when Toyota could not afford the large inventories of American car makers and had to build a variety of vehicles efficiently in small volumes.

According to the Lean Enterprise Institute, the starting point was Henry Ford's flow production at Highland Park, Michigan, in 1913, which combined interchangeable parts, standard work and moving conveyance. Kiichiro Toyoda, Taiichi Ohno and colleagues at Toyota rethought Ford's approach for a world of high variety and low volume.

WhenMilestone
1896 and 1924Sakichi Toyoda's looms stop automatically when a thread breaks, the origin of jidoka
1938Kiichiro Toyoda's Koromo plant applies flow production and the idea of Just-in-Time
Late 1940s onwardsTaiichi Ohno develops JIT and jidoka into a working system through years of experiment
1950sThe supermarket method, inspired by how shops restock only what customers take, becomes the kanban system
1988John Krafcik coins the term lean in his Sloan Management Review article "Triumph of the Lean Production System"
1990Womack, Jones and Roos publish The Machine That Changed the World, a study of the global car industry
1996Womack and Jones publish Lean Thinking and its five principles
1998Rother and Shook publish Learning to See, popularising value stream mapping

Our Just-in-Time guide tells the Toyota story in more detail, and what is Kanban shows how the factory kanban became a method for knowledge work.

The Toyota Production System house

Toyota describes TPS as resting on two pillars: Just-in-Time, which it defines as making only what is needed, when it is needed, and in the amount needed; and jidoka, automation with a human touch, where a machine stops itself or a worker stops the line when something is wrong. Lean practitioners often draw this as a house:

  • Roof: the goal, which is the best quality, lowest cost and shortest lead time, delivered safely.
  • Left pillar, Just-in-Time: takt time, continuous flow and pull (kanban).
  • Right pillar, jidoka: stop at every abnormality, separate people's work from machine work, and fix the cause.
  • Foundation: stable processes, standard work, levelled production (heijunka) and kaizen (continuous improvement).

The two pillars depend on each other: low inventory without built-in quality means constant stoppages, and quality without flow leaves problems hidden in stock.

The five principles of lean manufacturing

In Lean Thinking (1996), James Womack and Daniel Jones distilled the Toyota approach into five principles. They are meant to be applied in order, then repeated.

PrincipleWhat it meansFactory exampleProject example
1. ValueDefine value from the customer's point of viewA customer needs 200 hinges a week, delivered on time and to tolerance; a polished finish is not requiredThe sponsor needs a working payroll integration, not a 60-page design document
2. Value streamMap every step from order to delivery and classify each as value-adding or wasteMap the hinge's path from steel coil to dispatch, including stores and waitingMap the request from intake to release, including approvals and hand-offs
3. FlowMake the value-adding steps follow each other without stops, batches or detoursMove the press next to the bending machine and pass parts one at a timeReduce hand-offs and finish one feature before starting the next
4. PullProduce only when the next step or customer signals demandA kanban card authorises another box of hinges only when one is usedStart new work only when a WIP limit frees up space
5. PerfectionKeep improving, because there is always more waste to removeWeekly kaizen on changeover timeRegular retrospectives with one improvement action each

The Lean Enterprise Institute also stresses respect for people: lean is a practice of continuous experimentation by the people doing the work, not a set of cost-cutting tools imposed on them.

The eight wastes of lean manufacturing

Waste, or muda, is any activity that consumes time or resources without adding value for the customer. Toyota's seven wastes are usually credited to Taiichi Ohno; practitioners later added an eighth, unused skills. The acronym TIMWOODS is a common way to remember them.

WasteFactory exampleKnowledge-work exampleTypical countermeasure
TransportForklifting parts between distant buildingsPassing a document through five teams for sign-offRe-layout into cells; fewer hand-offs
InventoryPallets of finished parts waiting for ordersA backlog of half-finished tasks and unreviewed workPull systems and WIP limits
MotionOperators walking to fetch toolsSearching shared drives for the latest version5S, point-of-use storage, one source of truth
WaitingA machine idle while waiting for materialWaiting days for an approval or a replyBalance work to takt; agree response times
OverproductionMaking more than ordered, or making it earlyBuilding features or reports nobody asked forMake to demand; small batches
Over-processingTighter tolerances or more polishing than neededGold-plating, duplicate data entry, excessive reportingDefine value; simplify standards
DefectsScrap and reworkBugs, errors and rework after reviewJidoka, poka-yoke, root cause analysis
Skills (unused)Operators' improvement ideas ignoredExperts doing admin; ideas never asked forKaizen; involve people in redesign

Ohno singled out overproduction as the worst waste, because the extra inventory hides every other problem: defects sit undiscovered in stock and waiting is masked by buffers.

Muda, mura and muri

Lean treats waste as the visible symptom of two deeper causes. Removing muda without addressing them tends to bring the waste back.

TermMeaningExampleLean response
MudaWaste: activity that adds no valueParts waiting two days between operationsValue stream mapping, flow, pull
MuraUnevenness in demand or workloadProducing all of product A on Monday and all of product B on Friday, so stock swings up and downHeijunka (levelled, mixed production)
MuriOverburden on people or equipmentRunning a press beyond its rated speed, or one operator covering two stations at peakStandard work, capacity planning, preventive maintenance

Unevenness causes overburden at peaks, and overburden causes breakdowns, errors and therefore waste. That is why levelling the schedule is part of the foundation of the Toyota house rather than an optional extra.

Lean manufacturing tools and techniques

Lean has a large toolkit. The table summarises the tools you will meet most often and the problem each one solves.

5S

5S organises the workplace so that problems and abnormalities are obvious at a glance. The five Japanese words and their usual English equivalents are seiri (sort: remove what is not needed), seiton (set in order: a place for everything), seiso (shine: clean and inspect), seiketsu (standardise: make the first three routine) and shitsuke (sustain: keep the discipline). Many organisations add safety as a sixth S. Our guide to visual management covers 5S alongside other visual controls.

SMED and quick changeover

SMED, developed by Shigeo Shingo, separates changeover work into internal steps (machine stopped) and external steps (machine running), moves as much as possible to external and simplifies the rest. Shorter changeovers make small batches, and therefore flow and pull, economical. Kanban loops then cap the stock between steps; see Kanban cards and Kanban inventory management.

Kaizen, PDCA and the 5 whys

Kaizen means continuous improvement by everyone, usually through Plan, Do, Check, Act: try a small change, measure it, then standardise it or try again. The 5 whys is the simplest root cause technique: keep asking why a machine stopped until you reach something fixable, such as a missing lubrication standard rather than a blown fuse. For more complex problems, combine it with a fishbone diagram and our free root cause analysis template.

Worked example: mapping a value stream

Illustrative example. A small metal workshop makes one family of steel hinges in five steps. Demand is 400 hinges per working day on one shift of 420 available minutes (25,200 seconds), so takt time is 25,200 ÷ 400 = 63 seconds and every step below runs within it. The team walks the process and records each step's cycle time and the inventory waiting in front of it.

StepCycle time per unitInventory waiting before the stepDays of waiting (inventory ÷ 400 a day)
Raw steel storeNone800 units' worth2.0
1. Cut40 s00
2. Weld60 s4001.0
3. Paint50 s6001.5
4. Assemble60 s3000.75
5. Pack35 s2000.5
Total245 s (about 4 minutes)2,300 units5.75 working days

A hinge spends about four minutes being worked on and almost six working days waiting. The process cycle efficiency (value-adding time divided by total lead time) is 245 seconds out of 5.75 × 25,200 = 144,900 seconds of working time, about 0.17%. When nearly all of the lead time is waiting, speeding up operators barely changes it, which is why lean starts with waiting and inventory. (The days-of-waiting column treats each queue as being worked off at the daily demand rate, a simplification that ignores variation in demand and in step times.)

The team then designs a future state:

  1. Replace the stockpiles between steps with kanban loops capped at 100 units each, so a step only produces when the next one has used a container.
  2. Halve raw steel stock to one day by agreeing more frequent supplier deliveries.
  3. Run a SMED workshop on the paint line, whose long colour changeovers were the reason for large batches.
  4. Move cutting and welding next to each other so parts pass in small trays instead of on pallets.

With four loops of 100 units (0.25 days each) and one day of raw steel, lead time falls from about 5.75 to about 2 working days (process cycle efficiency rises to roughly 0.5%), while cycle times and output are unchanged. This is Little's Law at work: at the same throughput, less inventory means a shorter lead time. Our guides to value stream mapping and WIP limits explain both techniques step by step.

Tip

Map the current state by walking the process with a stopwatch, not from the ERP system or the procedure manual. The gap between the documented and the real process is usually where the waste is.

Worked example: takt time and line balance

Takt time is the pace at which you must finish one unit to meet demand. The formula is:

Takt time = available working time ÷ customer demand

Illustrative example. The assembly area has 420 minutes (25,200 seconds) of available time per shift and customers need 360 units per shift. Takt time is 25,200 ÷ 360 = 70 seconds. Every step must be able to finish a unit in 70 seconds or less, or the line will fall behind.

StationCycle timeCompared with 70 s taktComment
A: Sub-assembly55 s15 s spareHas capacity to absorb a task
B: Main assembly68 s2 s spareClose to takt; protect from disruption
C: Wiring and test82 s12 s overBottleneck: the line cannot meet demand
D: Final inspection and pack60 s10 s spareHas capacity

Station C limits the whole line to one unit every 82 seconds, or about 307 units per shift, well short of 360. The team has three options, in the order lean would normally try them:

  1. Remove waste inside station C first, for example pre-cut wire harnesses delivered to the point of use, which removes walking and measuring.
  2. Rebalance by moving a task, such as attaching labels, from C to station A or D, which have spare time.
  3. Only if those are not enough, add capacity: a second test fixture or a second operator at peak times.

Running station A faster would only pile sub-assemblies in front of C, which is overproduction. That is the central insight of the theory of constraints: improving anywhere other than the bottleneck does not raise output.

How to implement lean manufacturing step by step

Lean is introduced most successfully as a series of experiments on one value stream, led by the people who work in it. A typical sequence:

  1. Choose one value stream. Pick a product family with steady demand and a visible problem, such as long lead times.
  2. State the business reason. Agree one or two measurable goals, for example lead time and on-time delivery, and the baseline today.
  3. Form and train a small team of operators, a team leader, maintenance, quality and planning. Teach value, the eight wastes, flow and pull.
  4. Map the current state. Walk the process, record cycle times, inventory and waiting, and calculate lead time and takt time, as in the examples above.
  5. Run a waste walk at the gemba. Watch the work for an hour and list every instance of TIMWOODS you see, without blaming anyone.
  6. Stabilise the process. Apply 5S, write simple standard work for the main tasks and fix the most frequent breakdowns. Flow is impossible on an unstable process.
  7. Introduce flow and pull. Rearrange steps to reduce transport, cut batch sizes (with SMED where changeovers are long) and replace stockpiles with kanban loops.
  8. Improve in short cycles. Run regular kaizen events or weekly improvement huddles using PDCA, and measure the effect of every change against the baseline.
  9. Sustain and spread. Use visual boards, daily stand-ups at the work area and regular gemba walks by leaders, then repeat on the next value stream.

Treat the rollout as a project in its own right, with a charter, owners and a cadence of reviews. Our guide to manufacturing project management covers the wider context, and the free workflow template is a simple way to document current and future process steps, owners and hand-offs.

If you are running several improvement projects across sites, a work management tool helps keep actions and measures visible. Wrike suits programmes that need cross-site dashboards and resource views, while Smartsheet suits operations teams that prefer a spreadsheet-style grid.

Tip

Make the first improvements ones that remove frustration for operators, such as searching for tools or waiting for materials. Early wins build the trust lean depends on.

Benefits and limitations of lean manufacturing

Benefits

  • Shorter lead times, because products spend less time waiting in inventory between steps.
  • Less inventory and floor space, releasing cash and room for other work.
  • Higher quality, because problems are stopped and fixed at the source rather than discovered at final inspection.
  • More flexibility to respond to changes in mix and demand.
  • Better use of people's skills, because operators are expected to solve problems and improve their own work.

Limitations and risks

  • Thin buffers can make supply chains fragile. Low inventory leaves little protection against disasters, transport disruption or supplier failure, a weakness widely discussed after the supply shortages of the COVID-19 period.
  • People can be overstretched when lean is done badly. Treating lean as headcount reduction, or removing all slack, creates stress and destroys the willingness to raise problems.
  • Tool-first rollouts tend to fade. Painting floor lines and running 5S audits without changing how managers lead rarely lasts.
  • Traditional cost accounting can work against it. Reducing inventory can look like a fall in absorbed overhead in the short term, even when cash and lead time improve.

Mature lean organisations manage these risks with deliberately sized buffers for critical parts, diversified suppliers where justified and genuine respect for people.

Lean vs Six Sigma, Agile and Kanban

Lean is often combined with, or confused with, related approaches. The table shows how they differ.

ApproachMain questionTypical toolsOrigin
LeanHow do we remove waste and make value flow?VSM, 5S, kanban, SMED, kaizenToyota Production System
Six SigmaHow do we reduce variation and defects?DMAIC, statistical process control, design of experimentsMotorola, 1980s
AgileHow do we deliver value in small increments and adapt to change?Iterations, backlogs, reviews, retrospectivesSoftware development, 2001 Agile Manifesto
Kanban MethodHow do we visualise knowledge work, limit WIP and improve flow?Kanban boards, WIP limits, flow metricsLean, adapted to knowledge work in the 2000s

Lean mainly attacks delay and waste, while Six Sigma mainly attacks variation and defects, which is why they are often combined. Our guides to Lean vs Six Sigma and Six Sigma explain when to use each, and Scrum vs Lean and Kanban vs Agile cover the software side.

Lean manufacturing lessons for project teams

You do not need a production line to use lean. Most of its tools have a direct equivalent in project and knowledge work.

Factory practiceProject or knowledge-work equivalent
Kanban cards cap inventoryA Kanban board with WIP limits caps work in progress
Andon signals a problemA red blocker flag on a card, raised at the daily stand-up
Jidoka: stop and fixStop starting new work when a defect or blocker appears; swarm on it
SMEDShorten set-up and hand-off time: templates, automated environments, clear definitions of ready
Value stream mappingMap a request from intake to delivery and measure flow efficiency
KaizenRetrospectives and lessons learned with one owned action each

The biggest lesson is usually about waiting. As in the hinge example, work items often spend far longer waiting for reviews, approvals and decisions than being worked on, so limiting work in progress does more for delivery dates than asking people to work faster.

Common lean manufacturing mistakes

  • Starting with tools instead of problems. Pick the business problem first, then the tool that addresses it.
  • Cutting inventory before stabilising the process. Low stock on an unreliable process simply stops the line; fix breakdowns and quality first.
  • Using lean to cut headcount. If improvement leads to redundancies, people stop suggesting improvements. Redeploy freed capacity to growth or further improvement.
  • Leaving managers out. Lean depends on leaders going to the gemba, coaching and responding to problems, not only on operators' effort.
  • Not measuring. Without a baseline for lead time, quality and delivery, you cannot tell whether a change helped. Lean is part of wider project quality management and needs the same discipline.

Frequently asked questions

What is lean manufacturing in simple terms?

Lean manufacturing means making what customers want, when they want it, with as little waste as possible. You look at every step in the process, remove the ones that add no value, make the rest flow without waiting and keep improving with the people who do the work.

What are the 5 principles of lean manufacturing?

Value, value stream, flow, pull and perfection. Define value from the customer's view, map the steps that deliver it, make those steps flow, produce only when pulled by demand and keep improving. They were set out by James Womack and Daniel Jones in Lean Thinking (1996).

What are the 8 wastes of lean?

Transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects and unused skills, often remembered as TIMWOODS. The first seven come from Toyota and are usually credited to Taiichi Ohno; unused skills was added later by practitioners.

What is the difference between muda, mura and muri?

Muda is waste, mura is unevenness in workload or demand and muri is overburden on people or machines. Unevenness causes overburden at peaks, and overburden causes errors and breakdowns, which create waste. Lean addresses all three, for example by levelling the schedule (heijunka).

Where did lean manufacturing come from?

From the Toyota Production System, developed in Japan by Kiichiro Toyoda, Taiichi Ohno and colleagues, mainly from the late 1940s onwards. The word lean was coined by John Krafcik in a 1988 article and popularised by The Machine That Changed the World (1990).

Is lean manufacturing the same as Just-in-Time?

No. Just-in-Time is one part of lean: producing only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. Toyota pairs it with jidoka, and lean also includes standard work, levelling, kaizen and respect for people. See our Just-in-Time guide.

What is 5S in lean manufacturing?

5S is a method for organising a workplace: sort, set in order, shine, standardise and sustain. Its purpose is to make the normal state obvious, so missing tools, leaks or excess stock are spotted immediately.

What is jidoka?

Jidoka, which Toyota describes as automation with a human touch, means a machine stops itself, or a worker stops the line, as soon as an abnormality occurs. The problem is fixed at its source so defects are not passed on. It is one of the two pillars of the Toyota Production System.

What are the disadvantages of lean manufacturing?

Low inventory can make supply chains vulnerable to disruption, poorly managed lean can overstretch staff, results fade when tools are adopted without changing leadership, and traditional cost accounting can make inventory reduction look unattractive in the short term.

What is the difference between lean manufacturing and Six Sigma?

Lean removes waste so work flows faster, while Six Sigma reduces variation so results are consistent, using statistical methods and the DMAIC cycle. Many organisations combine them as Lean Six Sigma. See Lean vs Six Sigma.

Is lean manufacturing only for factories?

No. Lean began in car factories but is now used in healthcare, logistics, offices, software, construction and project management. Kanban for knowledge work is a direct descendant of lean manufacturing.
