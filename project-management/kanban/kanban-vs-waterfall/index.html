Kanban vs Waterfall: Key Differences and When to Use Each

Kanban vs Waterfall is a choice between planning a whole project up front and moving through fixed phases in sequence (Waterfall), or managing a continuous flow of work items that you reprioritise and deliver one at a time (Kanban). Neither is better in general: Waterfall suits stable, well-defined projects that only have value when complete, while Kanban suits ongoing or changing work that can be delivered in pieces. This guide compares them side by side, gives you a scored decision check and a worked example, and shows how to combine them.

Key takeaways

  • Waterfall is a predictive project life cycle: define scope, baseline the plan, then run requirements, design, build, test and deploy in order.
  • Kanban is a method for managing flow: visualise the work, limit work in progress and improve continuously, delivering each item when it is ready.
  • They are not strictly alternatives. Kanban can run on top of any process, including the phases of a Waterfall project.
  • Choose Waterfall for fixed requirements, physical or regulated work and fixed-price commitments; choose Kanban for continuous requests, changing priorities and work that can be released in parts.
  • Waterfall measures progress against a baseline schedule and budget; Kanban uses flow metrics such as cycle time and throughput to forecast.
  • Hybrids are common: Waterfall governance, milestones and a Gantt chart at the top, with Kanban boards for day-to-day execution.

What is the difference between Kanban and Waterfall?

The main difference between Kanban and Waterfall is how they plan and deliver work. Waterfall plans the entire project at the start and completes each phase before the next, delivering the result at the end. Kanban plans continuously, pulls work items through a visual board as capacity frees up and delivers each item as soon as it is finished.

They also sit at different levels. Waterfall is a project life cycle: a way of structuring a project from start to finish. Kanban is a method for managing the flow of work through whatever process you already have. That is why the comparison is partly a false choice, and why many teams use a Kanban board inside a Waterfall project.

In PMI terms, Waterfall is the classic predictive approach and Kanban is an adaptive one; a deliberate mix is a hybrid approach. The PMBOK Guide 8th edition and the current PMP exam expect project managers to understand all three, so knowing when each fits is a core skill, not a matter of preference.

What is Waterfall?

Waterfall runs a project through a fixed series of phases, typically requirements, design, build, test, deployment and maintenance. Each phase produces approved deliverables that feed the next, and a phase gate lets sponsors check the work before money is committed to the next stage.

Scope, schedule and budget are planned in detail at the start and fixed in a project baseline. Progress is tracked against that baseline, usually with a Gantt chart and milestones, and changes go through formal change control.

The model is usually traced to Winston Royce's 1970 paper, which actually warned against a single pass and recommended feedback between phases. In practice, Waterfall's strength is predictability: everyone knows what will be built, when and for how much. Its weakness is late feedback, because users often see the result only near the end, when change is most expensive.

What is Kanban?

Kanban began on Toyota's factory floors as a pull system that replenished parts only when they were used. In the 2000s, David J. Anderson adapted it to knowledge work, and his 2010 book set out what became known as the Kanban Method.

A Kanban system makes work visible on a board, limits how many items can be in progress at each stage and manages the flow of items from request to delivery. Teams pull new work only when there is capacity, and they improve the process step by step using data.

Two reference sources describe it slightly differently:

  • The Kanban Method (Kanban University) starts with change-management principles: start with what you do now, agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change, respect the current process, roles and titles, and encourage leadership at all levels. Its general practices are to visualise, limit WIP, manage flow, make policies explicit, implement feedback loops and improve collaboratively.
  • The Kanban Guide (2025 edition) describes three practices: defining and visualising the workflow, actively managing items in the workflow, and improving the workflow. It requires four flow measures: WIP, throughput, work item age and cycle time.

The principle "start with what you do now" matters for this comparison. Kanban does not ask you to abandon Waterfall; it asks you to make your current process visible and improve it. Our guide to Kanban principles covers them in more detail.

Key differences explained

Planning and commitment

Waterfall commits early: you plan all the work, estimate it and agree a date and budget before execution starts. Kanban commits late and in small pieces: the team commits to an item only when it pulls it into progress, and Kanban planning happens continuously through replenishment meetings. Waterfall gives you a full plan on day one; Kanban gives you the flexibility to change direction without re-planning everything.

How work moves

In Waterfall, the whole project passes through each phase once: all design is done before any building starts. In Kanban, each item moves through the stages on its own. On any given day, one item may be in testing while another is still being analysed. A Kanban board's columns may look like Waterfall phases, but the batch size is one item, not the whole project.

Handling change

Waterfall treats change as an exception: a change request is assessed for impact on scope, time and cost, approved or rejected, and the baseline is updated. Kanban treats change as normal: anything not yet started can be reprioritised at the next replenishment at no cost, while work already in progress is usually finished first.

When value is delivered

Waterfall usually delivers once, at the end. That is fine when the result only has value as a whole, such as a building or a regulatory submission. Kanban delivers each item when it is finished, so customers start getting value, and giving feedback, much earlier.

Roles and responsibilities

Waterfall projects are typically led by a project manager who plans, assigns and controls the work, with specialists contributing in their phase. Kanban prescribes no roles and asks you to keep the ones you have; some organisations add a service delivery manager or a service request manager, but this is optional.

Measuring progress and forecasting

Waterfall measures progress against the baseline: percentage complete, milestones hit, and often earned value. Kanban measures flow: cycle time, throughput, WIP and work item age. Instead of estimating every task, Kanban teams forecast from historical throughput, for example "85% of items finish within 8 days" or "we finish 4 to 6 items a week".

Documentation and governance

Waterfall relies on documents approved at each gate: requirements specifications, design documents and test plans. Kanban relies on explicit policies on the board, such as WIP limits and a definition of done for each column. Kanban can still produce documents when they are needed; it just does not use them to control progress.

Risk

Waterfall reduces risk through up-front analysis and planning, but risks that only show up when users see the product surface late. Kanban reduces risk by delivering small pieces and getting feedback early, but without a plan it can drift if nobody owns priorities or the bigger picture.

Pros and cons of Kanban and Waterfall

WaterfallKanban
ProsClear plan, budget and date from the start; easy to contract and govern; strong documentation; suits fixed-price and regulated work; familiar to most stakeholdersEarly and continuous delivery; easy to change priorities; low disruption to adopt; exposes bottlenecks; works for ongoing services as well as projects
ConsLate feedback; change is slow and costly; problems found late in testing; risk of building the wrong thing when requirements were uncertainNo built-in long-range plan or fixed end date; needs discipline on WIP limits; can lose sight of the overall goal; less familiar to some sponsors and contracts

Most of the cons can be reduced. Waterfall teams can add early prototypes and interim reviews; Kanban teams can add a roadmap, milestones and regular service delivery reviews. The real question is which weaknesses your project can tolerate.

When to use Waterfall

Waterfall is usually the better choice when most of these are true:

  • Requirements are stable, well understood and unlikely to change.
  • The result only has value when it is complete, such as a building, a factory line or a data-centre move.
  • Regulation, safety or a contract requires documented, approved phases.
  • Late change is physically or financially very costly.
  • The customer needs a fixed scope, price and date before work starts.
  • The team has delivered similar projects before and can estimate accurately.

When to use Kanban

Kanban is usually the better choice when most of these are true:

  • Work arrives as a continuous stream of requests rather than one defined project.
  • Priorities change often, sometimes weekly or daily.
  • Items can be delivered and used independently.
  • You want to improve an existing process without reorganising teams or roles.
  • Timeboxed sprints do not suit the work, for example because urgent requests cannot wait for the next sprint.
  • You want to see and remove bottlenecks using data.

How to choose: a step-by-step decision check

Use this check with your sponsor and team at the start of a project or when setting up a new workstream:

  1. Describe the work. Is it a one-off project with a defined end, or an ongoing stream? Write one sentence for each major part.
  2. Score each part against the eight questions below, from 1 (strongly Waterfall) to 5 (strongly Kanban).
  3. Add up the scores and read the result from the bands.
  4. Look for mixed scores. A few very low answers, such as a fixed contract or a regulatory gate, usually mean a hybrid: keep those controls and use Kanban inside them.
  5. Agree and record the approach in your project management plan, including how you will report progress.
  6. Review after the first month and adjust.

Worked example: the same work run both ways

This illustrative example follows a five-person digital team asked to deliver 12 improvements to an online booking process, each of similar size.

Run as Waterfall

The team gathers requirements for all 12 improvements (weeks 1 to 2), designs them (weeks 3 to 4), builds them (weeks 5 to 10), tests them together (weeks 11 to 12) and releases everything in week 13. The sponsor gets a fixed date and a clear plan on day one. Customers see nothing until week 13.

Run as Kanban

The team orders the 12 items by value, sets a WIP limit of 3 and pulls the top items. Each item takes about three weeks from start to release, and after the first few the team finishes about one a week. The first improvement goes live in week 3.

In this illustration Kanban finishes the full set a week later, because each release carries its own testing and deployment overhead. But 10 of the 12 improvements were in customers' hands by week 12, and the team learned from each release as it went.

A change in week 6

In week 6, booking data shows many customers abandon at the payment step, and the sponsor wants a simpler payment page as the top priority.

  • Waterfall: the team raises a change request (see our change request template). Design must be reopened, the build plan reworked and testing extended. In this illustration the change is approved and the release moves to week 15, with 13 items.
  • Kanban: the new item goes to the top of the backlog at the next replenishment, is started in week 7 and goes live around week 9. The lowest-value original item slips to the end or is dropped.

Forecasting the remaining work

At the end of week 8, the Waterfall team reports progress against the baseline (for example, build 60% complete, on schedule). The Kanban team uses its throughput, about 0.8 to 1.2 items a week, to forecast that the remaining six items will take roughly five to eight weeks. The first answer sounds more precise; the second is based on what the team has actually delivered.

Combining Kanban and Waterfall (hybrid)

Many organisations need Waterfall's governance (business cases, budgets, phase gates, milestones) but want Kanban's visibility and flow for day-to-day work. A hybrid gives you both.

Kanban inside Waterfall phases: step by step

  1. Keep the phase plan and milestones. Leave your Gantt chart, milestones and gate reviews in place for sponsors.
  2. Turn work packages into cards. Break each phase's work into items that take days rather than weeks, and put them in the phase backlog.
  3. Design the board. Create columns for how work actually flows within the phase, and use swimlanes for phases or workstreams if several run at once.
  4. Set WIP limits on in-progress and review columns so the team finishes work before starting more.
  5. Make the gate a column. Add an "Awaiting approval" column and agree a definition of done that matches the gate criteria.
  6. Hold a short daily board walk from right to left, focusing on blocked and ageing items.
  7. Report both views. Give sponsors milestone status from the Gantt chart and flow measures from the board.

What to report to sponsors

Sponsors used to Waterfall reports want to know whether the project is on time and on budget. Keep giving them milestone dates and budget status, and add three flow measures: throughput (items finished per week), WIP and the oldest items in progress. A cumulative flow diagram is often the easiest single chart to explain.

Moving from Waterfall to Kanban

If your team wants to move further towards Kanban, follow the Kanban Method's advice to start with what you do now:

  1. Map your current process, phases and handoffs as board columns, without changing anything.
  2. Put all current work on the board and watch it for two to four weeks.
  3. Introduce WIP limits where work piles up.
  4. Make policies explicit, such as entry and exit criteria for each column.
  5. Start delivering smaller items as soon as they are ready, where the business can use them.
  6. Replace some status reports with flow metrics once stakeholders trust them.

Kanban vs Waterfall vs Scrum

Scrum is often the third option on the table. It is adaptive like Kanban, but organises work into fixed-length sprints with defined accountabilities and events, as described in the 2020 Scrum Guide.

Common mistakes when comparing or combining them

  • Treating board columns as phases. If the whole project moves through each column together, you have a Waterfall plan drawn as a board. Let items flow individually.
  • Choosing by fashion. Pick the approach that fits the work and constraints, not the one that sounds modern.
  • Using Kanban without WIP limits. A board without limits is a to-do list; you lose most of Kanban's benefits.
  • Dropping the plan entirely. Kanban teams still need a goal, priorities and, for projects, milestones stakeholders can rely on.
  • Forcing Waterfall on uncertain work. If requirements will change, a fixed plan becomes a series of change requests.
  • Reporting only one view in a hybrid. Sponsors need milestone status; teams need flow data. Give both.
  • Changing everything at once. Kanban works best as incremental change from your current process.
  • Ignoring contracts. Fixed-price contracts often need Waterfall-style scope control, even if delivery uses Kanban.

Tools and templates for Kanban, Waterfall and hybrid work

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Kanban and Waterfall?

Waterfall plans the whole project up front and runs it in sequential phases, delivering at the end. Kanban manages a continuous flow of work items, limits work in progress and delivers each item when it is ready.

Is Kanban agile or waterfall?

Kanban is generally classed as an Agile, adaptive approach. It can, however, be applied to any process, including the tasks inside a Waterfall project.

Is Kanban better than Waterfall?

Neither is better in general. Kanban suits continuous, changing work that can be delivered in parts; Waterfall suits stable, well-defined projects that only have value when complete.

Can a Waterfall project use a Kanban board?

Yes. Keep the phase plan, milestones and gates, and use a Kanban board with WIP limits to manage the tasks within each phase.

Which delivers faster, Kanban or Waterfall?

Kanban usually delivers individual items much sooner, because each is released when ready. Total completion time for a fixed set of work may be similar.

When is Waterfall the better choice?

When requirements are stable, late change is costly, regulation or contracts require approved phases, or the result only has value when complete, as in many construction projects.

When is Kanban the better choice?

When work arrives continuously, priorities change often and items can be used independently, as in support, maintenance, DevOps and content production.

Do Kanban teams use Gantt charts?

Not usually. Kanban relies on the board and flow metrics, though hybrid teams often keep a Gantt chart for milestones and dependencies.

How is progress measured in Kanban vs Waterfall?

Waterfall measures progress against a baseline schedule and budget, for example with earned value. Kanban tracks flow measures such as cycle time, throughput, WIP and work item age.

How does change control differ in Kanban and Waterfall?

Waterfall routes scope changes through formal change control and updates the baseline. In Kanban, anything not yet started can be reprioritised at the next replenishment.

Does Kanban have deadlines?

Kanban has no built-in deadlines, but teams can manage fixed-date items with classes of service and forecast delivery dates from past throughput.

Which is cheaper, Kanban or Waterfall?

Neither is inherently cheaper. Kanban can reduce the cost of building the wrong thing, while Waterfall can be efficient when requirements are truly fixed.

How do you move from Waterfall to Kanban?

Start with what you do now: map your current process on a board, add WIP limits where work piles up, make policies explicit and deliver smaller items as soon as they are ready.

Can Kanban be used in construction projects?

Yes, for managing tasks, snagging lists and design requests within phases, while the overall project usually keeps a Waterfall plan because of physical dependencies.
