Cycle Time in Kanban: Formula, Examples and How to Reduce It

Cycle time in Kanban is the elapsed time a work item takes from the moment the team starts it to the moment it is finished. It answers the question customers and stakeholders ask most often: once you have started, how long until it is done? This guide covers where to start and stop the clock, how to calculate cycle time, how to analyse it with percentiles and scatterplots, how to turn it into a service level expectation and how to reduce it, using a worked dataset of 20 finished items.

Key takeaways

  • Cycle time is elapsed time from start to finish for one item, measured between two points on your board that the team agrees and writes down.
  • A common convention is finish date − start date + 1, in calendar days, so an item started and finished on the same day has a cycle time of 1 day.
  • Cycle time data is usually skewed by a few long items, so percentiles (50th, 85th) describe it better than the average.
  • The 85th percentile is a popular basis for a service level expectation, such as 85% of items finish within 11 days.
  • Watch work item age every day: it is the live version of cycle time and warns you before an item finishes late.
  • The biggest levers for reducing cycle time are lower WIP, smaller items, less waiting and faster handling of blockers.

What is cycle time in Kanban?

Cycle time is the amount of elapsed time a work item spends in progress: from the point where the team starts it to the point where it is finished. It is measured per item, in calendar time, and includes all the waiting, reviewing and rework in between, not just the hours someone was actively working on it.

The Kanban Guide, written by Daniel Vacanti and John Coleman, names cycle time as one of four flow measures every Kanban system should track, alongside work in progress (WIP), throughput and work item age. Together they describe how work flows through a system. Cycle time is the one that speaks most directly to the customer, because it tells them how long they can expect to wait once their request is under way.

Two cautions on terminology:

  • Manufacturing often uses cycle time to mean the time to complete one unit at a single process step, which is a different measure. In knowledge work it usually means the whole start-to-finish time of an item. See lean manufacturing for the factory meaning.
  • Kanban communities differ. Some Kanban Method material, for example, calls the time from the commitment point to delivery lead time. The label matters less than the definition: write down where your clock starts and stops.

For the difference between the two most commonly confused measures, see cycle time vs lead time.

Where cycle time starts and stops

Cycle time is only meaningful when everyone agrees on the start and finish points. These are policies the team chooses and records on its board, usually as the commitment point and the delivery point.

Start pointFinish pointWhat it measuresTypical use
Card enters In progressCard enters DoneTeam's working processThe most common definition
Card enters Ready (committed)Card is released to usersCommitment to delivery, including releaseTeams with a separate deployment step
Card enters DevelopmentCard enters ReviewTime in one part of the processDiagnosing a specific stage
Request is loggedCustomer receives itThe customer's full wait (lead time)Service levels for internal customers
  • Pick one primary definition and use it on every report, so trends are comparable.
  • You can track more than one cycle time, such as one for the whole process and one for Review, as long as each is labelled.
  • Record the dates automatically if you can. Digital tools log status changes; on a physical board, write the start and finish dates on the card. Our Kanban cards guide shows a simple layout.
  • Decide what happens with items that move backwards. Most teams keep the original start date, so rework counts as part of the item's cycle time.

How to calculate cycle time

The formula

Cycle time = finish date − start date + 1 (in days)

The + 1 is a convention, not a law. Without it, an item started and finished on the same day would have a cycle time of zero, which understates the time it took. Practitioners such as John Coleman, co-author of the Kanban Guide, recommend counting this way so that the minimum cycle time is one day. Some tools count elapsed time in hours or fractions of a day instead. Either is fine if you use it consistently and say which one you use.

Average cycle time = sum of the cycle times of finished items ÷ number of finished items. It is easy to calculate but, as the worked example below shows, often misleading on its own.

Calendar days or working days?

Our advice: use calendar days unless you have a strong reason not to. It is simpler, it is what your customers experience, and it avoids debates about which days counted.

Cycle time vs lead time, work item age, throughput and takt time

Cycle time is easiest to use when you are clear about how it differs from its neighbours.

MeasureWhat it isUnitQuestion it answers
Cycle timeElapsed time from start to finish of one finished itemDays per itemOnce started, how long does work take?
Lead timeElapsed time from request to deliveryDays per itemHow long does the customer wait in total?
Work item ageElapsed time since an unfinished item startedDays per itemWhich items in progress are at risk now?
ThroughputNumber of items finished per periodItems per weekHow much do we deliver?
WIPNumber of items started but not finishedItemsHow much are we juggling?
Takt timeAvailable time ÷ customer demandTime per itemHow often must we finish an item to keep up with demand?

Cycle time looks backwards at finished work; work item age looks at work still in progress; throughput counts rather than times.

Worked example: analysing 20 finished items

Over five weeks in September and early October, the same team finished 20 items, including the six above. Sorted from shortest to longest, their cycle times in days were:

1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 18, 27

The total is 141 days, so the average cycle time is 7.05 days. But the middle value, the median, is only 5 days, and 14 of the 20 items finished in 7 days or less. Two long items (18 and 27 days) pull the average up; remove the 27-day item and the average drops to 6 days. This right-skewed shape, many short items and a long tail of a few slow ones, is typical of knowledge work, and it is why flow practitioners prefer percentiles to averages.

Calculating percentiles step by step

A percentile tells you the cycle time within which a given share of items finished. A simple, transparent way to calculate one is the nearest-rank method:

  1. Sort the cycle times from shortest to longest.
  2. Multiply the number of items (n) by the percentile. For the 85th percentile with 20 items: 20 × 0.85 = 17.
  3. Round up to the next whole number if the result is not whole. Here 17 is already whole.
  4. Count along the sorted list to that position. The 17th value is 11 days.
  5. State it plainly: 85% of items finished in 11 days or less.
PercentilePosition (20 items)Cycle timePlain-English reading
50th (median)10th5 daysHalf of items finished within 5 days
70th14th7 daysSeven in ten finished within a week
85th17th11 daysMost items, but not the slow tail, finished within 11 days
95th19th18 daysNearly all finished within 18 days

Spreadsheet functions such as PERCENTILE.INC interpolate between values, so they can give slightly different answers (for example 11.3 instead of 11). Either is acceptable; use the same method every time.

Seeing the shape with a histogram

Grouping the same data into ranges shows the distribution at a glance.

The peak is at 3 to 4 days, with a tail stretching to 27. When the team looked at the two items in the tail, one had waited 12 days for a decision from another department and the other had been far larger than a typical item. Both are classic causes of long cycle times, and both suggest specific fixes: escalation rules for external decisions and splitting large work before it starts.

Splitting by work type

Bugs move much faster than features, so a single blended figure would mislead both. Where work types differ greatly, report cycle time separately for each, or use swimlanes for the types. With only six bugs, treat their figures as provisional until more data builds up.

How to read a cycle time scatterplot

A cycle time scatterplot is the standard chart for this data. Each dot is one finished item: its finish date on the horizontal axis and its cycle time on the vertical axis. Horizontal lines mark percentiles, typically the 50th, 85th and 95th.

In our example, a scatterplot would show most dots clustered between 1 and 7 days, an 85th percentile line at 11 days, and two dots well above it, at 18 and 27 days. Those two are the first items to discuss at the next review.

What you seeWhat it suggestsWhat to do
Dots clustered tightly, low on the chartPredictable, short cycle timesKeep current policies; consider lowering WIP limits
A few dots far above the 85th percentile lineOutliers with a special causeInvestigate each: blockers, size, dependencies
Dots drifting upwards over timeCycle times getting longerCheck whether WIP is rising; look at the cumulative flow diagram
Two distinct bands of dotsTwo kinds of work mixed togetherSplit the chart by work type or class of service
Gaps with no dots, then many at onceBatch deliveryRelease smaller batches more often
Wide vertical spread everywhereLow predictabilityRight-size items and limit WIP before forecasting
Dots creeping down after a policy changeThe change is workingMark the date on the chart and keep monitoring

Some tools draw a rolling average and standard deviation band instead of percentiles. Jira's control chart and Azure DevOps's cycle time widget both work this way. These are useful for trends, but because cycle time data is skewed, percentiles are usually easier to use for forecasts. To see where time is going inside the process, pair the scatterplot with a cumulative flow diagram.

Setting a service level expectation from cycle time

A service level expectation (SLE) is a forecast of how long an item should take once started, made of two parts: a period of elapsed time and a probability. The Kanban Guide describes it as based on historical cycle time and recommends making it visible as part of the team's definition of workflow.

  1. Collect at least 10 to 20 finished items of similar type, using one consistent definition of start and finish. With less data, use a best guess and say so.
  2. Calculate the 85th percentile (some teams use the 70th or 90th). In the example it is 11 days.
  3. Write the SLE in plain words: 85% of items are finished within 11 days of starting.
  4. Set separate SLEs for very different work types, for example 4 days for bugs and 13 days for features.
  5. Make it visible on the board, for example on the column header after your commitment point.
  6. Use it every day to spot items at risk (see work item age below), and when someone asks when an item will be done: once started, there is roughly an 85% chance it will be finished within 11 days.
  7. Review it every month or quarter, or after a major process change. Use a rolling window such as the last 30 to 50 items so it reflects the current process.
Tip

An SLE is a forecast, not a promise or a target. If people are pressured to hit it, they start gaming start dates or avoiding hard items, and the data stops meaning anything.

For forecasting batches of work, such as when 40 items will be done, use throughput and Monte Carlo methods.

Using cycle time every day: work item age

Cycle time only exists once an item is finished, so it is a lagging measure. Work item age, the elapsed time since an unfinished item started, is its leading counterpart and is calculated the same way: today − start date + 1.

Suppose it is Wednesday 14 October and the team has three items in progress, with an SLE of 85% within 11 days and a 70th percentile of 7 days.

ItemStartedAge todayAgainst the SLEAction
C-131Mon 5 Oct10 daysOne day from the 85th percentileDiscuss first at the daily meeting; swarm or remove the blocker
C-132Thu 8 Oct7 daysAt the 70th percentileCheck it is moving; ask what it needs
C-133Tue 13 Oct2 daysWell withinNo action needed

Reviewing age every day, oldest item first, is how teams stop a 10-day item becoming a 27-day one. A WIP aging chart shows this visually, with every in-progress item plotted by stage and age against the percentile lines. Helping finish an old item rather than starting a new one is the heart of Agile swarming.

Cycle time and Little's Law

Little's Law, published by John Little in 1961, links the three main flow measures:

Average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput

In the example, the team finished 20 items in about five weeks, roughly 4 items a week. If the board held about 4 items in progress on average, Little's Law gives an average cycle time of about 1 week, or 7 days, which agrees with the 7.05 days measured. The equation also explains why WIP limits work: if throughput stays the same and WIP halves, average cycle time roughly halves too.

The law describes averages over a period in which the system is stable: work arrives at about the rate it leaves, items that start eventually finish, and WIP is roughly the same at the start and end. Use it to understand cause and effect and to sense-check your numbers, not to forecast an individual item. For the visual version, see how to read cycle time from a cumulative flow diagram.

How to reduce cycle time

Most of an item's cycle time is usually spent waiting, not being worked on. Measuring flow efficiency, active time as a share of total time, often shows that waiting accounts for most of it. That is good news: reducing queues is usually easier than making anyone work faster.

Limit work in progress

Lower WIP limits gradually, one column at a time, and agree that when a column is full people help finish work downstream instead of starting more. Of all the levers, this one has the most direct link to cycle time through Little's Law.

Right-size items before they start

Large items dominate the tail of the distribution. Before committing to an item, ask whether it is likely to finish within your SLE; if not, split it into smaller pieces that each deliver something useful. Techniques for splitting user stories apply to any kind of work item.

Cut waiting and hand-offs

Make queues visible with split columns such as Review: waiting and Review: doing, then agree turnaround policies for them. Fewer hand-offs between people and teams mean fewer queues. A value stream map of the whole process often reveals waits that nobody on the team controls.

Deal with blockers and ageing items quickly

Flag blocked items the moment they stop, escalate external dependencies early, and keep a record of every blocker. Over a few months, blocker clustering shows which causes cost the most days, so you can fix the system rather than individual cases.

Symptom in the dataLikely causeAction to try
Long tail of very slow itemsOversized work or external dependenciesRight-size before starting; escalation policy for dependencies
Cycle time rising month on monthWIP creeping upLower WIP limits; stop starting, start finishing
Most time spent in one columnBottleneck stageSwarm, cross-train, apply the Theory of Constraints

Avoid the tempting shortcuts that only make the number look better: starting the clock later, closing items before they are really done, or splitting items into pieces that deliver nothing on their own.

Measuring cycle time in project management tools

Most digital boards calculate cycle time from status history. A few notes from vendor documentation (checked 25 October 2026):

  • Jira's control chart plots the time each work item spent in the statuses you select, with average, rolling average and standard deviation. Choose the statuses that match your agreed start and finish points. See our Jira review.
  • Azure DevOps has cycle time and lead time widgets. Microsoft defines cycle time as the time from when an item first enters an In Progress state to when it reaches Completed, and notes that reopened items keep their original start, so rework is included.
  • ClickUp offers cycle time cards for its dashboards. See our ClickUp review.
  • Spreadsheets work for any board: record start and finish dates, calculate finish − start + 1, and use percentile functions and a scatter chart.

Whatever the tool, check that its start and finish statuses match your written policy, and whether it counts working or calendar days.

Common cycle time mistakes

  • Relying on the average. A few long items distort it. Report the median and 85th percentile.
  • Changing the start or finish point without resetting the history, which makes trends meaningless.
  • Mixing very different work types in one figure, such as bugs and large features.
  • Measuring only active time. Cycle time is elapsed time; waiting is part of what customers experience and where most improvement lies.
  • Turning it into a target. Pressure to hit a number leads to gaming rather than improvement.
  • Using it to compare individuals or teams. It describes the system, not anyone's performance.
  • Looking only backwards. Watch work item age daily so you act before items finish late.
  • Ignoring items that never finish. Abandoned or long-stuck items disappear from cycle time statistics; review them separately.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cycle time?

There is no universal figure, because it depends on the size and type of work. What matters is that cycle time is short enough for your customers, predictable (a narrow spread) and stable or improving over time. Compare your own trend, not other teams.

Is cycle time the same as lead time?

No. Lead time usually starts when the request is made, while cycle time starts when work begins, so cycle time is part of lead time. Some communities use the terms differently, so always define the start and finish points. See cycle time vs lead time.

How do you calculate average cycle time?

Add the cycle times of all finished items in a period and divide by the number of items. For example, 20 items totalling 141 days give an average of 7.05 days. Report the median and 85th percentile alongside it, because averages are skewed by a few long items.

Why do some teams add one day to cycle time?

So that an item started and finished on the same day counts as 1 day rather than 0. It is a counting convention recommended by several flow practitioners; whichever you choose, apply it consistently.

Should weekends count in cycle time?

Most flow practitioners recommend calendar days, including weekends, because that is what customers experience and it avoids maintaining a working calendar. Working days are acceptable if you apply them consistently and say so.

What is the 85th percentile cycle time?

It is the cycle time within which 85% of past items finished. With 20 items sorted from shortest to longest, it is the 17th value. Teams often use it as a service level expectation.

Why use percentiles rather than the average?

Cycle time data usually has a long tail of slow items, so the average sits above what most items actually take. Percentiles give a plain-English statement, such as 85% within 11 days, that people can plan with.

How many items do you need before cycle time data is useful?

You can start learning from about 10 items, and 20 to 30 give a reasonably stable picture. Keep using a rolling window of recent items so the figures reflect your current process.

What is the difference between cycle time and takt time?

Cycle time is how long items actually take. Takt time is the pace needed to meet demand: available time divided by the number of items customers need. It comes from lean manufacturing.

Does blocked time count in cycle time?

Yes. Cycle time is elapsed time from start to finish, so time spent blocked or waiting is included. That is deliberate: blocked time is one of the main things teams can reduce.

What happens to cycle time when an item moves backwards?

Most teams and tools keep the original start date, so the rework counts towards the item's cycle time. Azure DevOps, for example, documents this behaviour for reactivated items.

Should cycle time be used to measure individual performance?

No. Cycle time reflects the whole system, including queues, dependencies and policies that individuals do not control. Using it to judge people encourages gaming and hides problems.

Can Scrum teams use cycle time?

Yes. Many Scrum teams track cycle time for backlog items within and across sprints, and Scrum.org publishes the Kanban Guide for Scrum Teams for exactly this. It helps reveal items that are too large to finish comfortably within a sprint.

How do you show cycle time on a Kanban board?

Write the start date on each card and the finish date when it reaches Done, or let your tool record status changes. Show the SLE on the board and highlight cards whose age is approaching it. See Kanban boards.
