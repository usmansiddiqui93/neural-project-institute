Cycle Time in Kanban: Definition, Calculation and How to Reduce It

Cycle time measures how long one work item takes from the moment work starts until it is finished. It is the metric customers care about most: "once you start, how long until I have it?"

By Neural Project InstitutePublished 5 October 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • Cycle time is the time from starting an item to finishing it.
  • Use percentiles to set service level expectations.
  • Lower WIP, smaller items and fewer waits reduce cycle time.

Definition and calculation

Cycle time = finish date − start date for a single item. Teams decide exactly where "start" and "finish" are on their board, usually the commitment point and the delivery point, and use the same definition consistently.

Tip

Example: a card enters In progress on Monday 3 June and reaches Done on Thursday 6 June. Its cycle time is 4 days if you count both start and end days, or 3 days if you count elapsed days. Pick one convention and stick to it.

Reading a cycle time scatterplot

A scatterplot shows one dot per finished item: the date it finished on the horizontal axis and its cycle time on the vertical. Horizontal percentile lines show, for example, that 50% of items finished within 4 days and 85% within 9 days. Outliers high on the chart are worth investigating.

Service level expectations

Teams often use the 85th percentile as a service level expectation: "85% of items are finished within 9 days of starting." It sets realistic expectations without detailed estimates. See Kanban planning.

How to reduce cycle time

  • Lower WIP limits: by Little's Law, less WIP shortens cycle time.
  • Split large items into smaller ones.
  • Remove waiting: most cycle time is often spent waiting, not working; see flow efficiency.
  • Tackle blockers quickly and analyse their causes; see blocker clustering.
  • Reduce handoffs with cross-functional teams.
  • Watch aging items daily with a WIP aging chart.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cycle time?

It depends on the work. What matters is that cycle time is predictable and trending down over time.

Is cycle time the same as lead time?

No. Lead time usually starts when the request is made; cycle time starts when work begins. See cycle time vs lead time.
