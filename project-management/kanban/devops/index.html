What Is DevOps? Principles, Practices and DORA Metrics

DevOps is a way of working in which the people who build software and the people who run it share responsibility for getting changes to users quickly and keeping the service reliable. It combines a culture of shared ownership with Lean flow, automation and fast feedback, and its delivery performance is most often measured with DORA's five software delivery metrics. This guide explains the principles behind DevOps, the practices that put them into effect, how to calculate the metrics from a deployment log, and what DevOps changes for project managers.

Key takeaways

  • DevOps is a set of cultural values and technical practices, not a tool, a job title or a framework with a rulebook. Its aim is fast, safe flow of changes from idea to production and fast feedback back again.
  • CALMS (Culture, Automation, Lean, Measurement, Sharing) and Gene Kim's Three Ways (flow, feedback, continual learning) are the two most common summaries of its principles.
  • DORA now measures software delivery with five metrics: change lead time, deployment frequency and failed deployment recovery time (throughput), plus change fail rate and deployment rework rate (instability). Lists of four metrics with "time to restore service" are out of date.
  • DORA's research finds that speed and stability tend to improve together. Use the metrics to track one service's improvement over time, not as targets or to rank teams.
  • For project managers, DevOps shifts control from large release gates and change boards towards small batches, automated checks and peer review, the approach DORA's research associates with better delivery performance.

What is DevOps?

DevOps is a set of cultural values, practices and tools that brings software development and IT operations together, so that one team, or closely aligned teams, build, release and run a service. It applies Lean and Agile thinking to the path from code change to production, using small batches, automation and fast feedback to deliver quickly and reliably.

The word joins development (writing and changing software) and operations (deploying, running, monitoring and supporting it). The idea is older than any tool: the people who change a system and the people who keep it running should work towards the same goal and see the same results.

DevOps is easy to misread, so three clarifications help:

  • It is not only a job title. Many organisations employ "DevOps engineers" who build pipelines and automation, but a team does not become DevOps by hiring one, or by renaming its operations group.
  • It is not a toolset. Pipelines, containers and monitoring tools support DevOps practices; buying them without changing how teams share responsibility tends to automate the old hand-offs.
  • It has no single official guide. Unlike Scrum (the Scrum Guide) or Kanban (the Kanban Guide), DevOps has no governing body or definitive text. The most widely used evidence base is the survey research of DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment), published annually since 2014 and now hosted at dora.dev.

The problem DevOps set out to solve

In a traditional IT organisation, developers are rewarded for delivering change and operations staff for keeping systems stable. Each group optimises for its own goal. Developers hand over large releases; operations, wary of the risk, add approval steps and release windows; releases become larger and rarer; and each one is riskier than the last. When something fails, the investigation turns into a dispute about whose fault it was.

DevOps breaks that loop by making both groups accountable for the same outcome: changes that reach users quickly and work once they get there. That shared goal is why DevOps sits naturally beside Lean and Kanban: all three treat waiting, hand-offs and large batches as the main enemies of flow.

DevOps principles: CALMS and the Three Ways

Two summaries of DevOps principles are used widely. They overlap, but they answer different questions: CALMS describes what an organisation needs to have in place, while the Three Ways describe the direction of improvement.

The CALMS model

CALMS extends an earlier acronym, CAMS (Culture, Automation, Measurement, Sharing). Jez Humble, co-author of *The DevOps Handbook*, is generally credited with adding the L for Lean.

ElementWhat it means in practiceTypical evidence that it is in place
CultureShared responsibility for outcomes between the people who build and the people who run a serviceDevelopers take part in incident response; operations staff join planning
AutomationBuilds, tests, environment set-up and deployments run without manual stepsAny engineer can deploy a tested change through the same pipeline
LeanSmall batches, limited work in progress and the removal of waitingChanges are merged at least daily; queues before review and release are short
MeasurementDecisions are based on flow, quality and service data rather than opinionThe team reviews delivery metrics and service health regularly
SharingKnowledge, tools and lessons move freely between teamsPost-incident reviews are published; runbooks and dashboards are shared

The Three Ways

Gene Kim set out the Three Ways in a 2012 article and later used them as the backbone of *The Phoenix Project* and *The DevOps Handbook*. Each Way implies specific practices:

WayPrinciplePractices that put it into effect
First Way: flow and systems thinkingOptimise the whole value stream from requirement to customer, not one departmentValue stream mapping, small batches, WIP limits, continuous delivery
Second Way: amplify feedback loopsCreate fast feedback from right to left, from production and users back to developmentAutomated testing, monitoring and alerting, peer review, telemetry on user behaviour
Third Way: continual experimentation and learningBuild a culture that takes informed risks, learns from failure and practises until improvements stickBlameless post-incident reviews, time reserved for improvement, controlled experiments

The First Way is where DevOps meets the theory of constraints: improving a stage that is not the bottleneck does little for overall flow. A team that automates its build while changes still wait three days for a manual approval has optimised the wrong step.

Core DevOps practices

No single practice makes a team "do DevOps". The practices below appear in DORA's capability catalogue and in most practitioner accounts. The right-hand column shows which principle each one serves, which is useful when deciding where to start.

PracticeWhat it doesMain principle served
Version control for everythingApplication code, configuration, scripts and infrastructure definitions are stored and reviewed in one historyFlow; enables every other practice
Continuous integration and trunk-based developmentDevelopers merge small changes into the main branch at least daily, with automated build and test on every mergeFlow and feedback
Continuous deliveryEvery change that passes the pipeline is releasable, so release becomes a business decision, not a technical projectFlow
Test automationFast, reliable automated tests run on every changeFeedback
Infrastructure as codeServers, networks and environments are defined in versioned files and created automaticallyFlow; reduces configuration drift
Monitoring and observabilityTeams see how the service behaves in production and are alerted before users report problemsFeedback
Loosely coupled architecture and teamsTeams can test and deploy their service without coordinating with many othersFlow
Working in small batchesFeatures are split so that each change can be finished, checked and released in hours or daysFlow and feedback
Blameless post-incident reviewsAfter an incident, the team asks what in the system allowed it, not who to blameLearning

DORA's guidance on small batches is concrete: if a batch of work takes more than a week to complete and check, it is too big. Smaller changes are easier to review, test, release and, when something goes wrong, to diagnose and reverse.

Tip

If you are choosing where to start, find the longest wait in your path to production first. A value stream map from commit to live usually shows that changes spend far more time waiting for review, testing environments or approval than being worked on.

How DevOps fits with Agile, Lean, Kanban, SRE and ITIL

DevOps borrows from several older bodies of practice and overlaps with newer ones. The table separates what each one is, so you can combine them without confusing their purposes.

ApproachWhat it isRelationship to DevOps
AgileA set of values and principles for delivering software iterativelyDevOps extends Agile's short feedback loops past "done in development" to release and operation
LeanA management philosophy focused on value, flow and the removal of wasteSupplies the L in CALMS and the First Way's focus on flow
KanbanA strategy for managing and improving the flow of work using visualisation, WIP control and flow metricsA common way to run DevOps and operations teams day to day
Continuous deliveryA specific engineering practice that keeps software always releasableOne of the core technical practices inside DevOps
Site reliability engineering (SRE)Google's approach to running services, using software engineering, service level objectives and error budgetsGoogle's SRE Workbook describes SRE as one concrete implementation of DevOps principles
ITIL / IT service managementA framework of service management practices, including change enablement and incident managementCompatible when change and incident practices are designed for small, frequent, automated changes

DevOps and Kanban

DevOps and Kanban reinforce each other. Kanban makes the whole value stream visible, including operational work such as incidents and maintenance that is often invisible on development boards; DORA lists visual management, visibility of work in the value stream and WIP limits among its capabilities. Kanban's flow metrics then describe the work end to end, while DORA's metrics focus on the stretch from commit to production.

The two sets of measures differ in scope. Lead time in Kanban usually starts when a request is accepted; DORA's change lead time starts only when code is committed. A team can have a change lead time of a few hours and still keep customers waiting weeks, because most of the delay happens before anyone writes code. Track both. Teams running a mix of planned work and interruptions will find practical policies, including reserved capacity and classes of service, in our guide to Agile IT operations.

DevSecOps and platform engineering

DevSecOps moves security into the same flow: dependency scanning, static analysis and policy checks run in the pipeline on every change, and security specialists help teams build safe defaults instead of inspecting finished releases. DORA calls this capability pervasive security.

Platform engineering provides internal, self-service platforms (pipelines, environments, observability, templates) so that each product team does not have to build its own. DORA's 2025 report found that 90% of surveyed organisations had adopted at least one internal platform, and linked platform quality to an organisation's ability to benefit from AI. A platform team is a product team whose customers are other developers; it fails when it becomes a new ticket queue between development and operations.

How DevOps performance is measured: the five DORA metrics

DORA defines five software delivery performance metrics, grouped into throughput (how many changes move through the system) and instability (how well deployments go). The list below follows DORA's metrics guide on dora.dev, last updated in January 2026, and matches our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs.

MetricGroupDefinitionUsual unit
Change lead timeThroughputTime from a change being committed to version control to that change being deployed to productionHours or days (median and percentiles)
Deployment frequencyThroughputHow often deployments to production happen, or the time between deploymentsDeployments per day or week
Failed deployment recovery timeThroughputTime to recover from a deployment that fails and needs immediate interventionMinutes or hours (median)
Change fail rateInstabilityShare of deployments that need immediate intervention, such as a rollback or hotfixPercentage of deployments
Deployment rework rateInstabilityShare of deployments that are unplanned and result from an incident in productionPercentage of deployments

Why older articles list four metrics

DORA's history of its metrics, published on dora.dev in January 2026, explains the changes. The research began in 2014 with four candidate measures: deployment frequency, lead time, mean time to recover (MTTR) and change fail rate. By 2015 all four were in use, split into throughput and stability groups, and they later became known as the "four keys". In 2023 MTTR, also called time to restore service, was renamed failed deployment recovery time and narrowed to failures caused by a change to production; the old measure mixed in outages with external causes, such as a data centre failure, that say nothing about delivery. In 2024 DORA added deployment rework rate and regrouped the five metrics into throughput and instability.

DORA also measures reliability (formerly availability), but treats it as a measure of operational performance rather than a sixth delivery metric. Its own history page notes that the 2021 report wrongly called reliability the fifth metric.

How to read the metrics

  • Speed and stability are not a trade-off. DORA reports that the metrics are generally correlated: teams that are strong on one tend to be strong on all five.
  • Measure one application or service at a time. Blending a mobile app, a batch data platform and a website into one number hides the differences that matter.
  • Compare a service with its own past, not with other teams. DORA advises using the metrics to track improvement over time rather than to rank teams, and notes that comparing very different applications can mislead.
  • Never set a single metric as a target. A team told to raise deployment frequency can split deployments artificially. Read the throughput and instability groups together, so that one cannot improve at the other's expense unnoticed.

DORA metrics are one category in a wider measurement set that also includes flow, quality, value and team health measures.

Worked example: calculating DORA metrics from a deployment log

Illustrative example. A product team runs one customer-facing web service. Over four weeks (20 working days) it made 20 production deployments. To keep the arithmetic simple, each deployment carried one change. Before calculating anything, the team wrote down its counting rules, because the same log can produce different numbers under different rules.

Counting ruleTeam's choice
Change lead time startsWhen the change is committed to the main branch
Change lead time stopsWhen the deployment completes in production
Time unitElapsed clock hours, including nights and weekends
Failed deploymentA deployment needing immediate intervention: rollback, hotfix or feature switched off
Rework deploymentAn unplanned deployment made because of a production incident (a hotfix counts here, not as a failure)
Recovery timeFrom detection of the failure to service restored for users, in minutes

Step 1: deployment frequency

Deployment frequency = number of production deployments ÷ length of period. With 20 deployments in 4 weeks: 20 ÷ 4 = 5 deployments per week, or 20 ÷ 20 = 1 per working day.

Step 2: change lead time

The 20 lead times, sorted, in hours: 3, 4, 5, 6, 6, 8, 9, 20, 22, 24, 26, 27, 30, 46, 50, 52, 70, 72, 75, 120. Lead time data is skewed by a few slow changes, so report the median and a high percentile rather than the mean.

  • Median (half of changes deployed within this time): the average of the 10th and 11th values, (24 + 26) ÷ 2 = 25 hours.
  • 85th percentile (nearest-rank method): rank = 0.85 × 20 = 17, so the 17th value, 70 hours. About 85% of changes reached production within 70 hours.
  • Mean for comparison: 675 ÷ 20 = 33.75 hours, pulled upwards by the 120-hour change.

Step 3: change fail rate and deployment rework rate

Three deployments needed immediate intervention: two were rolled back and one was fixed by a hotfix. Two deployments were unplanned: the hotfix itself, and an urgent fix for a production incident that users reported, caused by a long-standing defect rather than by any of this period's deployments.

  • Change fail rate = failed deployments ÷ total deployments × 100 = 3 ÷ 20 × 100 = 15%.
  • Deployment rework rate = unplanned deployments caused by production incidents ÷ total deployments × 100 = 2 ÷ 20 × 100 = 10%.

Step 4: failed deployment recovery time

The three failures took 35, 50 and 240 minutes to recover. The median is 50 minutes; the mean is (35 + 50 + 240) ÷ 3 = 108.3 minutes, more than twice the median because of one long recovery. With only three failures, report the individual values alongside any summary figure.

Interpreting the results

MetricResultUnit
Deployment frequency5Deployments per week
Change lead timeMedian 25; 85th percentile 70Hours
Failed deployment recovery timeMedian 50 (values 35, 50, 240)Minutes
Change fail rate15% of deployments
Deployment rework rate10% of deployments

The numbers raise questions rather than answer them. Half of the changes reached production within about a day, yet six of the twenty (30%) took more than 48 hours. When the team traced those six, most had waited over a day for code review. Waiting time, not deployment speed, was the constraint.

Following DORA's improvement approach, the team committed to one constraint: review waiting time. It agreed a working policy that open reviews are picked up before new work is started, tracked time to first review as a leading indicator, and planned to recalculate the five metrics after another four weeks. It also opened a blameless review of the 240-minute recovery, because one long recovery matters more to users than the median suggests.

Tip

Keep units apart when you report: frequency is a rate, lead and recovery times are durations, and fail and rework rates are percentages of deployments. Never add or average them into a single "DevOps score".

Two limitations apply. Twenty deployments is a small sample, so a single extra failure moves the change fail rate by 5 percentage points; look at the trend over several periods before drawing conclusions. And the figures describe one service only: they say nothing about whether customers value what was deployed, which needs outcome measures such as adoption or satisfaction.

How to start a DevOps improvement

DORA recommends treating improvement as a repeating cycle owned by the whole cross-functional team, rather than a tool roll-out. Adapted for a team starting out:

  1. Choose one service and set a baseline. DORA's online Quick Check gives a survey-based starting point; a month of deployment records gives a measured one.
  2. Map the path from commit to production. Record each step and how long changes wait before it. Include approvals, environment requests and manual testing.
  3. Agree the biggest constraint. Pick one: often manual testing, long-lived branches, environment provisioning or change approval.
  4. Turn it into a plan with leading indicators. For example, time to first code review, build duration or the share of tests automated. These move weeks before the DORA metrics do.
  5. Make the improvement work visible. Put it on the team's Kanban board alongside feature work, with capacity reserved for it.
  6. Check progress and repeat. Recalculate the metrics, discuss them in a retrospective, and move to the next constraint.

What DevOps changes for project managers

DevOps does not remove the need for planning, risk management or stakeholder communication, but it changes where control sits.

Change approval

Many organisations route every production change through a change advisory board (CAB). DORA's research found that heavyweight external approval, such as a CAB or senior-manager sign-off, was associated with worse software delivery performance, and found no evidence that it lowered change fail rates. Its 2019 report concluded that approval works best through peer review during development, recorded in the team's tools, supported by automated testing and monitoring. The CAB's role shifts towards coordinating across teams and deciding business trade-offs. Our guide to change control explains how to separate scope and contract changes, which still need formal decisions, from routine technical changes.

Release and delivery planning

When deployment is routine, release becomes a business decision about when users should see a feature, often controlled with feature flags. Plans shift from fixed release dates for large bundles to forecasts based on throughput and a release plan that is revised as data arrives. Deploying and releasing are no longer the same event.

Risk and reporting

Small, frequent changes carry less risk each, but they need different controls: automated tests, rollback procedures and monitoring replace large pre-release test phases. Record these controls as risk responses in the risk register. For reporting, the five DORA metrics and Kanban flow metrics give stakeholders a factual view of delivery capability; combine them with outcome measures so that reports show value as well as speed. Use a root cause analysis template to structure post-incident reviews without assigning blame.

DevOps changes how software reaches users, but the surrounding project still needs a life cycle, estimates and governance. See our guide to software project management for how those fit together.

AI and DevOps: what the 2025 DORA research found

DORA's 2025 report, *State of AI-assisted Software Development* (published 23 September 2025, based on survey responses from nearly 5,000 technology professionals), found that 90% of respondents used AI at work. Its central finding is that AI acts as an amplifier: it strengthens teams with good practices and intensifies the problems of teams without them.

  • AI adoption showed a positive relationship with software delivery throughput and product performance.
  • It continued to show a negative relationship with delivery stability: producing more change faster exposes weak testing, version control and feedback loops downstream.
  • Teams with loosely coupled architectures and fast feedback saw gains; tightly coupled systems with slow processes saw little benefit.

The practical lesson is that AI coding assistants do not replace DevOps foundations. DORA's guidance on small batches specifically warns against oversized AI-generated pull requests, which are harder to review. Generated code still needs human review, automated tests and clear accountability for what reaches production; confidential code and data also need clear rules about which AI tools may see them.

Limitations and when DevOps is harder to apply

DevOps principles apply widely, but some contexts limit how far the practices can go. The continuous delivery guide shows how teams adapt pipelines to regulated, embedded and legacy settings; the points below concern DevOps as a whole.

  • Regulated or safety-critical software may require formal verification and release certification, so production releases stay infrequent. Shared ownership, automation and fast feedback still apply before release, and DORA lists "using industry as a shield against improving" as a pitfall.
  • Small teams with little operational load may gain more from version control, automated testing and a simple pipeline than from a platform team or an SRE practice.
  • Non-software projects can borrow the ideas (small batches, fast feedback, blameless learning) but not the DORA metrics, which are defined for software deployments.

Common DevOps mistakes

  • Creating a separate "DevOps team" between development and operations. This adds a third silo and another hand-off. A platform team that offers self-service tools is different from a team that performs deployments on others' behalf.
  • Buying tools before changing ownership. A pipeline that ends at a manual sign-off three days later has automated the fast part of the process.
  • Automating a broken process. Remove unnecessary steps and approvals first, then automate what remains.
  • Using DORA metrics as targets or league tables, or tracking speed alone. Both invite gaming and hide instability, as explained under how to read the metrics.
  • Mixing old and new definitions. "Time to restore service" also counted outages with external causes; failed deployment recovery time counts only failures caused by a change. A trend that switches definition part-way is not comparable.
  • Blaming individuals after incidents. People stop reporting near misses, and the organisation loses the information it needs to learn. Capture what you learn with a lessons learned process.

DORA metrics cover only the stretch from commit to production. See our guide to Kanban metrics for the flow measures that cover the rest of the journey.

Frequently asked questions

What is DevOps in simple terms?

DevOps is a way of working in which the people who build software and the people who run it share responsibility for releasing changes quickly and keeping the service reliable, using small batches, automation and fast feedback.

Is DevOps a role or a culture?

Primarily a culture and set of practices. Many organisations also employ DevOps engineers, who usually build and maintain deployment pipelines, infrastructure automation and monitoring, but hiring one does not make a team DevOps on its own.

When did DevOps start?

The term spread after Patrick Debois organised the first devopsdays conference in Ghent, Belgium, on 30–31 October 2009. The underlying ideas grew from Agile and Lean practice in the preceding years.

What does CALMS stand for in DevOps?

Culture, Automation, Lean, Measurement and Sharing. It extends the earlier CAMS acronym; Jez Humble is generally credited with adding Lean.

What are the Three Ways of DevOps?

Gene Kim's three principles: flow and systems thinking from development to the customer, amplified feedback loops from production back to development, and a culture of continual experimentation and learning.

What are the DORA metrics?

Five software delivery metrics: change lead time, deployment frequency and failed deployment recovery time (throughput), and change fail rate and deployment rework rate (instability). Deployment rework rate was added in 2024.

Why did DORA replace time to restore service?

In 2023 DORA renamed mean time to restore as failed deployment recovery time and limited it to failures caused by a change to production. The old measure also counted outages with external causes, which do not reflect delivery performance.

Is DevOps part of Agile?

DevOps extends Agile ideas beyond development into release and operations. Agile shortens the loop for building software; DevOps shortens the loop for getting it safely to users and learning from how it behaves.

What is the difference between DevOps and continuous delivery?

Continuous delivery is a specific engineering practice that keeps software always releasable. DevOps is the broader culture and set of practices, of which continuous delivery is a central part.

How is DevOps different from SRE?

Site reliability engineering is Google's approach to running services with software engineering, service level objectives and error budgets. Google's SRE Workbook presents it as one concrete way of implementing DevOps principles.

What is a blameless post-incident review?

A review after an incident that asks what in the system allowed the problem to happen, rather than who caused it, so that people share information openly and the team can fix the underlying causes.
