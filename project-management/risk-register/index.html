Risk Register: What It Is, Columns, Example and Steps

A risk register is the document or tool where a project records every identified risk, with its score, owner, planned response and current status. It is the working record of project risk management: the place the team looks to decide what to worry about this week. This guide explains the columns to use, how to score and write entries, a full example, and an eight-step process for creating and maintaining one.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • Last updated 25 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • A risk register records each risk with a clear description, probability, impact, score, owner, response and status.
  • Write every risk as cause, event and effect, and agree scoring scales before anyone scores anything.
  • Every risk needs one named owner and specific actions with dates; a response with no owner is only a hope.
  • Review the register on a fixed rhythm, focusing on the highest scores and on anything that has changed.
  • When a risk happens it becomes an issue: close it in the register and log it in the issue log.
  • A short, current register that people use is worth more than a long one that nobody reads.

What is a risk register?

A risk register is a structured list of a project's identified risks. For each risk it records what could happen, how likely and how serious it is, who owns it, what is being done about it and where it stands now. It is created during planning and updated throughout the project, so it always shows the current risk picture.

You will also hear it called a risk log. The two terms mean the same thing in most organisations. Some teams keep risks inside a wider RAID log (risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies), but the information recorded about each risk is the same.

A risk register holds both threats (risks with a negative effect) and opportunities (risks with a positive effect). Many registers contain only threats, which means the team misses chances to finish earlier, cheaper or better. If you are not sure what counts as a risk, start with our guide to what project risk is.

How the standards describe it

  • PMI. In the PMBOK Guide (6th edition), the risk register is first produced by the Identify Risks process, holding the identified risks, potential owners and potential responses. Later analysis and response planning add scores, priorities and agreed actions. See our PMBOK Guide overview.
  • PRINCE2. PRINCE2 has always used a risk register. In PRINCE2 7th edition it is kept as one of the records inside the project log, which the project manager maintains. PRINCE2 also distinguishes the risk owner (accountable for managing the risk) from the risk actionee (who carries out a specific response). Read more in our PRINCE2 guide.
  • ISO 31000. The international risk management standard asks organisations to record and report their risk management activity, but it does not prescribe a register format. A risk register is the most common way projects meet that expectation.

Why a risk register matters

A register is not paperwork for its own sake. Kept well, it does six useful jobs.

BenefitWhat it gives you in practice
VisibilityEvery known risk is in one place, so the team, sponsor and PMO see the same picture
PrioritisationScores show which risks deserve time and money now and which only need watching
AccountabilityEach risk has a named owner and dated actions, so nothing falls between people
Better reservesScores and cost impacts give a sound basis for contingency reserve and schedule buffers
Early warningTriggers tell the team what signs to watch for before a risk turns into an issue
Organisational learningClosed risks and what happened to them feed lessons learned for future projects

It is also evidence: when an auditor or a new sponsor asks how risks were handled, the register shows what was known, when, and what was decided.

There is no single mandatory format. The table below lists the columns most project registers use, marked as essential or optional. Start with the essential ones; add optional columns only if someone will actually use them.

How to write a risk description

The description is the most important field and the one most often written badly. Use a cause, event, effect structure: because of a known fact (cause), something uncertain may happen (event), which would affect an objective (effect). This makes the risk specific enough to score and to act on.

Weak entryWhy it is weakBetter entry
Budget overrunAn outcome, not a risk; no cause, so no obvious actionBecause the supplier quote is valid only until 31 January, prices may rise if the contract is signed late, adding up to £12,000 to costs
ResourcesA category, not an eventBecause the only database specialist is also assigned to another project, they may be unavailable in sprint 4, delaying migration by two weeks
Testing might be lateNo cause and no effect on objectivesBecause the test environment is shared with the finance team, access may be restricted during year-end, delaying user acceptance testing and go-live
The client is difficultA judgement about people, not a riskBecause the client has not yet named a decision-maker, approvals may take longer than the five days in the plan, pushing back each design milestone
Tip

If you cannot think of an action for a risk, the description is probably too vague. Rewrite it until the cause is clear.

Probability and impact scales

Agree the scales during planning and record them in the project management plan. Without written definitions, one person's 'likely' is another person's 'possible' and the scores mean nothing. The scale below is a sensible starting point; adjust the money and time values to the size of your project.

ScoreProbabilityImpact on costImpact on scheduleImpact on quality or scope
1Very unlikely (under 10%)Under 1% of budgetUnder one weekBarely noticeable
2Unlikely (10-30%)1-3% of budgetOne to two weeksMinor; sponsor would accept it
3Possible (30-50%)3-5% of budgetTwo to four weeksNoticeable; needs sponsor approval
4Likely (50-70%)5-10% of budgetOne to two monthsSignificant; affects a key deliverable
5Very likely (over 70%)Over 10% of budgetOver two monthsDeliverable unusable or objective missed

When a risk affects more than one objective, score impact on the dimension where it is worst. A risk with a small cost effect but a two-month delay scores 4 for impact, not 2.

Risk score and priority bands

Multiply probability by impact to get a score from 1 to 25, then group scores into bands so everyone knows what each band requires. These bands match our free risk register template:

BandScoreWhat it means for the project
High15-25Active response agreed now; owner reports on it at every review; sponsor informed
Medium8-14Response planned; reviewed regularly; escalate if the score rises
Low1-7Accept and monitor; review less often; close when it can no longer occur

Scores are a tool for ranking, not precise measurements. A risk scored 12 is not exactly twice as serious as one scored 6. Where you need numbers in money or days, use expected monetary value or Monte Carlo simulation, and use sensitivity analysis to see which risks drive the result.

Risk register example

Here is an extract from the register for a fictional project: replacing a mid-sized company's customer relationship management (CRM) system over nine months with a budget of £250,000. It shows six entries, including one opportunity.

IDRisk descriptionPIScoreOwnerResponseStatus
R-001Because the vendor is changing its licence model in Q2, licence costs may rise, adding up to 6% to the budget339Procurement leadMitigate: negotiate a fixed price for 24 months before contract signatureOpen
R-002Because customer data sits in three legacy systems with different formats, migration may take longer than planned, delaying go-live by a month4416Data leadMitigate: run a trial migration in month 3; budget two extra weeks of data cleansingOpen
R-003Because sales staff are busiest in December, they may not attend training before the January launch, reducing adoption3412Change managerAvoid: move launch to February; record short training videosOpen
R-004Because only one engineer understands the legacy integration, their absence could stall interface work for weeks2510Delivery managerMitigate: pair a second engineer and document the interfaces by month 2Monitoring
R-005Because the regulator is consulting on new data rules, reporting fields may need to change during the project236Compliance leadAccept: monitor the consultation monthly; hold £5,000 contingencyMonitoring
R-006 (opportunity)Because the vendor offers a pre-built connector for our finance system, we may be able to drop a custom interface, saving about £15,000339Solution architectExploit: confirm the connector covers our requirements in the design phaseOpen

How to read the example

  • Sort by score. R-002 (16) is the only high risk, so it gets the most attention at the next review.
  • Look at the response type. R-003 is avoided by changing the plan, which removes the December clash entirely. That is often cheaper than mitigation.
  • Accept does not mean ignore. R-005 is accepted, but it still has an owner, a monitoring action and a reserve.
  • Opportunities score the same way. R-006 uses probability and benefit instead of probability and harm, and it has an owner and an action like any threat.
  • Every action is specific. Each response says what will be done and by when, so the owner can report progress in a sentence.

How to create a risk register in 8 steps

The steps below work for most projects, whether you use a spreadsheet or project management software. Steps 1 to 6 happen during planning; steps 7 and 8 continue until the project closes.

  1. Agree the structure. Choose your columns, probability and impact scales, score bands and review frequency. Write them into the risk management plan or the project management plan so they stay consistent.
  2. Identify risks. Run a workshop with the team and key stakeholders. Use prompts such as the work breakdown structure, the schedule, assumptions, past lessons learned, supplier contracts and a category checklist. Expert judgement and a fishbone diagram help uncover causes. Capture everything first; filter later.
  3. Write each risk properly. Turn each idea into a cause, event and effect statement. Merge duplicates and drop items that are certain (those are issues or constraints, not risks).
  4. Score probability and impact. Score as a group, using the agreed scales, and record the reasoning for high scores. Let the spreadsheet calculate the score.
  5. Assign owners. Give each risk one named owner who has the knowledge and authority to act, not just the project manager by default.
  6. Plan responses. For every high and medium risk, choose a strategy and write specific actions with dates. Estimate the residual score and note any secondary risks the response creates. Add triggers and, for serious threats, a contingency plan.
  7. Review and update. At every review, update scores, check actions, add new risks and close those that can no longer happen. Record the review date on each entry you touch.
  8. Report and close. Summarise the top risks in the project status report. When a risk occurs, record it in the issue log and mark the risk as occurred. At project closure, archive the register and use it in the lessons learned review.
Tip

Book the first review before the identification workshop ends. Registers fail most often in the weeks after they are created, when nobody looks at them again.

If a planned response would change scope, cost or schedule, it may need approval through change control before it is carried out.

Worked example: one risk from identification to closure

A register is a living document. This example follows risk R-002 from the CRM project through four reviews to show how an entry changes over time.

DateEventPIScoreStatus and note
10 FebRaised in the identification workshop; trial migration planned for month 34416Open. High; owner reports weekly
14 AprTrial migration moves 92% of records cleanly; the remainder needs manual fixes3412Open. Probability reduced; data cleansing extended by one week
9 JunCleansing complete; second rehearsal succeeds within the planned window236Monitoring. Residual risk within tolerance; contingency held
30 JunLive migration completed on schedule---Closed. Risk can no longer occur; result noted for lessons learned

Notice three things. The score falls because the responses worked, not because someone was optimistic. Each change is backed by evidence (the trial and rehearsal results). And the risk is closed only when the migration is over, not when the score becomes low.

Tip

Keep a short history note on important risks. Being able to show how a score changed, and why, is invaluable at gate reviews and audits.

Who owns and updates the risk register

The project manager owns the register as a document, but they should not write every entry or own every risk. Shared ownership is what keeps it accurate. A RACI matrix is a good way to agree these responsibilities.

RoleResponsibility for the register
Project managerMaintains the register, runs reviews, checks quality of entries, reports top risks and escalates when needed
Risk ownerAccountable for one or more risks: keeps the entry current, makes sure actions happen, reports changes
ActioneeCarries out a specific response action and tells the owner when it is done (a PRINCE2 term; many teams merge it with owner)
Project teamRaises new risks at any time and contributes to scoring and responses
Sponsor or project boardSets risk tolerances, approves responses that need money or scope change, accepts significant residual risk
PMOProvides the standard format and scales, reviews registers across projects and spots shared risks; see PMO

Risks outside the project's authority, such as a change in company strategy, should be escalated to the person or body who can act on them, usually the sponsor, programme or portfolio. Record the escalation and who accepted it, then remove it from active management in the project register. Clear escalation routes are part of good project governance.

How to run a risk register review

Reviews are where the register earns its keep. A well-run review is short, focused on change and ends with clear actions.

A 30-minute review agenda

  1. New risks (5 minutes). Anyone may add a risk. Write it properly, score it and give it an owner before moving on.
  2. High risks (10 minutes). Each owner of a high risk gives a one-line update: actions done, actions due, any change in score.
  3. Changes and triggers (5 minutes). Has any trigger fired? Has any medium or low risk moved? Have any responses created secondary risks?
  4. Close and archive (5 minutes). Close risks that can no longer occur and move occurred risks to the issue log.
  5. Escalations and reporting (5 minutes). Agree what goes to the sponsor or board and what goes in the status report.

How often to review

Project typeSuggested review rhythm
Short project (under three months)Weekly, as part of the team meeting
Typical project (three to 18 months)Fortnightly in depth, with high risks checked weekly
Large or regulated projectMonthly formal review plus weekly owner updates; board review at each stage gate
Agile teamAt sprint planning or the retrospective, plus during backlog refinement

Whatever the rhythm, update the register immediately when something significant changes, such as a supplier failing, a key person leaving or a regulator publishing new rules.

Risk register vs issue log, RAID log and risk matrix

Several related tools are often confused. The difference that matters most is between a risk (something that might happen) and an issue (something that has happened).

Risk registerIssue logRAID logRisk matrix
What it holdsUncertain future eventsProblems happening nowRisks, assumptions, issues and dependenciesA grid showing probability against impact
Main questionWhat might happen?What is wrong now and who is fixing it?What do we need to keep track of?How serious is each risk, at a glance?
Level of detailDetailed, one row per riskDetailed, one row per issueOften summary levelVisual only; no actions or owners
RelationshipOccurred risks move to the issue logMay reveal new risksCan replace separate logs on small projectsUsually drawn from the register's scores

A risk assessment is different again. It is the activity of identifying and analysing risks, often for one decision, site or activity (common in health and safety). Its results can be recorded in a risk register, but the register is the ongoing record, not the assessment itself.

Tailoring the risk register to your project

The right level of detail depends on the size, complexity and risk of the project. Scale the register up or down rather than using the same format everywhere.

ContextWhat to keepWhat to add or drop
Small, low-risk projectID, description, score, owner, action, statusDrop category, proximity and residual score; review in the weekly meeting; a RAID log may be enough
Typical projectAll essential columns plus trigger and residual scoreAdd cost impact in money for high risks to size contingency
Large or regulated projectFull column set with history and approvalsAdd quantitative analysis, links to the schedule and budget, formal acceptance records and audit trail
Agile teamA lightweight register or risk cards on the boardTurn risk responses into backlog items; discuss risks during refinement and planning; see product backlog
Programme or portfolioProject registers plus a summary registerRecord cross-project and aggregated risks at programme level; escalate common risks from project registers

Whatever the scale, keep three things: one owner per risk, specific actions with dates, and a regular review.

Tools for keeping a risk register

Most projects start with a spreadsheet, and for many that is all they need. A spreadsheet is free, flexible and familiar. Its weaknesses show up as projects grow: version control, no automatic reminders for owners, and no easy way to link risks to tasks.

OptionWorks well whenWatch out for
Spreadsheet (Excel or Google Sheets)One project, a small team, simple reportingMultiple copies, missed updates, manual reporting
Project management softwareSeveral projects, owners who need reminders, risks linked to tasks and dashboardsSet up custom fields and a standard form before rolling out
Specialist risk or GRC softwareEnterprise or regulated environments with formal audit needsMore set-up and training; may be more than a single project needs

If you move the register into project management software, look for custom fields (probability, impact, calculated score), request forms for raising risks, dashboards and automatic reminders. Tools such as Wrike support this kind of set-up well for teams managing several projects.

Common risk register mistakes

  • Vague descriptions. 'Budget' or 'resources' cannot be scored or acted on. Fix: rewrite as cause, event and effect.
  • Recording issues as risks. If it has already happened, it belongs in the issue log. Fix: check every new entry for uncertainty.
  • Everyone owns it, so nobody does. Teams or departments listed as owners. Fix: one named person per risk.
  • Scoring without scales. Scores drift and cannot be compared. Fix: publish the scale definitions next to the register.
  • Write once, read never. The register is built at kick-off and not opened again. Fix: put risk review on every meeting agenda.
  • Only threats. Opportunities are never recorded, so never pursued. Fix: ask 'what could go better than planned?' in every workshop.
  • Responses with no actions. 'Mitigate' on its own is not a plan. Fix: write the action, the actionee and the date.
  • Too many risks. A list of 300 items hides the 15 that matter. Fix: merge duplicates, close dead risks and report the top ten.
  • Scores that never change. If nothing moves, nobody is updating it. Fix: record the review date and challenge entries untouched for a month.
  • Deleting closed risks. History is lost. Fix: mark as closed and archive; use them in lessons learned.

To check a register you have inherited, start with owners and dated actions: fixing those makes the biggest difference.

Frequently asked questions

What is a risk register in simple terms?

It is a list of the things that might go wrong (or better than expected) on a project, with how likely and serious each one is, who is dealing with it and what they are doing about it.

What should a risk register include?

At minimum: ID, description (cause, event, effect), probability, impact, score, owner, response, actions with dates and status. Many teams add category, trigger, contingency plan, residual score and the date last reviewed.

Who is responsible for the risk register?

The project manager maintains it and runs reviews. Individual risk owners keep their own entries up to date, and the sponsor or board sets tolerances and accepts significant risks.

How often should a risk register be updated?

Review it on a fixed rhythm, typically weekly or fortnightly, and update it immediately when something significant changes. Large projects often add a formal monthly review and a review at each stage gate.

Is a risk register the same as a risk log?

Yes. The terms are used interchangeably. Some organisations keep risks in a RAID log instead, but the information about each risk is the same.

What is the difference between a risk register and a risk matrix?

The register is the detailed list with owners and actions. The risk matrix (or probability and impact grid) is a visual summary that plots each risk by its scores so you can see priorities at a glance.

What is the difference between a risk register and a risk assessment?

A risk assessment is the activity of identifying and analysing risks, often for a specific decision or activity. The risk register is the ongoing record where the results are kept and managed.

Do small projects need a risk register?

Yes, but it can be very light: a short list with description, score, owner, action and status, reviewed in the weekly meeting. A simple RAID log often does the job.

Should opportunities go in the risk register?

Yes. Recording opportunities, such as a cheaper supplier or a reusable component, alongside threats means the team pursues them deliberately instead of by luck.

When should a risk be closed?

Close it when it can no longer occur, for example because the relevant phase is complete. If it has occurred, mark it as occurred and log it in the issue log. Archive closed risks rather than deleting them.

How many risks should a risk register have?

There is no correct number. Most projects have somewhere between ten and fifty active risks. If the list grows much longer, merge duplicates and focus reporting on the top ten.

Does PRINCE2 use a risk register?

Yes. PRINCE2 has always used a risk register. In PRINCE2 7th edition it is kept as part of the project log maintained by the project manager, alongside the issue register and other records.
