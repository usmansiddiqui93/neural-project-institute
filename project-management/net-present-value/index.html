Net Present Value (NPV): Formula, Examples and Uses

Net present value (NPV) is the value, in today's money, of all the cash a project is expected to spend and earn over its life. You discount each future cash flow at a required rate of return and add them up: if the total is positive, the project is expected to earn more than that rate and create value. This guide shows you how to calculate it step by step, with a full worked example, a comparison of competing projects and the spreadsheet traps to avoid.

Key takeaways

  • NPV = the sum of each period's cash flow divided by (1 + r)^t, including the year-zero investment at face value.
  • A positive NPV means the project beats the required return; between mutually exclusive options, the higher NPV usually wins.
  • The discount rate is a business decision: get it from finance and keep it consistent across all the projects you compare.
  • NPV, IRR, payback and ROI can rank the same projects differently; NPV is the most reliable guide to value created.
  • Always test sensitivity: show how far benefits can fall, or costs rise, before NPV turns negative.
  • In Excel and Google Sheets, =NPV() assumes the first value is a year away, so add the year-zero cash flow separately.

What is net present value (NPV)?

Net present value (NPV) is the sum of a project's future cash inflows and outflows, each discounted back to its value today, minus the initial investment. Discounting uses a required rate of return, called the discount rate. A positive NPV means the project is expected to return more than that rate; a negative NPV means it is expected to return less.

The idea behind NPV is the time value of money. $1 today is worth more than $1 in three years' time, for three reasons:

  • Opportunity cost. Money you have now can be invested elsewhere and earn a return.
  • Inflation. Prices tend to rise, so future money usually buys less.
  • Risk. A promised future benefit may not arrive in full, or at all.

Discounting reverses compounding. If $7,938 invested at 8% grows to $10,000 in three years (7,938 x 1.08^3), then $10,000 received in three years is worth about $7,938 today at an 8% rate. NPV applies that logic to every year of a project and nets the results into one figure.

In project work, NPV usually appears in the business case and in project selection. It helps a sponsor or portfolio board answer one question: is this investment worth more than it costs, once timing and the cost of money are taken into account? It sits alongside the other appraisal measures covered in our guides to cost-benefit analysis and return on investment.

The NPV formula

NPV = CF0 + CF1 / (1 + r)^1 + CF2 / (1 + r)^2 + ... + CFn / (1 + r)^n

Or, written as a sum: NPV = sum of CFt / (1 + r)^t, for t = 0 to n.

SymbolMeaningExample
CFtNet cash flow in period t (inflows minus outflows)-$250,000 in year 0; +$80,000 in year 2
CF0Cash flow today, usually the initial investment (negative)-$250,000
rDiscount rate per period, as a decimal9% = 0.09
tPeriod number; 0 is today, 1 is the end of year one0, 1, 2 ... n
nNumber of periods in the appraisal5 years

You will often see the formula written as "sum of discounted cash flows minus the initial investment". It is the same calculation: the year-zero cash flow is not discounted, because (1 + r)^0 = 1.

The discount factor

The discount factor for period t is 1 / (1 + r)^t. Multiply any cash flow by its factor to get its present value. The table shows factors for common rates; notice how quickly distant cash flows shrink at higher rates.

Tip

The standard formula assumes each cash flow arrives at the end of its period. If your benefits arrive steadily through the year, the end-of-year convention slightly understates them. That is usually acceptable for a business case, but state the assumption, and use monthly periods or dated cash flows (XNPV) when timing really matters.

How to calculate NPV step by step

  1. Define the options. Include the "do nothing" or "do minimum" baseline. NPV measures the difference a project makes, so you need something to compare against.
  2. Choose the appraisal period. Use the useful life of the asset or solution, typically three to ten years for business change and IT projects. Longer for infrastructure.
  3. List the cash flows for each period. Include capital costs, implementation costs, running costs, savings, extra revenue and any residual value. Use your cost estimate for the cost side.
  4. Net them per period. Inflows minus outflows for year 0, year 1, year 2 and so on.
  5. Agree the discount rate with finance. Use the organisation's hurdle rate or cost of capital, and check whether it is real or nominal (see below).
  6. Calculate the discount factor for each period: 1 / (1 + r)^t.
  7. Multiply each net cash flow by its factor to get its present value.
  8. Add the present values. The total is the NPV.
  9. Test sensitivity. Recalculate at higher and lower rates, with lower benefits and higher costs, and with a delayed start.
  10. Document the assumptions so reviewers can challenge them and so the figure can be checked again at each stage gate.

What to include in the cash flows

Most NPV errors come from what goes into the cash flows, not from the arithmetic. Use incremental cash (the cash that changes because of the project) rather than accounting profit.

IncludeExclude
Capital spend: equipment, software, build costsSunk costs already spent, such as last year's feasibility study
Implementation: labour, contractors, training, data migrationDepreciation and amortisation (non-cash; count the purchase instead)
Running costs: licences, maintenance, support, extra staffFinancing costs such as loan interest (the discount rate already reflects the cost of money)
Savings: labour, materials, avoided costs, decommissioned systemsOverheads that would be paid anyway
Extra revenue or marginBenefits you cannot link to the project
Opportunity costs, such as using a building you could otherwise rent outDouble-counted benefits (the same saving claimed by two projects)
Residual or resale value, and end-of-life or decommissioning costs

Tax effects (tax relief on capital allowances, tax on extra profit) belong in a full corporate appraisal. If you are not sure how to treat them, ask finance rather than leaving them out silently.

Worked example: warehouse automation project

A distribution company is considering a warehouse automation project. Finance has set a discount rate of 9% for projects of this risk.

  • Year 0: $250,000 for equipment, software, installation and training.
  • Year 1: net cash flow of $45,000. Labour and error savings are lower while the team ramps up, less maintenance and licences.
  • Years 2 to 4: net cash flows of $80,000, $85,000 and $85,000 once the system is fully used.
  • Year 5: $85,000 of net savings plus a $20,000 residual value for the equipment, so $105,000.
YearNet cash flowDiscount factor at 9%Present valueCumulative PV
0-$250,0001.0000-$250,000-$250,000
1$45,0000.9174$41,284-$208,716
2$80,0000.8417$67,334-$141,381
3$85,0000.7722$65,636-$75,746
4$85,0000.7084$60,216-$15,529
5$105,0000.6499$68,243$52,713

The NPV is about $52,713. The present value of all the future net cash flows is $302,713, which is more than the $250,000 investment. The project is expected to return more than 9% a year and add around $52,700 of value in today's money.

What the table also tells you

  • Undiscounted gain: total net cash of $400,000 against $250,000 spent, a $150,000 surplus and a simple ROI of 60%. Discounting reduces the gain to about $52,700, which shows how much timing matters over five years.
  • Payback period: cumulative undiscounted cash turns positive during year four, at about 3.5 years (3 + 40,000 / 85,000).
  • Discounted payback: cumulative PV turns positive during year five, at about 4.2 years (4 + 15,529 / 68,243). The project only clears the 9% hurdle in its final year, so the residual value and the year-five savings matter.
  • IRR: the rate at which NPV equals zero is about 15.9%, well above the 9% hurdle.
Tip

Notice that without the $20,000 residual value, NPV falls to about $39,700. Residual values are often guesses. Show the NPV with and without them, so decision makers can see how much depends on the assumption.

Interpreting the result

NPVWhat it meansTypical decision
PositiveReturns exceed the required rate; the project adds valueAccept, subject to risk, strategy and funding
ZeroReturns exactly equal the required rateFinancially neutral; decide on non-financial grounds
NegativeReturns fall short of the required rateReject on financial grounds unless there is a mandatory, regulatory or strategic reason

A negative NPV does not always mean "do not do it". Compliance projects, safety work and replacing unsupported systems often have negative NPVs but are still necessary. In that case, NPV helps you choose the least costly way to meet the requirement: compare the options and pick the one with the highest (least negative) NPV.

What is a good NPV?

Any positive NPV is good in the sense that the project beats the hurdle rate. But the size of the number depends on the size of the project. An NPV of $50,000 is excellent on a $100,000 investment and marginal on a $5 million one. Two ways to put it in context:

  • Profitability index (PI) = present value of future cash flows / initial investment. In the example, PI = 302,713 / 250,000 = 1.21: each $1 invested returns $1.21 in today's money. PI above 1 means a positive NPV.
  • Headroom: how far the key assumptions can move before NPV reaches zero. A project whose NPV survives a 20% fall in benefits is more robust than one that turns negative at 5%.

Comparing projects with NPV

The measures disagree. Payback and IRR favour C because it is small and pays back quickly. NPV favours B because it creates the most value in total, even though most of its benefits arrive later.

Which measure to trust

  • Mutually exclusive options (you can only do one, such as three ways of replacing the same system): choose the highest NPV, here B. IRR can mislead when projects differ in size or timing, because a high percentage return on a small sum can be worth less than a lower percentage on a large one.
  • Limited budget, several independent projects (capital rationing): rank by profitability index and fund down the list until the budget runs out. This maximises total NPV per dollar available.
  • Cash-constrained or high-uncertainty settings: payback is a useful secondary check. Project B's benefits lean heavily on years four and five, so ask how confident you are in them. At a 15% rate, B's NPV is still about $13,400 while A's falls to about $2,000, so B is the more robust of the two.

Prioritising across a portfolio also involves strategy, risk and capacity, not just NPV.

NPV vs IRR, ROI and payback period

MeasureWhat it tells youTime value of money?Main strengthMain weakness
NPVValue created in today's moneyYesDirect measure of value; adds up across projectsNeeds a discount rate; not intuitive for some audiences
IRRThe rate of return at which NPV = 0YesEasy to compare with a hurdle rateCan mislead on size and timing; can give multiple answers when cash flows change sign more than once
ROINet gain as a % of costUsually noSimple and widely understoodIgnores timing; $1 in year 5 counts the same as $1 in year 1
Payback periodTime to recover the investmentNo (discounted payback: yes)Shows liquidity and exposureIgnores everything after payback
Profitability indexPV of benefits per $1 investedYesRanks projects under a budget limitCan favour small projects when only one can be chosen

A sensible business case reports NPV as the headline measure, with IRR, payback and a simple ROI as supporting figures for readers who prefer them. Our return on investment guide shows how ROI and discounted ROI are calculated for projects.

NPV and IRR in more detail

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero. In the warehouse example, NPV is positive at 9% and at 15%, and turns negative just above 15.9%. If the hurdle rate is below the IRR, NPV is positive; if it is above, NPV is negative. For a single, conventional project (outflow first, then inflows) NPV and IRR always give the same accept-or-reject answer. They only conflict when you rank projects of different size or timing, as in the comparison above.

Choosing the discount rate

The discount rate has more influence on NPV than almost any other input, and it is not the project manager's decision alone. In most organisations it comes from finance. Typical sources:

  • Cost of capital. Companies often use their weighted average cost of capital (WACC), the blended cost of their debt and equity, as the starting point.
  • Hurdle rate. Many organisations set a minimum required return above the cost of capital, sometimes with higher rates for riskier categories such as new products or new markets.
  • Risk-adjusted rate. A higher rate for riskier projects. Do not also cut the cash flows for the same risk, or you will count it twice.
  • Public-sector rates. Governments publish rates for appraising public spending. HM Treasury's Green Book has used a 3.5% real discount rate for the first 30 years, falling for longer periods. An independent review published in June 2026 recommended 3% (checked 25 October 2026). Check the current Green Book guidance before you use either figure.

Real or nominal: be consistent

A nominal rate includes inflation; a real rate excludes it. Use nominal cash flows (with expected price rises built in) with a nominal rate, or today's prices with a real rate. Mixing them is a common error. As an approximation, a 9% nominal rate with 2.5% inflation is a real rate of about 6.3% (1.09 / 1.025 - 1).

Monthly or quarterly periods

If you model cash flows monthly, convert the annual rate rather than dividing it by 12. The equivalent monthly rate for 9% a year is (1.09)^(1/12) - 1 = 0.72% a month, not 0.75%.

Sensitivity and scenario analysis for NPV

An NPV is only as good as the estimates behind it. Reviewers will trust it more if you show how it behaves when assumptions change.

One-at-a-time tests

Change from the base case (9%)NPVChange
Base case$52,713-
Annual net benefits 10% lower$22,442-$30,271
Initial investment 10% higher$27,713-$25,000
Benefits start one year later$27,719-$24,994
No residual value$39,715-$12,998

The project's NPV stays positive in every single test, but a delay or a cost overrun cuts it roughly in half. Break-even: net benefits could fall by about 17% across all five years before NPV reached zero (250,000 / 302,713 = 0.83). That is a clear, useful message for a sponsor.

Scenarios

ScenarioKey assumptionsNPV at 9%IRR
Worst caseInvestment $275,000; slower ramp-up; benefits $65,000-$70,000; residual $10,000-$37,1314.2%

The worst case is negative, so the decision depends on how likely it is and what can be done to prevent it. Typical mitigations are a phased rollout, a fixed-price supplier contract or a pilot site. These are exactly the risks to record in your risk management plan.

For larger investments, replace three scenarios with a probability distribution of outcomes. Our guides to sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo simulation show how.

How to calculate NPV in Excel and Google Sheets

Both Excel and Google Sheets have an NPV function with the same syntax: =NPV(rate, value1, [value2], ...). The catch is that it treats the first value as arriving one period from now. It is designed for years 1 to n, not year 0.

Suppose the warehouse example sits in cells B2:B7, with year 0 in B2 and the rate (9%) in E1.

FormulaResultCorrect?
=NPV(E1, B3:B7) + B2$52,713Yes: years 1-5 discounted, year 0 added at face value
=NPV(E1, B2:B7)$48,361No: every cash flow is discounted one year too many
=IRR(B2:B7)15.9%Yes: IRR takes the full range including year 0

The wrong version understates NPV by exactly one year's discounting ($52,713 / 1.09). It is the most common spreadsheet error in business cases, so check for it whenever you review someone else's model.

Irregular dates: XNPV and XIRR

If cash flows fall on specific dates rather than neat periods, use =XNPV(rate, values, dates) and =XIRR(values, dates). XNPV discounts each value from the first date using a 365-day year, and the first value is not discounted, so you include year 0 in the range. Both functions are available in Excel and Google Sheets.

NPV across the project life cycle

NPV is not a one-off calculation. It should be revisited whenever the investment decision is revisited.

StageHow NPV is used
Idea and feasibilityRough order-of-magnitude NPV to screen options; wide ranges are normal
Business case and approvalDetailed NPV with sensitivity; the headline financial figure for the decision; summarised in the project charter
Stage gatesUpdated with actual costs and refined estimates; a falling NPV is a reason to re-scope, pause or stop
Major change requestsLarge changes to scope, cost or timing should show their effect on NPV through change control
Closure and benefits reviewCompare forecast with actual benefits and capture the learning in lessons learned to improve future estimates

PMI's PMBOK Guide treats the business case and benefits management plan as key inputs to starting a project, and financial measures such as NPV, IRR and payback are part of the project selection methods taught for the PMP and CAPM exams. Our PMBOK Guide overview explains how the standard is structured. Who approves the case and how often it is reviewed is a matter of project governance.

Advantages and limitations of NPV

Advantages

  • Accounts for the time value of money, unlike simple ROI and payback.
  • Gives a single money figure that shows the value created.
  • NPVs of independent projects can be added together, which helps portfolio planning.
  • Uses every cash flow over the appraisal period, not just those up to payback.
  • Works with any pattern of cash flows, including negative years in the middle.

Limitations

  • Sensitive to the discount rate, which is a judgement.
  • Depends on cash flow forecasts that may be optimistic, especially benefits.
  • Can look more precise than the inputs justify; always show a range.
  • Does not capture benefits that are hard to price, such as customer experience, staff wellbeing or strategic options. Describe these alongside the NPV rather than forcing a number.
  • Can disadvantage long-term projects whose benefits arrive many years ahead.
  • Does not show the scale of investment on its own, so pair it with PI or the initial cost.

Common NPV mistakes

  • Discounting the year-zero investment, or using =NPV() over the whole range in a spreadsheet.
  • Using profit instead of cash flow, which brings in depreciation and timing differences.
  • Including sunk costs that will not change whatever you decide.
  • Counting financing costs twice, as interest in the cash flows and again in the discount rate.
  • Mixing real and nominal figures and rates.
  • Using an annual rate on monthly cash flows, or dividing the annual rate by 12.
  • Forgetting running, upgrade or decommissioning costs, which often arrive after go-live.
  • Optimism bias in benefits: assuming full benefits from day one, without a ramp-up period.
  • Comparing options over different lifespans without adjusting. Use a common period or an equivalent annual value.
  • Choosing between mutually exclusive options by IRR instead of NPV.
  • Presenting a single number without sensitivity, scenarios or the key assumptions.

Frequently asked questions

What is net present value in simple terms?

It is what a project's future cash is worth today, minus what it costs. Each future amount is reduced (discounted) to reflect the fact that money now is worth more than money later. If the result is positive, the project is expected to create value at the chosen rate of return.

What does a positive NPV mean?

The project's discounted cash inflows exceed its discounted outflows, so it is expected to earn more than the discount rate. It adds value, although you still need to weigh risk, strategy and available funding.

What does a negative NPV mean?

The project is not expected to earn the required rate of return. It may still go ahead for regulatory, safety or strategic reasons; then NPV helps you choose the least costly option.

What is a good NPV?

Any NPV above zero beats the hurdle rate. To judge how good it is, compare it with the size of the investment using the profitability index, and check how far assumptions can change before NPV falls to zero.

What discount rate should I use for NPV?

Use the rate your finance team sets, usually based on the organisation's cost of capital or a hurdle rate, sometimes adjusted for risk. Public-sector projects use published rates such as HM Treasury's Green Book rate in the UK. Use the same rate for all the options you compare.

What is the difference between NPV and IRR?

NPV is a money value at a given discount rate. IRR is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero. For a single conventional project they give the same accept-or-reject decision, but when ranking mutually exclusive projects of different size or timing, rely on NPV.

What is the difference between NPV and ROI?

Simple ROI divides net gain by cost and ignores when the gain arrives. NPV discounts each cash flow by its timing, so it distinguishes a project that pays back in year one from one that pays back in year five.

Why is NPV better than the payback period?

Payback ignores the time value of money and every cash flow after the payback point. NPV counts all cash flows over the appraisal period and discounts them. Payback is still useful as a secondary check on liquidity and exposure.

How do you calculate NPV in Excel?

Enter =NPV(rate, year 1 to year n cash flows) and add the year-zero cash flow separately, for example =NPV(9%, B3:B7) + B2. For dated cash flows use =XNPV(rate, values, dates), which includes the first value undiscounted.

Can NPV be used for non-profit or public-sector projects?

Yes. Public bodies often use NPV, sometimes called net present social value when it includes non-cash benefits valued in money terms. They use a published social discount rate, such as the Green Book rate in the UK.

Should inflation be included in NPV?

Yes, but consistently. Either forecast cash flows with inflation and use a nominal discount rate, or use today's prices with a real discount rate. Do not mix the two.

Do sunk costs count in NPV?

No. Money already spent cannot be recovered whatever you decide, so it is excluded. Only future, incremental cash flows that depend on the decision belong in the calculation.

Is NPV on the PMP exam?

Project selection measures such as NPV, IRR, payback period and benefit-cost ratio commonly appear in PMP and CAPM preparation materials, usually at a conceptual level: which option to choose, rather than long calculations. Check the current PMI exam content outline for the exact scope.

What is the profitability index?

The present value of future cash flows divided by the initial investment. A PI above 1 means NPV is positive. It is useful for ranking independent projects when the budget cannot fund them all.
