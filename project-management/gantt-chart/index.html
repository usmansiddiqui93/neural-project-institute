Gantt Chart: What It Is, How to Make One, Examples

A Gantt chart is a bar chart that shows a project's activities against a timeline, so you can see at a glance what happens when, in what order and who is doing it. This guide explains every part of the chart, how to read one, how to build one in eight steps and how to use it to track progress, with a fully dated worked example.

  • Published 22 September 2026
  • Last updated 18 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • A Gantt chart shows each activity as a horizontal bar on a timeline, with arrows for dependencies and diamonds for milestones.
  • Build it from activities, durations and dependencies so the dates calculate themselves, rather than typing dates in by hand.
  • The critical path and float show which bars can move without delaying the finish and which cannot.
  • Save a baseline before work starts and compare progress against it at least weekly.
  • Keep stakeholder views short (20 to 40 lines) and hold the detail underneath in collapsible summary rows.
  • Spreadsheets are fine for simple charts; move to software when you need linked dependencies, rescheduling and resource views.

What is a Gantt chart?

A Gantt chart is a horizontal bar chart used to plan and track a project schedule. Activities are listed down the left-hand side and time runs across the top. Each activity is drawn as a bar whose position shows when it starts and finishes and whose length shows its duration. Arrows link dependent activities and diamonds mark milestones.

A well-built Gantt chart answers five questions that a plain task list cannot:

  • What work has to be done, grouped into phases or deliverables.
  • When each piece of work starts and finishes.
  • In what order the work must happen, and what is waiting on what.
  • Who is responsible for each activity.
  • Are we on track? How actual progress compares with the plan.

The Gantt chart is the most common way of presenting a schedule, but it is a view of the schedule rather than the schedule itself. The underlying model is the list of activities, durations and dependencies that you build during project scheduling. Get that model right and the chart draws itself; get it wrong and the chart is just a picture.

Parts of a Gantt chart

Most Gantt charts, whether drawn in a spreadsheet or in software, share the same building blocks.

Summary rowsPhases or WBS levels that roll up their child tasksCollapse them for executive views

You do not need every element on every chart. A one-page plan for a board meeting may show only summary bars and milestones, while the working schedule for the project team shows every activity, link and owner.

How to read a Gantt chart

If someone hands you a Gantt chart, read it in this order.

Bars and the timescale

Start with the timescale at the top so you know whether each column is a day, a week or a month. Then read each bar from left to right: the left edge is the start date, the right edge is the finish date and the length is the duration. Bars that overlap vertically happen at the same time; bars that sit end to end happen in sequence.

Arrows

An arrow from the end of one bar to the start of another is a finish-to-start dependency: the second activity cannot start until the first finishes. Arrows from start to start or finish to finish show the other dependency types explained further down. A bar with no arrow coming in, other than the first activity, is a warning sign that a dependency may be missing.

Diamonds

A diamond is a milestone: a point in time with no duration, such as "design approved" or "go-live". Milestones are often the dates stakeholders care about most, so look along the diamonds first if you only have a minute.

Shading and the today line

Darker shading inside a bar shows how much of the activity is complete. Compare it with the today line: if the shading on a bar stops to the left of the line, that activity is behind where it should be. If it reaches beyond the line, it is ahead.

Summary bars, baselines and colours

Summary bars, often drawn as black brackets, span all the activities beneath them. Thin bars under the main bars are usually the baseline, so a gap between the two shows slippage. Red bars normally mark the critical path. Always check the legend, because colour conventions differ between tools and organisations.

Tip

A quick five-question check for any Gantt chart: When does it finish? Which milestones are next? What is on the critical path? Is anything to the left of the today line unfinished? Has the finish moved since the baseline?

A short history of the Gantt chart

The chart is named after Henry Gantt (1861–1919), an American engineer and management consultant who designed his charts around 1910 to 1915. His early charts were used to compare planned and actual output in factories, so that managers could see where work was ahead of or behind schedule.

He was not the first to draw schedules this way. According to Wikipedia's history of the Gantt chart, the Polish engineer Karol Adamiecki devised a similar chart, which he called a harmonogram, in 1896, but published only in Polish and Russian, so it was little known in the West. The Swiss engineer Hermann Schürch also published comparable bar charts for construction work in the 1910s.

Gantt charts were used by the US Army during the First World War, and Wallace Clark's 1922 book *The Gantt Chart: A Working Tool of Management* helped spread the technique internationally. For decades the charts were drawn by hand on paper or wall boards, which made changes slow. Computer scheduling, and network techniques such as the critical path method and the PERT chart from the late 1950s, made it possible to link activities and recalculate dates automatically. Modern Gantt chart software combines both ideas: the network logic underneath and the familiar bars on top.

How to make a Gantt chart step by step

These eight steps work in any tool, from a spreadsheet to enterprise scheduling software.

1. Define the scope and break it down

Start from the agreed scope and break it into deliverables and work packages with a work breakdown structure. The WBS becomes the summary rows of your chart, so the chart is organised around what you are delivering rather than a random list of jobs.

2. List the activities

Break each work package into activities that one person or team can own and that last somewhere between a day and a few weeks. Name each with a verb and a noun. Add milestones for approvals, handovers and deadlines.

3. Estimate durations

Estimate the working time each activity needs, in one unit (usually working days). Ask the people who will do the work, and use a three-point estimate where the work is uncertain.

4. Identify dependencies

For each activity, record its predecessors: the activities that must finish (or start) before it can begin. Most links are finish-to-start. Note any waiting time as a lag, such as concrete curing or a supplier lead time.

5. Choose a tool and set the calendar

Enter the activities into a spreadsheet template or scheduling tool. Set the project start date and the working calendar: working days, weekends, public holidays and any shutdown periods. A missing holiday is one of the most common reasons a chart's dates are wrong.

6. Link the activities and let the dates calculate

Enter the dependencies rather than typing start dates. The tool then calculates every date from the start date, durations and links. Add milestones as zero-duration activities at the points they depend on.

7. Assign owners and check workload

Assign an owner, and resources if you track them, to every activity. Check that nobody is booked on several full-time activities at once. If they are, move non-critical work using its float or level the resources.

8. Review, baseline and share

Check the critical path and float, confirm the finish date meets the business need, and walk the team through the chart. When everyone agrees, save a baseline (see project baseline) and share the chart in the format each audience needs.

Tip

If most of your activities have a start date typed in, the chart is a picture, not a schedule. Replace fixed dates with dependency links wherever you can, and keep date constraints for genuine external deadlines.

Gantt chart example: an office move

A 40-person team is moving to a new office. The project starts on Monday 2 November 2026, the team works Monday to Friday and there are no holidays in the period. Durations are in working days.

IDActivityDurationPredecessorStartFinish
AConfirm requirements and budget3 days-Mon 2 NovWed 4 Nov
BSelect and order furniture5 daysAThu 5 NovWed 11 Nov
CBook removal firm2 daysAThu 5 NovFri 6 Nov
DInstall network and phones6 daysAThu 5 NovThu 12 Nov
EDeliver and install furniture5 daysBThu 12 NovWed 18 Nov
FPack and label3 daysCMon 9 NovWed 11 Nov
GMove and unpack2 daysD, E, FThu 19 NovFri 20 Nov
MMilestone: new office open0 daysGFri 20 NovFri 20 Nov

Drawn on a weekly timescale, the bars look like this (■ = the activity runs that week, ◆ = milestone):

Activityw/c 2 Novw/c 9 Novw/c 16 Nov
A Confirm requirements■ Mon–Wed
B Order furniture (critical)■ Thu–Fri■ Mon–Wed
C Book removals■ Thu–Fri

F Pack and label■ Mon–Wed
G Move and unpack (critical)■ Thu–Fri
M Office open◆ Fri 20 Nov

Reading the example

There are three paths from A to G. The longest, A-B-E-G, takes 3 + 5 + 5 + 2 = 15 working days, so it is the critical path and sets the opening date of Friday 20 November. A software tool would show those bars in red.

PathLengthTotal float on the path
A-B-E-G15 days0 (critical)
A-D-G11 days4 days on D
A-C-F-G10 days5 days shared by C and F
  • D (network and phones) has 4 days of total float and 4 days of free float. It could finish as late as Wednesday 18 November without moving the opening.
  • C and F share 5 days of total float. C has no free float of its own, because any slip in C pushes F straight away, but F can slip 5 days before it touches G. If C uses some of the float, there is less left for F. See float and slack for the difference between total and free float.
  • B and E are where the project manager should spend most attention. A late furniture order moves the opening date day for day.

Dependencies, leads and lags on a Gantt chart

The arrows on a Gantt chart represent four possible dependency types. Finish-to-start covers most links, but the others let you model overlapping work realistically.

TypeRuleExampleHow it looks
Finish-to-start (FS)B cannot start until A finishesFurniture is installed after it is orderedArrow from end of A to start of B
Start-to-start (SS)B cannot start until A startsPacking can start once the removal firm starts workArrow from start of A to start of B
Finish-to-finish (FF)B cannot finish until A finishesSnagging cannot finish until installation finishesArrow from end of A to end of B
Start-to-finish (SF)B cannot finish until A startsThe old phone system stays live until the new one startsArrow from start of A to end of B (rare)

Two adjustments refine any link:

  • A lag adds waiting time. "Paint walls, then lay carpet FS + 2 days" leaves two days for the paint to dry.
  • A lead is a negative lag that allows overlap. "Start testing FS − 3 days" lets testing start three days before the build finishes.

Our guide to leads and lags has worked examples. Use date constraints such as "must start on" only for genuine external fixes like a booked venue, because each one stops the chart from rescheduling naturally. Some charts also use a hammock activity, a bar whose length is set by other activities, to show support work such as supervision or equipment hire.

Tracking progress with a Gantt chart

A Gantt chart earns its keep during delivery, when it shows what has changed and what that means for the finish date.

Worked tracking example

Back to the office move. At the end of Friday 13 November (working day 10), the project manager updates the chart:

ActivityBaseline finishStatus at 13 NovForecast finish
A Confirm requirementsWed 4 NovCompleteWed 4 Nov
B Order furnitureWed 11 NovComplete, one day lateThu 12 Nov (actual)
C Book removalsFri 6 NovCompleteFri 6 Nov
D Network and phonesThu 12 NovCompleteThu 12 Nov
E Install furnitureWed 18 Nov20% complete (planned 40%)Thu 19 Nov
F Pack and labelWed 11 NovCompleteWed 11 Nov
G Move and unpackFri 20 NovNot startedMon 23 Nov
M Office openFri 20 Nov-Mon 23 Nov

Because B is on the critical path, its one-day slip pushes E, G and the opening milestone back by one working day. On the chart, the current bars for E and G now sit one day to the right of their grey baseline bars, and the shading on E stops short of the today line. In schedule terms the project has a one-day variance against the baseline; see schedule variance for the earned value version of the same idea.

The project manager now has clear options:

  • Crash the critical activity: pay for a second installation crew so E finishes a day earlier. See schedule compression.
  • Fast track: move the IT equipment on Thursday 19 November while the last furniture is still going in, overlapping E and G.
  • Accept the new date and tell stakeholders through the project status report.

Note that slips on D, C or F would not have mattered on this scale, because those activities had float. That is the main value of the critical path on a Gantt chart: it tells you which delays deserve action.

A weekly update routine

  1. Pick a status date (for example, close of business every Friday).
  2. Record actual start and finish dates for anything that started or finished.
  3. Update percent complete or remaining duration for activities in progress.
  4. Let the tool reschedule unfinished work from the status date.
  5. Compare the forecast finish and milestones with the baseline.
  6. Check whether the critical path has moved and note any near-critical activities.
  7. Agree actions for any slip, and record approved changes through change control before re-baselining.

Gantt chart vs PERT chart, timeline, Kanban and roadmap

Gantt charts are often confused with other planning visuals. Each answers a different question.

ToolShowsStrengthUse it when
Gantt chartActivities as bars on a timeline, with linksEasy to read dates, overlaps and progressYou need a dated, trackable schedule
PERT chartActivities and dependencies as a network, with three-point estimatesShows logic and uncertainty clearlyDurations are uncertain or logic is complex
Network diagramBoxes and arrows with early and late datesMakes the critical path calculation visibleYou are building or checking the logic
Project timelineKey phases and milestones onlyVery simple for executivesStakeholders need the big picture
Project roadmapGoals and themes over months or quartersStrategic, outcome-focusedCommunicating direction, not tasks
Kanban boardWork items by status in columnsShows flow and bottlenecksWork arrives continuously and is not tightly scheduled

They combine well. Many teams build the logic in a network view, present it as a Gantt chart, summarise it for executives as a timeline and manage day-to-day work on a Kanban board.

When to use a Gantt chart (and when not to)

A Gantt chart works well when

  • The work has a clear sequence and real dependencies, as in construction, events, office moves, migrations and product launches.
  • Several teams or suppliers have to hand work over to each other.
  • There is a fixed deadline and you need to know whether you can meet it.
  • Stakeholders need to see dates and milestones.
  • You report progress against an approved baseline, as in most Waterfall and hybrid projects.

Consider something lighter when

  • The work is a stream of small, independent requests, where a Kanban board shows flow better.
  • Scope changes every sprint, so detailed bars would need rebuilding every fortnight. Plan releases and milestones on the chart and leave the detail to the backlog.
  • The project is a handful of tasks for one person, where a to-do list is enough.

Advantages and limitations of Gantt charts

AdvantagesLimitations
Easy for anyone to read, with little trainingLarge charts become cluttered and hard to follow
Shows sequence, overlap and duration at a glanceDoes not show effort, cost or risk unless you add them
Makes the critical path and float visibleCan give false precision when work is uncertain
Shows progress against a baseline clearlyNeeds regular updating to stay useful
Highlights handovers between teamsDependencies are hard to maintain in a spreadsheet
Works for almost any project typeEncourages detailed up-front planning that may not suit highly iterative work

Most of the limitations come from how the chart is maintained rather than the chart itself. Linked dependencies, a sensible level of detail and a weekly update routine deal with nearly all of them.

Common Gantt chart mistakes

  • Typing dates instead of linking activities. The chart cannot reschedule itself, so it goes out of date the first time anything moves.
  • Too much detail. Hundreds of one-hour tasks make the chart unreadable and impossible to maintain. Keep activities between about a day and a few weeks.
  • Dangling activities. An activity with no successor can finish late without moving anything on the chart, which hides the real critical path.
  • Ignoring calendars. Weekends, public holidays and part-time resources change the real dates even when the logic is right.
  • No baseline. Without one, you can see where you are but not how far you have drifted.
  • Over-allocated people. Bars can look fine while one person is booked on three full-time activities at once. Check workload, not just dates.
  • Showing everyone everything. Executives want milestones and summary bars; the team wants the detail. Build one schedule and filter it for each audience.
  • Updating only when asked. A chart updated once a month is a history book. Update it on a fixed cadence.

How to make a Gantt chart in Excel or Google Sheets

Spreadsheets do not have a built-in Gantt chart type, but there are two common methods.

Method 1: the stacked bar chart

  1. Create columns for Task, Start date and Duration (in days).
  2. Select the Task and Start date columns and insert a stacked bar chart.
  3. Add the Duration column as a second data series on the same chart.
  4. Format the Start date series with no fill and no border, so only the duration bars remain visible.
  5. Reverse the order of the vertical (category) axis so the first task appears at the top.
  6. Set the horizontal axis minimum to your project start date and adjust the date format and gridlines.

Method 2: the shaded grid

List tasks in rows and put days or weeks across the columns, then shade the cells each task covers, manually or with conditional formatting that compares each column's date with the task's start and finish. This is how our Gantt chart template works: enter owners and start and end dates, and the day count calculates for you.

Both methods show timing well but do not calculate dependencies, float or the critical path, and bars do not move when a predecessor slips. For linked logic in a spreadsheet, use the project schedule template; for anything larger, use software.

Gantt chart software and templates

For small projects, a free Gantt chart template in Google Sheets or Excel is often enough. Move to Gantt chart software when you need any of the following:

  • Dependencies that reschedule bars automatically when something slips.
  • Critical path highlighting and baselines.
  • Resource and workload views across several projects.
  • Real-time collaboration, comments and progress updates from the team.
  • Portfolio views that roll several project charts into one.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Gantt chart used for?

A Gantt chart is used to plan, communicate and track a project schedule. It shows what work needs doing, when each activity starts and finishes, the order of the work, who is responsible and how progress compares with the plan.

What are the main elements of a Gantt chart?

The main elements are the task list, a timescale, bars for each activity, dependency arrows, milestones, progress shading, owners, a today line and, on more advanced charts, baseline bars and critical path highlighting.

Who invented the Gantt chart?

It is named after Henry Gantt, who designed his charts around 1910 to 1915. The Polish engineer Karol Adamiecki had devised a similar chart, the harmonogram, in 1896, but it was published only in Polish and Russian and was little known outside Eastern Europe.

How do I make a Gantt chart?

Break the scope into a work breakdown structure, list the activities, estimate durations, identify dependencies, enter everything into a template or tool with a start date and calendar, link the activities so dates calculate, assign owners, then review the critical path and save a baseline.

What is the difference between a Gantt chart and a timeline?

A project timeline shows key phases and milestones in a simple form, usually for stakeholders. A Gantt chart shows every activity with its duration, dependencies and progress, so it is the working tool for managing the schedule.

Can I make a Gantt chart in Excel?

Yes. Use a stacked bar chart with the start date series hidden, or a grid of shaded cells. Excel will not calculate dependencies or the critical path, so bars do not move when a predecessor slips. A ready-made Gantt chart template saves setup time.

What does a diamond mean on a Gantt chart?

A diamond is a milestone: a significant point in time with zero duration, such as an approval, a handover or a go-live date.

What do the arrows on a Gantt chart mean?

Arrows show dependencies between activities. Most are finish-to-start, meaning the next activity cannot start until the previous one finishes. Start-to-start, finish-to-finish and start-to-finish links are also possible.

Is a Gantt chart Agile or Waterfall?

It is most associated with Waterfall and other plan-driven approaches, but many Agile and hybrid teams use simplified Gantt charts for releases, milestones and dependencies between teams. See agile Gantt charts.

What is the difference between a Gantt chart and a PERT chart?

A Gantt chart shows activities as bars on a timeline, which makes dates and progress easy to read. A PERT chart shows activities as a network of boxes and arrows with three-point estimates, which makes logic and uncertainty easier to analyse.

What is a baseline in a Gantt chart?

A baseline is a saved copy of the approved planned dates, usually shown as thin bars under the current bars. The gap between the two shows how far each activity has slipped or moved ahead.

How many tasks should a Gantt chart have?

There is no fixed limit, but a view of 20 to 40 lines is readable on one screen or page. Hold larger schedules in collapsible summary rows and filter the view for each audience.

How often should I update a Gantt chart?

At least weekly on most projects, on a fixed status date. Fast-moving projects or the final weeks before a deadline may need updates every day or two.

Which tools have Gantt charts?

Most project management tools offer a Gantt or timeline view, including Wrike, Smartsheet, monday.com, Asana and ClickUp, though features such as dependencies, baselines and critical path often depend on the plan. Compare them in our Gantt chart software guide.
