What Is Project Management? Definition, Process, Examples

Project management is the discipline of planning, organising and leading work to deliver a specific result within agreed limits of time, cost, scope and quality. It turns an idea, such as a new website, an office move or a product launch, into a plan people can carry out and leaders can track. This guide explains how project management works in practice: the five phases, the constraints you balance, the main methods, who does what, and a worked example you can follow from start to finish.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 4 September 2026
  • 26 min read

Key takeaways

  • Project management applies knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to a temporary piece of work so that it delivers an agreed result.
  • Every project balances scope, time and cost, with quality at the centre; changing one almost always affects the others.
  • Projects move through five phases: initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure.
  • You can run a project with a predictive (Waterfall), adaptive (Agile) or hybrid approach; the right choice depends on how stable the requirements are.
  • The current PMI standard, the PMBOK Guide 8th edition (November 2025), is built on six principles and seven performance domains.
  • You can start with a one-page charter, a task list and a weekly status check, then add more structure as projects grow.

What is project management?

Project management is the practice of planning, organising, leading and controlling the work of a temporary project so that it delivers a defined result, such as a product, service or change, within agreed limits of time, cost, scope and quality. It covers the whole journey, from agreeing why the project exists to handing over the result and capturing what the team learned.

The professional bodies describe it in similar terms. Their wording is worth knowing if you plan to sit an exam, because each definition stresses something slightly different.

SourceDefinition (summarised)What it emphasises
PMI (PMBOK Guide)The application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirementsMeeting requirements through applied capability
APM (UK Association for Project Management)The application of processes, methods, knowledge, skills and experience to achieve specific objectives for changeProjects as a way of delivering change
PRINCE2Planning, delegating, monitoring and controlling the project, and motivating the people involved, to achieve its objectives within expected targetsControl and governance in stages

Project management in plain English

Imagine you are organising a dinner for six friends. You decide the menu, buy ingredients, cook and serve. You are doing a tiny project, but you hardly need project management because everything is in your head and the risks are small.

Now imagine your company is moving 120 people to a new office in 14 weeks. The IT team, facilities, HR, a removals firm, the landlord and every department are involved, the budget is fixed and the old lease ends on a set date. Without someone defining what is in scope, sequencing the work, tracking costs and spotting problems early, things fall through the gaps. Project management is the set of practices that stops that happening.

What counts as a project?

A project is a temporary effort with a defined start and end, undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. Temporary does not mean short: a project can last two weeks or five years. Unique does not mean never done before: your tenth office move is still unique because the building, people and budget are different. Recurring work, such as running payroll every month, is operations rather than a project.

Why project management matters

Good project management does not guarantee success, but it makes success far more likely and failure far less surprising. Its main benefits are:

  • Clear goals: everyone knows what the project will deliver and how success will be judged.
  • Deliberate trade-offs: decisions about scope, time and cost are made openly, by the right people, instead of by accident.
  • Fewer surprises: risks are identified early, while they are still cheap to deal with.
  • Efficient use of people and money: work is sequenced so that people are not waiting on each other or doing the same thing twice.
  • Accountability: each piece of work has an owner and a due date.
  • Stakeholder confidence: sponsors and customers can see progress and trust the forecast.
  • Learning: lessons from one project improve the next.

The table below shows the problems that most often appear when projects are run informally, and the practice that prevents each one.

SymptomUsual causeProject management practice that helps
The project keeps growingScope was never written down or agreedA scope statement and change control to stop scope creep
Deadlines slip without warningNo schedule, or one that ignores dependenciesA project schedule built from the work breakdown, with milestones
The budget runs out earlyCosts estimated as one guess, not built up from the workA bottom-up project budget and regular cost tracking
Work is done twice or not at allUnclear rolesA RACI matrix that names who is responsible and accountable
A problem is found too late to fix cheaplyRisks were not discussedA risk register reviewed at every status meeting
Senior people are unhappy at the endExpectations were never confirmedStakeholder analysis and a communication plan

The triple constraint and other project constraints

Every project works within limits. The best-known model is the triple constraint, also called the project management triangle: scope (what you deliver), time (when you deliver it) and cost (what it takes in money and effort). Quality sits in the middle, because squeezing any side of the triangle without adjusting another usually lowers quality.

Modern standards treat the triangle as a starting point rather than the whole picture. Most projects also have to manage risk, resources (people, equipment, suppliers), benefits (the value the result must produce) and sometimes sustainability or regulatory limits.

Worked example: a deadline moves forward

Suppose your project to launch a customer portal is planned for 12 weeks with a budget of £80,000. The sales director asks for launch in 9 weeks to coincide with a trade show. You cannot simply agree; you set out the options and their consequences:

OptionWhat changesConsequence
Reduce scopeLaunch with the core features only; add reporting in a second releaseDate met, budget held; some users wait for reporting
Add resourcesBring in two contract developers for six weeksDate likely met; cost rises by an estimated £18,000; new people need onboarding time
Overlap tasks (fast-tracking)Start testing before development is fully completeDate possibly met; higher risk of rework
Keep the planLaunch in week 12 as agreedNo extra cost or risk; trade-show opportunity missed

The sponsor, not the project manager, makes the final call, because it is a business trade-off. Your job is to make the trade-off visible with honest numbers. Techniques such as fast-tracking and crashing are covered in more detail in our scheduling guides.

The project management process: 5 phases

Most projects move through five phases, often called the project life cycle. PMI's process groups, and the five focus areas in the PMBOK Guide 8th edition, use the same names. The table shows what each phase is for and what it usually produces.

PhaseKey questionMain activitiesTypical outputs
1. InitiationShould we do this, and why?Confirm the need, check feasibility, appoint a sponsor and project manager, identify stakeholdersBusiness case, project charter, stakeholder register
2. PlanningHow will we do it?Define scope, break down the work, estimate, schedule, budget, plan risks, quality, communication and procurementScope statement, WBS, schedule, budget, risk register, project management plan
3. ExecutionAre we doing the work?Build the deliverables, lead the team, manage suppliers, engage stakeholdersDeliverables, issue log, change requests
4. Monitoring and controllingAre we on track?Compare progress with the baseline, manage changes, risks and issues, report statusStatus reports, updated forecasts, approved changes
5. ClosureDid we deliver, and what did we learn?Get formal acceptance, hand over to operations, close contracts, release the team, review lessonsAcceptance sign-off, lessons-learned report, archived records

Two points are often missed. First, monitoring and controlling is not really a separate stage: it runs alongside execution from the first day of work. Second, in Agile projects, planning, execution and review repeat in short cycles, while initiation and closure still happen once at the start and end.

How to manage a project step by step

If you have just been handed your first project, this ten-step process works for most small and medium projects. Each step links to a guide or a free template so you do not have to start from a blank page.

  1. Confirm the purpose. Write down, in two or three sentences, the problem the project solves and the outcome the sponsor expects. Agree how success will be measured, ideally as SMART project objectives.
  2. Identify stakeholders. List everyone who is affected by the project or can influence it, and note what each needs from you. See project stakeholder management.
  3. Write a project charter. Capture purpose, objectives, high-level scope, key milestones, budget, risks and the sponsor's sign-off on one or two pages. Use our free project charter template.
  4. Define the scope. List what is included and, just as important, what is excluded. Our project scope template gives you a structure.
  5. Break down the work. Split the deliverables into smaller pieces until each can be estimated and assigned. This is the work breakdown structure (WBS).
  6. Build the schedule. Estimate each task, identify dependencies, set milestones and find the critical path. A Gantt chart template is the simplest way to see it.
  7. Set the budget. Add up labour, materials, suppliers and a contingency reserve for known risks. Our budget template does the arithmetic for you.
  8. Plan risks, roles and communication. Start a risk register template, agree responsibilities in a RACI matrix template, and decide who gets which update and how often.
  9. Execute, track and adjust. Hold short regular check-ins, update progress weekly, compare it with the plan, and use change control for any request that affects scope, time or cost. A simple project status report keeps sponsors informed.
  10. Close the project. Get formal acceptance, hand over to the people who will run the result, close supplier contracts, thank the team and hold a lessons-learned review with our lessons learned template.
Tip

Scale the paperwork to the project. A two-week internal project might need only a one-page charter, a task list and a weekly five-minute check-in. A two-year capital project needs every document above and formal governance. The thinking behind each step matters more than the document.

Worked example: a website redesign from start to finish

To see how the pieces fit together, follow one realistic project through its life cycle. A 60-person professional services firm wants to replace its outdated website. The figures are illustrative but typical for a project of this size.

Initiation: the charter

The marketing director agrees to sponsor the project and appoints a project manager from the marketing team. They write a one-page charter:

Charter itemContent
PurposeReplace the current website, which is hard to update and performs poorly on mobile
ObjectivesLaunch a new site within 12 weeks; double enquiries from the contact form within six months of launch; meet accessibility guidelines
In scopeDesign, build, content migration of 40 key pages, contact forms, analytics, staff training
Out of scopeClient portal, online payments, translation
Budget£60,000 including a £5,000 contingency reserve
Key milestonesDesign approved (week 4), build complete (week 9), launch (week 12)
Main risksContent not ready on time; design approval delayed; agency capacity
Sponsor sign-offMarketing director, dated

Planning: breaking down the work

The project manager and the web agency build a work breakdown structure, then turn it into a schedule with dependencies and milestones.

WBS elementMain deliverablesOwnerWeeks
1. DiscoveryRequirements, site map, content auditProject manager and agency1 to 2
2. DesignWireframes, visual design, design approvalAgency designer2 to 4
3. ContentRewritten copy for 40 pages, imagesMarketing team3 to 8
4. BuildTemplates, forms, analytics, accessibility checksAgency developers5 to 9
5. TestingFunctional, browser, accessibility and user acceptance testingAgency and project team9 to 11
6. Launch and handoverGo-live, redirects, staff training, support handoverProject manager11 to 12

Content (element 3) sits on the critical path: if the marketing team is late with copy, the build and testing cannot finish on time, so the project manager schedules weekly content check-ins from week 3.

Planning: the risk register

RiskLikelihoodImpactResponseOwner
Copy for 40 pages is lateHighHighAgree a page-by-page content schedule; prioritise the 15 most visited pages; budget for a freelance copywriter if neededMarketing manager
Partners disagree on the designMediumMediumShow two design routes early; one decision-maker named in the charterSponsor
Agency loses a developer mid-buildLowHighContract requires named back-up; weekly progress demosProject manager

Monitoring: a status check in week 6

At the end of week 6 the project manager runs a simple earned value check. The total budget (budget at completion, BAC) is £60,000. By now the plan said £30,000 of work should be done (planned value, PV). The work actually completed is worth £24,000 of the budget (earned value, EV), and £27,000 has been spent (actual cost, AC).

MeasureFormulaResultWhat it tells you
Schedule variance (SV)EV - PV£24,000 - £30,000 = -£6,000Behind schedule
Cost variance (CV)EV - AC£24,000 - £27,000 = -£3,000Over budget for the work done
Schedule performance index (SPI)EV / PV0.80Delivering 80p of planned work per £1 planned
Cost performance index (CPI)EV / AC0.89Getting 89p of value per £1 spent
Estimate at completion (EAC)BAC / CPI£60,000 / 0.89 = about £67,500Final cost if current efficiency continues

The cause is the content risk coming true: only 12 of 40 pages are ready. The project manager presents two options to the sponsor: hire a freelance copywriter for four weeks (about £4,000, using most of the contingency reserve) or launch with 25 pages and add the rest within a month. The sponsor chooses the copywriter. Because this removes the bottleneck rather than accepting it, the project manager forecasts a final cost of about £62,000 rather than the £67,500 the formula projects, and the sponsor approves a £2,000 budget increase. The decision is recorded in the change log and the risk register is revised.

Closure: handover and lessons

The site launches in week 12 at a final cost of £61,800, within the approved revised budget. The sponsor signs the acceptance form, the agency hands over admin access and documentation, the marketing team is trained to edit pages, and support moves to the agency's maintenance contract. A one-hour lessons-learned session records three lessons: start content first, name a single design approver, and track content weekly from day one.

Knowledge areas, principles and performance domains

If you study project management formally, you will meet three ways of organising the subject, depending on which edition of PMI's PMBOK Guide you read. Many courses and job descriptions still use the older terms, so it helps to know all three.

PMBOK 6th edition (2017)PMBOK 7th edition (2021)PMBOK 8th edition (November 2025)
Organised aroundProcessesPrinciples and outcomesPrinciples, performance domains and non-prescriptive processes
Main structure10 knowledge areas and 5 process groups12 principles and 8 performance domains6 principles, 7 performance domains and 5 focus areas
Areas or domainsIntegration, scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement, stakeholdersStakeholders, team, development approach and life cycle, planning, project work, delivery, measurement, uncertaintyGovernance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources, risk
Processes49 defined processesProcess detail largely removed40 non-prescriptive processes grouped under initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, closing

According to PMI, the 8th edition simplifies the 7th edition's principles and domains, reintroduces process guidance in a non-prescriptive form, and expands coverage of AI, project management offices and procurement. Its six principles include adopting a holistic view, focusing on value, embedding quality and integrating sustainability.

PRINCE2, the other widely used framework, is organised differently. PRINCE2 7 (2023) has seven principles, seven practices and seven processes, with people placed at the centre of the method.

Project management methodologies and approaches

A methodology is a defined way of running projects. At the highest level there are three approaches: predictive (plan in detail up front), adaptive (plan and deliver in short iterations) and hybrid (a mix of both). Within those sit specific methods and frameworks.

MethodologyHow it worksOften used for
WaterfallSequential phases, each completed and approved before the next startsConstruction, regulated projects, fixed-price contracts
AgileDeliver working results in short iterations, gather feedback and adaptSoftware, digital products, marketing, research
ScrumAgile framework with fixed-length Sprints, a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and DevelopersProduct development teams
KanbanVisualise work on a board, limit work in progress and improve flowSupport, operations, continuous delivery, content teams
PRINCE2Stage-based method with strong governance and business justificationGovernment and large organisations, especially in the UK and Europe
LeanRemove waste and maximise value for the customerManufacturing, process improvement, service design
Six SigmaReduce variation and defects using data (DMAIC)Quality and process improvement projects
Critical chainSchedule around resource limits and protect the end date with buffersMulti-project environments with shared resources
HybridPredictive governance and milestones with Agile delivery insideLarge organisations adopting Agile, product launches with fixed dates

How to choose an approach

There is no single best methodology. Look at the characteristics of your project and lean towards the approach that suits most of them.

Project characteristicLeans predictiveLeans adaptive (Agile)
RequirementsClear and stable from the startExpected to change as you learn
Customer involvementAvailable at set approval pointsAvailable often, ideally every iteration
Cost of change late in the projectHigh (for example, concrete already poured)Low (for example, software that can be updated)
Value of early partial deliveryLittle: the result is only useful when completeHigh: users benefit from early releases
Regulation and contractsFixed scope and price, formal sign-offsFlexible scope within a time and budget
TeamSpecialists working in sequenceSmall cross-functional team working together

If your answers are mixed, a hybrid approach is usually sensible.

Who is involved: roles in project management

Projects succeed when everyone knows their role. Titles vary between organisations, but these are the roles you will meet most often.

RoleMain responsibility
SponsorSenior person who owns the business case, provides funding, makes key decisions and removes obstacles
Project managerPlans and coordinates the work, leads the team, manages risks, stakeholders and budget, and reports progress
Project teamSpecialists who carry out the work and produce the deliverables
StakeholdersAnyone affected by the project or able to influence it, including users, customers, suppliers and regulators
Product Owner (Scrum)Maximises the value of the product and orders the Product Backlog
Scrum Master (Scrum)Helps the team use Scrum effectively and removes impediments
Project coordinatorSupports the project manager with schedules, documents, meetings and tracking
PMOA project management office that sets standards, supports projects and reports across the portfolio

What a project manager does in a typical week

  • Runs short check-ins with the team and clears blockers.
  • Updates the schedule, budget and risk register with what actually happened.
  • Reviews change requests and takes the significant ones to the sponsor.
  • Sends a short status report: progress, next steps, risks, decisions needed.
  • Talks to key stakeholders one to one, especially those who are worried or unconvinced.
  • Looks ahead two to four weeks for resource clashes, approvals and supplier deliveries.

A short history of project management

People have managed large projects for thousands of years, but project management as a named discipline is mostly a twentieth-century development.

PeriodDevelopment
1910sHenry Gantt popularises the bar chart that now carries his name
Late 1950sThe critical path method (CPM) is developed in industry and PERT for the US Navy's Polaris programme
1969The Project Management Institute (PMI) is founded in the United States
1989 and 1996PRINCE is introduced in the UK, followed by PRINCE2 in 1996
1996PMI publishes the first edition of A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge
2001The Agile Manifesto is published by 17 software practitioners
2020The current Scrum Guide is released

2023PRINCE2 7 is released
November 2025PMBOK 8th edition: six principles, seven performance domains, 40 non-prescriptive processes
July 2026PMI launches the updated PMP exam, with more weight on the business environment

Common project management mistakes

Most project problems come from a small number of habits. Spotting them early is half the fix.

MistakeWhat it looks likeHow to fix it
Starting without a clear objectiveDifferent people describe the project differentlyWrite a charter and get the sponsor to sign it
Planning the tasks but not the scopeLong task lists, but no agreement on what is excludedWrite an in-scope and out-of-scope list before scheduling
Estimating top-down only"It should take about three months"Estimate from the WBS and ask the people doing the work
No contingencyAny problem immediately becomes a crisisHold a reserve of time and money linked to identified risks
Saying yes to every changeScope grows, the date does not moveAssess every change for impact on time, cost and quality; let the sponsor decide
Reporting only good newsGreen status until the week before the deadlineReport honestly and early; bring options, not just problems
Skipping closureThe team moves on and the same mistakes repeatHold a short lessons-learned review and share it

How to get started in project management

Many project managers start "accidentally", by being asked to coordinate something at work. If you want to move into the field deliberately, this path works well:

  1. Learn the fundamentals. Work through this guide, the project phases and the core documents: charter, scope, schedule, budget, risk register.
  2. Practise on a small project. Volunteer to run an internal improvement, an event or a system roll-out, and use the templates linked above.
  3. Build your people skills. Run meetings, write clear updates and practise handling disagreement.
  4. Pick a certification that fits your stage. The table below summarises the main options.
  5. Grow your experience. Record the projects you lead, their size and outcomes; certifications such as the PMP require documented experience.

PMPPMIPractising project managers; requires 35 hours of project management education plus documented experience
PRINCE2 Foundation and PractitionerPeopleCertRoles in the UK, Europe and government

Frequently asked questions

What is project management in simple terms?

Project management is organising a one-off piece of work so that it delivers what was agreed, on time and within budget. It means deciding what will be done, planning who does what and when, tracking progress and dealing with problems before they grow.

What are the 5 phases of project management?

Initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure. Monitoring and controlling runs alongside execution rather than after it. See project management phases.

What is an example of project management?

Redesigning a company website is a typical example: the project manager agrees the goal and budget in a charter, breaks the work into design, content, build and testing, schedules it over 12 weeks, tracks progress and costs, manages risks such as late content, and closes the project with a handover and lessons-learned review.

What are the three main constraints in project management?

Scope, time and cost, known as the triple constraint, with quality at the centre. Changing one usually affects at least one of the others. Modern standards also consider risk, resources and benefits. See project constraints.

What does a project manager actually do?

A project manager defines goals and scope with the sponsor, builds the plan, coordinates the team, manages risks, budget and stakeholders, reports progress and closes the project. Day to day, much of the job is communication: check-ins, updates and decisions. See project manager roles and responsibilities.

What are the main types of project management?

By approach, the main types are predictive (Waterfall), adaptive (Agile, including Scrum and Kanban) and hybrid. By industry, common types include construction, IT, software, marketing, healthcare and event project management. See project management methodologies.

What is the difference between project management and operations management?

Project management delivers a temporary, unique result and then ends; operations management runs ongoing, repeatable work. Implementing a new payroll system is a project; running payroll every month is operations. See what is a project.

What is the difference between project management and product management?

A project manager is responsible for delivering a defined result within time, cost and scope. A product manager decides what product to build and why, and owns it for its whole life. The two roles work closely together, especially in software.

What skills do you need for project management?

Planning, estimating, scheduling and budgeting, plus communication, leadership, negotiation and stakeholder management. Judgement, organisation and calm under pressure matter as much as technical method. See project management skills.

Is project management a good career?

Project management skills are used in almost every industry, from IT and construction to healthcare and government, so the role offers variety and clear progression from coordinator to project, programme and portfolio manager. It suits people who enjoy organising, problem-solving and working with others.

Can you get into project management with no experience?

Yes. Many people start by coordinating small projects in their current role or by working as a project coordinator or assistant project manager. The CAPM certification is designed for people without project leadership experience. See how to become a project manager.

Do I need a certification to work in project management?

No, but a certification shows employers that you know the methods and terminology. Common choices are CAPM for beginners, PMP for experienced project managers, PRINCE2 in the UK and Europe, and Scrum certifications for Agile roles. See project management certification.

What tools are used in project management?

Common techniques include the work breakdown structure, Gantt charts, the critical path method, Kanban boards, RACI matrices, risk registers and status reports. Teams usually manage them in project management software or, for small projects, spreadsheets and templates. See our project management software rankings.

What is the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

It is the current edition of PMI's project management standard and guide, published in November 2025. It is built on six principles and seven performance domains (governance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources and risk) and reintroduces 40 non-prescriptive processes grouped into five focus areas. See our PMBOK Guide overview.

Is Agile a type of project management?

Agile is an adaptive approach to project management in which the team delivers in short iterations and adjusts based on feedback. Scrum and Kanban are popular ways to apply it. See what is Agile project management.
