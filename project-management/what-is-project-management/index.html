What Is Project Management? Definition, Process, Examples

Project management is the discipline of planning, organising and leading work to deliver a specific result within agreed limits of time, cost, scope and quality. It turns an idea, such as a new website, an office move or a product launch, into a plan people can carry out and leaders can track. This guide explains the five phases, the constraints you balance, the main methods and roles, and walks through a worked example from start to finish.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 4 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Project management applies knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to a temporary piece of work so that it delivers an agreed result.
  • Every project balances scope, time and cost, with quality at the centre; changing one almost always affects the others.
  • Projects move through five phases: initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure.
  • You can run a project with a predictive (Waterfall), adaptive (Agile) or hybrid approach, depending on how stable the requirements are.
  • The current PMI standard, the PMBOK Guide 8th edition (November 2025), is built on six principles and seven performance domains.
  • Start small: a one-page charter, a task list and a weekly status check cover most simple projects.

What is project management?

Project management is the practice of planning, organising, leading and controlling the work of a temporary project so that it delivers a defined result, such as a product, service or change, within agreed limits of time, cost, scope and quality. It covers the whole journey, from agreeing why the project exists to handing over the result and capturing what the team learned.

The professional bodies use similar wording. Each definition stresses something slightly different, which is worth knowing if you plan to sit an exam.

SourceDefinition (summarised)What it emphasises
PMI (PMBOK Guide)The application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirementsMeeting requirements
APM (UK Association for Project Management)The application of processes, methods, knowledge, skills and experience to achieve specific objectives for changeProjects as the way organisations deliver change

Project management in plain English

Cooking dinner for six friends is a tiny project, but you hardly need project management: everything is in your head and the risks are small. Moving 120 people to a new office in 14 weeks is different. IT, facilities, HR, a removals firm, the landlord and every department are involved, the budget is fixed and the old lease ends on a set date. Without someone defining the scope, sequencing the work, tracking costs and spotting problems early, things fall through the gaps. Project management is the set of practices that stops that happening.

What counts as a project?

A project is a temporary effort, with a defined start and end, undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. Temporary does not mean short: a project can last two weeks or five years. Unique does not mean never done before: your tenth office move is still unique because the building, people and budget differ. Recurring work, such as running payroll every month, is operations.

Why project management matters

Good project management does not guarantee success, but it makes success more likely and failure less surprising. It gives everyone a shared goal, makes trade-offs deliberate, surfaces risks while they are cheap to deal with, gives each piece of work an owner, and lets sponsors trust the forecast. The table shows the problems that most often appear when projects are run informally, and the practice that prevents each one.

SymptomUsual causePractice that helps
The project keeps growingScope was never written down or agreedA scope statement and change control to prevent scope creep
Deadlines slip without warningNo schedule, or one that ignores dependenciesA schedule built from the work breakdown, with milestones
The budget runs out earlyCost was one guess, not built up from the workA bottom-up budget and regular cost tracking
Work is done twice or not at allUnclear rolesA RACI matrix naming who is responsible and accountable
A problem is found too late to fix cheaplyRisks were never discussedA risk register reviewed at every status meeting
Senior people are unhappy at the endExpectations were never confirmedStakeholder analysis and a communication plan

The triple constraint and other project constraints

Every project works within limits. The best-known model is the triple constraint, or project management triangle: scope (what you deliver), time (when) and cost (money and effort). Quality sits in the middle, because squeezing one side without adjusting another usually lowers quality. Modern standards treat the triangle as a starting point: most projects also manage risk, resources and benefits.

Worked example: a deadline moves forward

Your project to launch a customer portal is planned for 12 weeks with a budget of £80,000. The sales director asks for launch in 9 weeks to coincide with a trade show. Rather than simply agreeing, you set out the options:

OptionWhat changesConsequence
Reduce scopeLaunch core features only; add reporting in a second releaseDate met, budget held; some users wait for reporting
Add resourcesTwo contract developers for six weeksDate likely met; cost rises by an estimated £18,000; onboarding time needed
Overlap tasks (fast-tracking)Start testing before development is completeDate possibly met; higher risk of rework
Keep the planLaunch in week 12 as agreedNo extra cost or risk; trade-show opportunity missed

The sponsor makes the final call, because it is a business trade-off. Your job as project manager is to make the trade-off visible with honest numbers.

The project management process: 5 phases

Most projects move through five phases, often called the project life cycle. PMI's process groups, and the five focus areas in the PMBOK Guide 8th edition, use the same names.

PhaseKey questionMain activitiesTypical outputs
1. InitiationShould we do this, and why?Confirm the need, check feasibility, appoint a sponsor and project manager, identify stakeholdersBusiness case, project charter, stakeholder register
2. PlanningHow will we do it?Define scope, break down the work, estimate, schedule, budget, plan risk, quality and communicationScope statement, WBS, schedule, budget, risk register, project management plan
3. ExecutionAre we doing the work?Build the deliverables, lead the team, manage suppliers, engage stakeholdersDeliverables, issue log, change requests
4. Monitoring and controllingAre we on track?Compare progress with the baseline, manage changes, risks and issues, report statusStatus reports, updated forecasts, approved changes
5. ClosureDid we deliver, and what did we learn?Formal acceptance, handover to operations, close contracts, release the team, review lessonsAcceptance sign-off, lessons-learned report, archived records

Two points are often missed. Monitoring and controlling is not a separate stage: it runs alongside execution from the first day of work. And in Agile projects, planning, execution and review repeat in short cycles, while initiation and closure still happen once.

How to manage a project step by step

If you have just been handed your first project, this ten-step process works for most small and medium projects.

  1. Confirm the purpose. Write down, in two or three sentences, the problem the project solves and how success will be measured.
  2. Identify stakeholders. List everyone affected by the project or able to influence it, and what each needs from you.
  3. Write a project charter. Capture purpose, objectives, high-level scope, milestones, budget, risks and the sponsor's sign-off on one or two pages. Our free project charter template gives you the structure.
  4. Define the scope. List what is included and, just as important, what is excluded.
  5. Break down the work. Split the deliverables into pieces small enough to estimate and assign: the work breakdown structure (WBS).
  6. Build the schedule. Estimate each task, identify dependencies, set milestones and find the critical path, usually on a Gantt chart.
  7. Set the budget. Add up labour, materials, suppliers and a contingency reserve for known risks.
  8. Plan risks, roles and communication. Start a risk register template, agree who is responsible for what, and decide who gets which update and how often.
  9. Execute, track and adjust. Hold short regular check-ins, update progress weekly against the plan, and assess every change request for its effect on scope, time and cost.
  10. Close the project. Get formal acceptance, hand over to the people who will run the result, close contracts, thank the team and hold a lessons-learned review.
Tip

Scale the paperwork to the project. A two-week internal project might need only a one-page charter, a task list and a five-minute weekly check-in. A two-year capital project needs every document above and formal governance. The thinking behind each step matters more than the document.

Worked example: a website redesign from start to finish

To see how the pieces fit together, follow one project through its life cycle. A 60-person professional services firm wants to replace its outdated website. The figures are illustrative.

Initiation: the charter

The marketing director sponsors the project and appoints a project manager, who writes a one-page charter:

Charter itemContent
PurposeReplace a website that is hard to update and performs poorly on mobile
ObjectivesLaunch within 12 weeks; double contact-form enquiries within six months; meet accessibility guidelines
In scopeDesign, build, migration of 40 key pages, contact forms, analytics, staff training
Out of scopeClient portal, online payments, translation
Budget£60,000 including a £5,000 contingency reserve
MilestonesDesign approved (week 4), build complete (week 9), launch (week 12)
Main risksContent late; design approval delayed; agency capacity

Planning: breaking down the work

With the web agency, the project manager builds a WBS and turns it into a schedule.

WBS elementMain deliverablesOwnerWeeks
1. DiscoveryRequirements, site map, content auditProject manager and agency1 to 2
2. DesignWireframes, visual design, approvalAgency designer2 to 4
3. ContentRewritten copy for 40 pages, imagesMarketing team3 to 8
4. BuildTemplates, forms, analytics, accessibility checksAgency developers5 to 9
5. TestingFunctional, browser, accessibility and user acceptance testingAgency and project team9 to 11
6. Launch and handoverGo-live, redirects, staff training, support handoverProject manager11 to 12

Content sits on the critical path: if the copy is late, build and testing cannot finish on time. The risk register reflects that:

RiskLikelihoodImpactResponseOwner
Copy for 40 pages is lateHighHighPage-by-page content schedule; prioritise the 15 most visited pages; freelance copywriter as a fallbackMarketing manager
Partners disagree on the designMediumMediumShow two design routes early; one named decision-makerSponsor
Agency loses a developer mid-buildLowHighContract requires a named back-up; weekly demosProject manager

Monitoring: a status check in week 6

At the end of week 6 the project manager runs a simple earned value check. The budget at completion (BAC) is £60,000. The plan said £30,000 of work should be done by now (planned value, PV). The work actually completed is worth £24,000 of the budget (earned value, EV), and £27,000 has been spent (actual cost, AC).

MeasureFormulaResultMeaning
Schedule variance (SV)EV - PV£24,000 - £30,000 = -£6,000Behind schedule
Cost variance (CV)EV - AC£24,000 - £27,000 = -£3,000Over budget for the work done
Schedule performance index (SPI)EV / PV0.8080% of the planned work is done
Cost performance index (CPI)EV / AC0.8989p of value for every £1 spent
Estimate at completion (EAC)BAC / CPI£60,000 / 0.89 = about £67,500Final cost if nothing changes

The cause is the content risk coming true: only 12 of 40 pages are ready. The project manager offers the sponsor two options: a freelance copywriter for four weeks (about £4,000, using most of the contingency) or launch with 25 pages and add the rest a month later. The sponsor chooses the copywriter. Because this removes the bottleneck, the project manager forecasts a final cost of about £62,000 rather than £67,500, and the sponsor approves a £2,000 budget increase. The decision goes in the change log and the risk register is updated.

Closure: handover and lessons

The site launches in week 12 at a final cost of £61,800, within the revised budget. The sponsor signs acceptance, the agency hands over access and documentation, marketing staff are trained to edit pages and support moves to the agency's maintenance contract. A one-hour review records three lessons: start content first, name a single design approver, and track content weekly from day one.

Knowledge areas, principles and performance domains

If you study project management formally, you will meet three ways of organising the subject, depending on which edition of PMI's PMBOK Guide you read. Many courses and job adverts still use the older terms.

PMBOK 6th edition (2017)PMBOK 7th edition (2021)PMBOK 8th edition (November 2025)
Organised aroundProcessesPrinciples and outcomesPrinciples, domains and non-prescriptive processes
Main structure10 knowledge areas, 5 process groups12 principles, 8 performance domains6 principles, 7 performance domains, 5 focus areas
Areas or domainsIntegration, scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement, stakeholdersStakeholders, team, development approach and life cycle, planning, project work, delivery, measurement, uncertaintyGovernance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources, risk
Processes49 defined processesProcess detail largely removed40 processes grouped under initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, closing

According to PMI, the 8th edition simplifies the 7th edition's principles and domains, brings back process guidance in a non-prescriptive form, and expands coverage of AI, project management offices and procurement. Its six principles include adopting a holistic view, focusing on value, embedding quality and integrating sustainability.

PRINCE2, the other widely used framework, is organised differently: PRINCE2 7 (2023) has seven principles, seven practices and seven processes, with people at the centre.

Tip

Whichever framework you follow, the underlying questions are the same: what are we delivering and why, how will we do it, who is involved, what could go wrong, and how will we know we are on track?

Project management methodologies and approaches

A methodology is a defined way of running projects. There are three broad approaches: predictive (plan in detail up front), adaptive (plan and deliver in short iterations) and hybrid (a mix of both). Within those sit specific methods.

MethodologyHow it worksOften used for
WaterfallSequential phases, each approved before the next startsConstruction, regulated projects, fixed-price contracts
AgileDeliver working results in short iterations, gather feedback and adaptSoftware, digital products, marketing
ScrumAgile framework with fixed-length Sprints, a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and DevelopersProduct development teams
KanbanVisualise work on a board, limit work in progress, improve flowSupport, operations, content teams
PRINCE2Stage-based method with strong governance and business justificationGovernment and large organisations
Lean and Six SigmaRemove waste (Lean) and reduce variation using data (Six Sigma)Process improvement, manufacturing
HybridPredictive governance and milestones with Agile delivery insideLarge organisations, product launches with fixed dates

How to choose an approach

There is no single best methodology. Look at your project and lean towards the approach that suits most of its characteristics.

CharacteristicLeans predictiveLeans adaptive (Agile)
RequirementsClear and stable from the startExpected to change as you learn
Customer involvementAt set approval pointsOften, ideally every iteration
Cost of late changeHigh (concrete already poured)Low (software can be updated)
Value of early partial deliveryLittle: useful only when completeHigh: users benefit from early releases
ContractsFixed scope and price, formal sign-offsFlexible scope within a time and budget

If your answers are mixed, a hybrid approach is usually sensible. Each industry also adds its own vocabulary and risks; see project management by industry for construction, IT, marketing, healthcare and more.

Who is involved: roles in project management

Titles vary between organisations, but these are the roles you will meet most often.

RoleMain responsibility
SponsorOwns the business case, provides funding, makes key decisions and removes obstacles
Project managerPlans and coordinates the work, leads the team, manages risks, stakeholders and budget, reports progress
Project teamSpecialists who carry out the work and produce the deliverables
StakeholdersAnyone affected by the project or able to influence it: users, customers, suppliers, regulators
Product Owner and Scrum MasterIn Scrum, the Product Owner maximises product value and the Scrum Master helps the team use Scrum effectively
PMOA project management office that sets standards, supports projects and reports across the portfolio

What a project manager does in a typical week

  • Runs short check-ins with the team and clears blockers.
  • Updates the schedule, budget and risk register with what actually happened.
  • Reviews change requests and takes significant ones to the sponsor.
  • Sends a short status report: progress, next steps, risks, decisions needed.
  • Looks two to four weeks ahead for resource clashes, approvals and supplier deliveries.

To do this well you need technical skills (scoping, estimating, scheduling, budgeting, risk), people skills (communication, leadership, negotiation, facilitation) and business sense (understanding the case for the project and the value it must deliver).

Project management tools and techniques

Techniques help you think; tools help you record, share and track. These are the ones you will use most:

Tool or techniqueQuestion it answers
Work breakdown structureWhat exactly do we have to deliver?
Gantt chart and critical pathWhen does each task happen, and which tasks will delay the whole project if they slip?
Kanban boardWhat is in progress, and where is work getting stuck?
RACI matrixWho is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed?
Risk register or RAID logWhat could go wrong, and what are we doing about it?
Status reportWhat has been done, what is next, and what decisions are needed?
Lessons-learned reviewWhat should we repeat or change next time?

A short history of project management

People have managed large projects for thousands of years, but project management as a named discipline is mostly a twentieth-century development.

WhenDevelopment
1910sHenry Gantt popularises the bar chart that carries his name
Late 1950sThe critical path method (CPM) is developed in industry, and PERT for the US Navy's Polaris programme
1969The Project Management Institute (PMI) is founded
1996PMI publishes the first edition of the PMBOK Guide, and PRINCE2 is released in the UK
2001The Agile Manifesto is published
2021 to 2023PMBOK 7th edition moves to principles and performance domains; PRINCE2 7 is released
2025 to 2026PMBOK 8th edition (November 2025); PMI launches an updated PMP exam (July 2026)

Common project management mistakes

Most project problems come from a few habits. Spotting them early is half the fix.

MistakeWhat it looks likeHow to fix it
Starting without a clear objectivePeople describe the project differentlyWrite a charter and have the sponsor sign it
Planning tasks but not scopeLong task lists, no agreement on what is excludedWrite in-scope and out-of-scope lists before scheduling
Estimating top-down only"It should take about three months"Estimate from the WBS with the people doing the work
No contingencyAny problem becomes a crisisHold a reserve of time and money linked to known risks
Saying yes to every changeScope grows, the date does not moveAssess each change for impact; let the sponsor decide
Reporting only good newsGreen status until the week before the deadlineReport early and honestly, and bring options

How to get started in project management

Many project managers start "accidentally", by being asked to coordinate something at work. To move into the field deliberately:

  1. Learn the fundamentals: this guide, the five phases and the core documents (charter, scope, schedule, budget, risk register).
  2. Practise on a small project: an internal improvement, an event or a system roll-out, using the templates above.
  3. Build your people skills: run meetings, write clear updates and practise handling disagreement.
  4. Choose a certification that fits your stage (see the table) and keep a record of the projects you lead.
CertificationAwarding bodySuits
CAPMPMIBeginners without project leadership experience
PMPPMIPractising project managers; requires 35 hours of project management education plus documented experience
PRINCE2 Foundation and PractitionerPeopleCertRoles in the UK, Europe and government
Scrum certificationsScrum.org, Scrum Alliance and othersAgile and product teams

Frequently asked questions

What is project management in simple terms?

Organising a one-off piece of work so that it delivers what was agreed, on time and within budget: deciding what will be done, planning who does what and when, tracking progress and dealing with problems before they grow.

What are the 5 phases of project management?

Initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure. Monitoring and controlling runs alongside execution rather than after it.

What is an example of project management?

Redesigning a company website: the project manager agrees the goal and budget in a charter, breaks the work into design, content, build and testing, schedules it over 12 weeks, tracks progress and cost, manages risks such as late content, and closes with a handover and lessons-learned review.

What are the three main constraints in project management?

Scope, time and cost, known as the triple constraint, with quality at the centre. Changing one usually affects at least one of the others.

What does a project manager actually do?

Defines goals and scope with the sponsor, builds the plan, coordinates the team, manages risks, budget and stakeholders, reports progress and closes the project. Much of the day-to-day job is communication.

What are the main types of project management?

By approach: predictive (Waterfall), adaptive (Agile, including Scrum and Kanban) and hybrid. By industry: construction, IT, software, marketing, healthcare, events and many more.

What is the difference between project management and operations management?

Project management delivers a temporary, unique result and then ends; operations management runs ongoing, repeatable work. Implementing a payroll system is a project; running payroll every month is operations.

What is the difference between project management and product management?

A project manager delivers a defined result within time, cost and scope. A product manager decides what product to build and why, and owns it for its whole life. The roles work closely together, especially in software.

What skills do you need for project management?

Planning, estimating, scheduling and budgeting, plus communication, leadership, negotiation and stakeholder management. Judgement and calm under pressure matter as much as method.

Is project management a good career?

Project management skills are used in almost every industry, so the role offers variety and clear progression from coordinator to project, programme and portfolio manager. It suits people who enjoy organising, problem-solving and working with others.

Can you get into project management with no experience?

Yes. Many people start by coordinating small projects in their current job, or as a project coordinator or assistant project manager. The CAPM is designed for people without project leadership experience.

Do I need a certification to work in project management?

No, but a certification shows employers you know the methods and terminology. Common choices are CAPM for beginners, PMP for experienced project managers, PRINCE2 in the UK and Europe, and Scrum certifications for Agile roles.

What tools are used in project management?

Work breakdown structures, Gantt charts, the critical path method, Kanban boards, RACI matrices, risk registers and status reports, usually managed in project management software or, for small projects, spreadsheets and templates.

What is the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

The current edition of PMI's project management standard and guide, published in November 2025. It is built on six principles and seven performance domains (governance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources and risk) and includes 40 non-prescriptive processes grouped into five focus areas.
