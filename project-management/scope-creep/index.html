Scope Creep: Causes, Examples and How to Prevent It

Scope creep is the uncontrolled growth of a project's scope without corresponding adjustments to time, cost or resources. It is one of the most common reasons projects run late and over budget.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 16 September 2026
  • 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • Scope creep is uncontrolled scope growth.
  • Clear exclusions and change control prevent it.
  • Always show the time and cost impact of new requests.

Common causes

  • Vague or incomplete requirements.
  • No formal change control process.
  • Stakeholders adding requests directly to the team.
  • Gold plating by the team.
  • Poor communication of exclusions.
  • A sponsor who isn't engaged in trade-off decisions.

Examples

  • A website project gradually adds a blog, a chatbot and multilingual support without extending the deadline.
  • An office move expands to include new furniture and IT upgrades without extra budget.
  • A report grows from five charts to twenty because each meeting adds "just one more".

Warning signs

  • Tasks appear that aren't in the WBS.
  • Requests arrive by email or chat, bypassing the project manager.
  • The team works overtime without a schedule change.
  • Deliverables are repeatedly "almost done".

How to prevent it

  1. Write a clear scope statement with exclusions.
  2. Get it signed off.
  3. Set up change control: log, assess, decide, update.
  4. Show the impact of every change on time and cost.
  5. Educate stakeholders and the team about the process.
  6. In Agile projects, route new requests to the backlog for the Product Owner to prioritise.

Frequently asked questions

What is scope creep?

Uncontrolled growth in project scope without matching changes to time, cost or resources.

Is all scope change scope creep?

No. Approved changes that add value are normal; uncontrolled changes are the problem.

Is gold plating a form of scope creep?

Yes. Gold plating means adding unrequested features, usually by the team, which is a form of scope creep.

Who is responsible for preventing scope creep?

The project manager, supported by the sponsor and a clear change process.

How do you say no to a stakeholder to avoid scope creep?

Explain the impact on time and cost and offer options: defer, swap for something else, or approve with more resources.

Does Agile prevent scope creep?

Agile expects scope to change but controls it through backlog prioritisation within fixed iterations.

How does a change request help prevent scope creep?

A change request is a formal proposal to modify scope, schedule, cost or other baselines, so changes are assessed and approved rather than creeping in.

How do you measure scope creep?

Compare current scope with the baseline and track the number and impact of unapproved changes.

Can scope creep be positive?

Unplanned improvements can add value, but they should still go through change control.

What tools help control scope creep?

A scope statement, WBS, change log and project management software with approval workflows.
