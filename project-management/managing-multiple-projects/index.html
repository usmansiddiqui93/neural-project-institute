Managing Multiple Projects: A Practical System That Works

Managing multiple projects means running several projects at the same time while keeping priorities, deadlines and people under control. The approach that works is a simple system rather than heroic effort: one master view of all your work, priorities agreed in the open, a capacity check before you commit, a fixed weekly routine, and delegation and reporting you do once rather than five times. This guide sets out that system step by step, with worked examples you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • Keep every project in one master view with the same columns, so you can compare them at a glance.
  • Rank projects with an agreed scoring model; the ranking, not the loudest stakeholder, settles conflicts.
  • Check capacity before saying yes: compare the hours your projects need with the hours you really have.
  • Context switching has a real cost, so batch similar work and limit how many projects are active at once.
  • Run a fixed weekly rhythm: plan on Monday, review risks midweek, report and replan on Friday.
  • When a new urgent project arrives, make the trade-off explicit: something else must move, shrink or stop.

What does managing multiple projects mean?

Managing multiple projects is the practice of planning, running and controlling several projects at once, often with shared people, budgets and stakeholders. It combines normal project management on each project with a layer of cross-project decisions: which project comes first, who works on what, and when to say no. The goal is to deliver the most valuable work without overloading anyone.

It is not the same as programme or portfolio management, although it borrows from both:

ApproachWhat is being managedTypical owner
Managing multiple projectsSeveral projects, related or not, that one person or team is responsible forProject manager, team lead, agency delivery lead
Programme managementRelated projects coordinated to achieve shared benefitsProgramme manager
Portfolio managementAll projects and programmes in an organisation, selected and prioritised against strategyPortfolio manager or PMO

If your projects share a common goal, you may really be running a programme; see programme vs project vs portfolio management and project manager vs program manager.

Why managing multiple projects is hard

Running one project well is demanding. Running five adds problems that do not exist on any single project:

  • Competing priorities: every sponsor believes their project is the most important.
  • Shared people: the same specialist is needed on two projects in the same week.
  • Context switching: each switch between projects costs time to reload the details.
  • Reporting overhead: five projects can mean five status reports, five meetings and five formats.
  • Invisible work: small projects without a plan quietly consume hours until they become urgent.

The cost of context switching

Gerald Weinberg, in *Quality Software Management: Systems Thinking*, offered a rule of thumb that is still widely quoted: each additional project you work on at the same time costs a share of your time in switching between them. The figures below are his heuristic, not the result of a controlled study, but they match many people's experience.

Projects at the same timeTime available per projectTime lost to switching
1100%0%
240% each20%
320% each40%

The practical lesson is not that you can only run one project, but that you should reduce the number of switches: batch similar work, give each project fixed time blocks and pause low-priority work rather than nudging everything forward a little each day. Kanban teams make the same point with work-in-progress limits.

The multi-project system at a glance

The rest of this guide explains seven steps. Together they form a system you can set up in an afternoon and run in about two hours a week.

StepWhat you doWhat it prevents
1. Master viewList every project in one place with the same columnsSurprises, forgotten projects, scattered information
2. PrioritiseScore and rank projects with agreed criteriaDecisions driven by whoever shouts loudest
3. Capacity checkCompare hours needed with hours availableOvercommitment and burnout
4. Weekly rhythmPlan, review and report on fixed daysReactive, interrupt-driven weeks
5. Shared peopleResolve resource conflicts with clear rulesTwo projects claiming the same person
6. Delegate and standardiseHand off routine work and reuse templatesYou becoming the bottleneck

Step 1: build one master view

Put every project you are responsible for, including the small ones, in one list with the same columns. This can be a spreadsheet, a board or a portfolio view in a project management tool. What matters is that it is the single place you and your stakeholders look.

ColumnWhat to recordWhy it helps
ProjectShort name and IDCommon reference in conversations
SponsorThe person who decides on scope and priorityYou know whom to escalate to
Priority rankResult of Step 2Settles conflicts quickly
Status (RAG)Red, amber or green, using agreed definitionsCompare health across projects
Next milestone and dateThe next significant checkpointShows what is coming up across all projects
Hours needed this monthYour estimate of effort, yours and the team'sFeeds the capacity check
Key peopleWho is working on itReveals shared-resource conflicts
Top risk or issueOne line, linked to the full logHighlights where attention is needed
Next actionThe very next step and its ownerKeeps every project moving

Agree what each status colour means, or RAG becomes opinion. For example: green means on track to hit the next milestone; amber means at risk but recoverable within the project's own tolerance; red means the milestone will be missed or a decision is needed from the sponsor.

Our project tracker template gives you a ready-made structure, and a single RAID log with a project column keeps risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies for all projects in one place.

Tip

Include work that is not formally a project but takes regular time, such as recurring reports or supplier reviews. If it uses your hours, it belongs in the capacity check.

Step 2: prioritise with a scoring model

When everything is urgent, nothing is. A simple weighted scoring model turns a political argument into a transparent decision. Agree the criteria and weights with your manager or the sponsors first, then score each project from 1 (low) to 5 (high).

CriterionWeightWhat a 5 looks like
Strategic or business value30%Directly supports a top organisational goal or major revenue
Deadline urgency25%Fixed external date within weeks
Cost of delay20%Penalties, lost revenue or compliance exposure if late
Ease (inverse of effort)15%Small effort for the value delivered
Dependencies10%Other projects or teams are waiting on it

Worked example: ranking five projects

A project manager in a mid-sized company is responsible for five projects. Each is scored against the criteria, and the weighted score is the sum of score x weight.

ProjectValueUrgencyCost of delayEaseDependenciesWeighted scoreRank
A. Regulatory reporting change455344.301
B. CRM migration534233.652
D. Supplier onboarding process343413.203
C. Intranet refresh322422.604
E. Documentation clean-up211511.905

For project A: (4 x 0.30) + (5 x 0.25) + (5 x 0.20) + (3 x 0.15) + (4 x 0.10) = 1.20 + 1.25 + 1.00 + 0.45 + 0.40 = 4.30.

Share the ranking with all sponsors. When projects A and C need the same week, there is nothing to argue about: A goes first unless the sponsors agree to change the scores. Revisit the scores monthly or when something significant changes. Our priority list template does the scoring and sorting for you.

Tip

Keep the model simple. Five criteria are enough. If people start debating whether a score is 3.5 or 4, the model has done its job: the discussion is now about value and urgency, not volume.

Step 3: check capacity before you commit

Priorities tell you the order. Capacity tells you how much of the list you can actually do. Most overload comes from never doing this sum.

Worked example: closing a capacity gap

Continuing the example, the project manager works 40 hours a week. Line management, email and general administration take about 8 hours, so 32 hours are available for project work. Their estimates of weekly hours needed are A 12, B 10, D 8, C 6 and E 3, a total of 39 hours. Allowing roughly 10% for switching between five projects (our own planning assumption, not a standard) adds about 4 hours, so demand is around 43 hours against 32. The gap is 11 hours a week.

Working late every night is not a plan. Instead, they take the numbers to their manager with options, starting with the lowest-ranked work:

ActionHours saved per weekAgreed with
Pause E (documentation clean-up) for six weeks3Manager
Hand day-to-day coordination of C to a project coordinator4Manager and C's sponsor
Move one milestone on D by two weeks, reducing its weekly effort3D's sponsor
Lower switching overhead with four active projects instead of five1No approval needed
Total saved11

Demand falls to about 32 hours, matching the hours available. The highest-priority projects are untouched, and every trade-off was agreed by the person who owns it.

The same method scales to a team: add up each person's available hours, compare with demand by role, and resolve gaps before they become missed dates.

Step 4: plan your week in a fixed rhythm

A fixed weekly rhythm stops multiple projects from turning every day into a reaction to the latest message. The routine below takes around two hours of planning and reporting time each week.

WhenTime boxActivityOutput
Monday morning45 minutesReview the master view; choose the three most important outcomes for the week; block calendar timeWeekly plan and time blocks
Every day15 minutesCheck today's blocks, deadlines and blockers across all projectsUpdated next actions
Tuesday to ThursdayFixed blocksFocused project work, one project per blockProgress on priority outcomes
Wednesday30 minutesCross-project risk and issue review; check dependencies and shared peopleUpdated RAID log, early escalations
Friday afternoon45 minutesUpdate statuses, send one combined report, plan next week's prioritiesPortfolio report and draft plan

Time blocking and batching

Give each high-priority project its own blocks of at least 90 minutes, and protect them as you would a meeting. Then batch similar tasks across projects: write all status updates in one sitting, hold supplier calls back to back, and review all approvals together. Some people go further and theme days, such as Tuesday for project B and Thursday for project D, which works well when stakeholders know when to expect you.

Keep meetings proportional

Not every project needs a weekly hour-long meeting. Match meeting time to priority and risk: a short stand-up for the top project, a fortnightly check-in for stable ones, and an asynchronous written update for the rest.

Step 5: share people between projects

When two projects need the same person at the same time, resolve it with a rule rather than a fight. Work through these steps in order:

  1. Check the ranking. If one project is clearly higher priority, it gets the person first.
  2. Check the critical path. If the task is on one project's critical path and has float on the other, schedule around the float.
  3. Level the work. Move non-critical tasks to smooth the person's workload; see resource leveling.
  4. Find an alternative. Another person, a contractor or a reduced scope may solve it.
  5. Escalate with options. If none of this works, take the conflict to both sponsors with the impact of each choice.

Make roles clear on every project so people know what they are accountable for. A RACI matrix per project, built from the same RACI template, avoids gaps and duplicated effort. Limiting the number of projects each specialist works on at once also reduces their own switching cost.

Step 6: delegate and standardise

If every decision and update has to pass through you, you become the bottleneck for every project. Delegate what does not need your judgement:

  • meeting scheduling, minutes and action tracking;
  • collecting updates from team members;
  • maintaining trackers and logs;
  • first drafts of routine reports and documents;
  • day-to-day coordination of stable, low-risk projects.

A project coordinator or a capable team member can often take these on, which is also good development for them. Agree what they can decide alone and when they should come to you.

Standardise everything you repeat. Use the same plan structure, status report, risk log and kick-off agenda on every project. Each new project then starts from a template rather than a blank page, and stakeholders learn where to find information. Capture what worked in each project's close-out and fold it back into the templates.

Step 7: report once, for everyone

Instead of writing separate reports for each project, produce one short portfolio report or dashboard from the master view, and give each sponsor their section. A good one-page report shows, for each project: RAG status, progress against the next milestone, the top risk or issue, decisions needed, and what happens next week.

Report by exception: say little about green projects and focus attention on amber and red ones, with a clear ask. If you use a project management tool, a shared dashboard can replace most of the written report. For the format of individual reports, see our project status report guide.

Handling a new urgent project

New requests are the biggest threat to a working multi-project system. Use the same steps every time, so saying 'not yet' is a process rather than a personal refusal:

  1. Capture it through one intake route, such as a work request form, rather than accepting work in corridors.
  2. Score it with the same model as your current projects.
  3. Estimate the effort and check it against your capacity.
  4. Show the trade-off: which existing work would move, shrink or stop to make room.
  5. Let the right person decide: the sponsors or your manager, not you alone.
  6. Update the master view and tell every affected stakeholder.

A short script helps: 'I can start this next week. To do that, project C's next milestone would move from the 10th to the 24th, or we pause project E. Which would you prefer?' You are not refusing the work; you are making its cost visible. Managing these conversations well is a core part of stakeholder management.

How the approach changes with the number of projects

SituationWhat worksWatch out for
2 or 3 projectsA simple shared list, time blocks per project, a combined Friday updateLetting the noisiest project take all your attention
4 to 8 projectsFull master view, scoring model, capacity check, a coordinator for routine work, a dashboardContext switching and over-commitment of shared specialists
Many projects across teamsA formal intake process, portfolio governance, resource management and a PMOStarting too many projects at once; decisions without capacity data

At the larger end, multi-project management becomes portfolio management. A project management office (PMO) often owns the intake process, standards and portfolio reporting, and Agile organisations apply similar ideas through Agile portfolio management.

Tools for managing multiple projects

A spreadsheet is fine for a handful of small projects. Once you have shared people, dependencies and several sponsors, a project management tool saves a great deal of manual updating. Look for:

  • Portfolio or cross-project views: all projects in one list or timeline with consistent fields.
  • Workload or resource views: who is over- or under-allocated across projects.
  • Dashboards: live status for sponsors, so you write fewer reports.
  • Request forms: one intake route for new work.
  • Templates: new projects start with your standard structure.
  • Dependencies and Gantt charts: to see how a delay on one project affects another.

Wrike, our top-ranked tool, is a good fit for people running many projects with shared resources, because it combines cross-project portfolio views, workload charts, dashboards and request forms in one platform; it takes some setup time to get the most from it. monday.com and Asana are easier to start with and also offer multi-project views, and Smartsheet suits people who like spreadsheet-style planning.

Common mistakes when managing multiple projects

  • Saying yes without a trade-off: every new project uses capacity that was already allocated.
  • Keeping priorities in your head: if the ranking is not written and shared, every stakeholder assumes theirs is first.
  • Starting everything at once: five half-finished projects deliver less value than three finished ones.
  • Different formats for every project: you lose time translating, and stakeholders cannot compare.
  • Ignoring small projects: they skip risk reviews and then become urgent.
  • Not counting your non-project work: meetings, email and line management are real hours.
  • Doing everything yourself: routine coordination is the first thing to delegate.
  • Hiding overload: working evenings to cover a capacity gap hides the problem from the people who could fix it.
  • Never re-ranking: priorities change; review the scores at least monthly.

How to answer 'how do you manage multiple projects?' in an interview

Interviewers ask this to see whether you have a repeatable method, not just stamina. Structure your answer around your system and back it with a real example:

  1. State your method in one sentence: for example, one master view, agreed priorities, a capacity check and a weekly rhythm.
  2. Explain how you prioritise: the criteria you use and who agrees them.
  3. Describe how you protect capacity: time blocks, delegation and how you handle new requests.
  4. Give a specific example: the number of projects, a conflict you faced, what you did and the measurable result.
  5. Mention communication: how you kept sponsors informed and made trade-offs visible.

Keep the example honest and specific. A short story about negotiating a milestone change with data is more convincing than a claim that you never miss a deadline.

Frequently asked questions

How do you manage multiple projects at once?

Keep all projects in one master view, rank them with agreed criteria, check your capacity before committing, follow a fixed weekly routine of planning, review and reporting, delegate routine work and produce one combined report for all stakeholders.

How many projects can a project manager handle?

There is no fixed number. It depends on project size, complexity, risk and the support available. One large, complex project can be a full-time job, while an experienced project manager with a coordinator might run several small, stable projects. Use a capacity check rather than a rule.

How do I prioritise multiple projects?

Agree criteria such as business value, deadline urgency, cost of delay, effort and dependencies with your manager or sponsors, score each project, and rank them by weighted score. Share the ranking and use it to settle conflicts, reviewing it at least monthly.

What is the best tool for managing multiple projects?

Choose a tool with cross-project portfolio views, workload views, dashboards and templates. Wrike is our top-ranked tool for multi-project and resource management; monday.com, Asana and Smartsheet are also strong options. See our project management tools ranking.

How do I track multiple projects in Excel or Google Sheets?

Create one sheet with a row per project and columns for sponsor, priority, RAG status, next milestone and date, hours needed, key people, top risk and next action. Update it weekly and use filters or conditional formatting to highlight red projects.

What do I do when two projects need the same person?

Check the priority ranking first, then the critical path and float on each project. Level non-critical work, look for an alternative person or reduced scope, and if that fails, escalate to both sponsors with the impact of each option.

How do I avoid burnout when managing multiple projects?

Count your real capacity, including non-project work, and compare it with demand. When there is a gap, raise it early with options rather than working longer hours. Protect focused time, batch similar tasks and delegate routine work.

Is managing multiple projects the same as programme management?

No. Programme management coordinates related projects to achieve shared benefits. Managing multiple projects can involve unrelated projects that happen to have the same project manager or team.

How do I handle a new urgent project when I am already at capacity?

Score it against your current projects, estimate the effort and show what would have to move, shrink or stop to make room. Let the sponsors or your manager decide, then update the master view and inform everyone affected.

Should I use one plan or separate plans for multiple projects?

Keep a separate plan for each project, but roll the key information (status, milestones, effort and people) into one shared view so you can see conflicts and compare projects.

How do I report efficiently on multiple projects?

Produce one short portfolio report or dashboard from your master view, with a section per project showing status, next milestone, top risk and decisions needed. Report by exception and focus on amber and red projects.

Does multitasking help when managing several projects?

Rapidly switching between projects usually slows you down, because each switch costs time to refocus. Gerald Weinberg's widely quoted rule of thumb suggests a significant share of time is lost with each extra concurrent project. Work in focused blocks instead.

What skills do you need to manage multiple projects?

Prioritisation, planning, estimation, delegation, stakeholder communication, negotiation and the discipline to keep one up-to-date view of all your work.

How do I answer 'how do you manage multiple projects' in an interview?

Describe your method (master view, prioritisation, capacity check and weekly rhythm), then give a specific example with the number of projects, a conflict you resolved and the result.
