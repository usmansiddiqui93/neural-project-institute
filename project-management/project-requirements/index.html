Project Requirements: Types, Examples and How to Gather Them

Project requirements are the conditions and capabilities a project's result must meet to satisfy its stakeholders and the business need behind it. They are the foundation for your scope, estimates, tests and acceptance, so getting them clear early saves rework later. This guide explains the main types of requirements with examples, then walks you through gathering, writing, prioritising, tracing and controlling them, using one worked example from start to finish.

Key takeaways

  • Requirements describe what the result must do and the conditions it must meet, not how the team will build it.
  • The main types are business, stakeholder, solution (functional and non-functional), transition and project requirements; high-level requirements in the charter are refined into detailed and technical ones.
  • Gather requirements with several techniques, such as interviews, workshops, observation and prototypes, and always ask why a requirement is needed.
  • Good requirements are necessary, unambiguous, singular, feasible and verifiable, with an ID, a source, a priority and an acceptance criterion.
  • Prioritise with a method such as MoSCoW, trace each requirement to its source, deliverable and test, and change the baseline only through change control.

What are project requirements?

Project requirements are statements of what a project's product, service or result must do, and the conditions it must satisfy, to meet the business need and stakeholder expectations. Each requirement should be specific enough to design, build and test against, and it should describe the need rather than prescribe a particular solution.

A useful test is what, not how. "Customers can pay by card online" is a requirement. "Integrate payment provider X using its hosted checkout" is a design decision. Mixing the two too early closes off options and hides the real need.

Strictly, product requirements describe the result (features, performance, quality) and project requirements describe conditions on the project (milestone dates, reporting, approvals). Most people use "project requirements" for both, as this guide does.

Requirements appear in several places as a project matures. Broad, high-level requirements go into the project charter. Detailed requirements live in a requirements document or business requirements document (BRD), or as user stories in a product backlog. A traceability matrix then links each one to the work and tests that satisfy it.

Why project requirements matter

Almost everything else in planning depends on requirements. Your project scope defines the work needed to deliver them, the work breakdown structure breaks that work down, estimates are based on it, and tests and acceptance criteria check that each requirement has been met. If the requirements are vague, every one of those downstream products inherits the vagueness.

PMI has highlighted this for years. Its Pulse of the Profession in-depth report on requirements management found that nearly half (47 percent) of unsuccessful projects failed to meet their goals because of inaccurate requirements management. The pattern is familiar: a missing requirement is cheap to fix in a workshop and expensive to fix after build or go-live.

  • Shared understanding. Sponsors, users and the delivery team agree on what success looks like before money is spent.
  • Fewer disputes. Clear, testable requirements make acceptance objective rather than a matter of opinion.
  • Controlled change. A baselined set of requirements lets you tell an approved change from scope creep.

High-level requirements

High-level requirements are broad statements of what the result must achieve, captured at initiation, usually in the business case or charter. They are short and written so a sponsor can approve them without technical knowledge. For example: "Suppliers can submit invoices electronically", "Invoices under the approval threshold are processed without manual keying" and "Finance can report on invoice status at any time." Each high-level requirement is later broken down into several detailed functional, non-functional and technical requirements.

Functional vs non-functional requirements

Functional requirements describe behaviour: what happens when a user or system does something. Non-functional requirements describe qualities and limits. A system can meet every functional requirement and still fail if it is slow, insecure or unusable, which is why non-functional requirements are the ones most often forgotten.

Non-functional categoryQuestion it answersExample
PerformanceHow fast, how many?Handles 5,000 invoices a day with no loss of response time
AvailabilityWhen must it be working?Available 07:00 to 20:00 on working days, with planned maintenance outside those hours
SecurityWho may do what?Only approvers can change payment details; all changes are logged
Usability and accessibilityCan people use it easily?A new user can log and code an invoice after a one-hour induction
ComplianceWhich rules apply?Keeps invoice records for the period required by tax rules

Technical requirements

Technical requirements state the technology conditions a solution must meet: platforms, integrations, data formats, security standards, infrastructure and support arrangements. They often come from enterprise architecture or IT security rather than from users. Give each one a source and a reason; a technical requirement nobody can justify is often an untested assumption.

Transition requirements

Transition requirements are temporary. They cover data migration, training, parallel running, cut-over and support during the change, and they disappear once the new state is stable. They are easy to miss because nobody "owns" them until go-live, so ask operations and support teams about them early.

Requirements vs scope, objectives and deliverables

These terms are closely linked and often confused. Objectives say what the project must achieve, requirements say what the result must do to achieve it, scope says what work and deliverables are included, and deliverables are the outputs handed over.

TermAnswersExample from an invoice project
Project objectivesWhat outcome are we after?Reduce processing cost per invoice by a third within 12 months of go-live
RequirementsWhat must the result do or be?The system matches invoices to purchase orders automatically
ScopeWhat work and outputs are in and out?In: invoice capture and approval. Out: expense claims
DeliverablesWhat do we hand over?Configured system, migrated data, trained users, support guide

When work is delivered by a supplier, many of these requirements are written into a statement of work so that both sides agree what will be accepted. See our guide to project scope for how requirements become a scope statement and baseline.

How to gather project requirements: step by step

Requirements gathering (often called elicitation) is a cycle of discovering, analysing, confirming and recording, repeated until the requirements are good enough to plan against. Scale the formality to your project's size and risk.

  1. Confirm the business need and objectives. Read the business case and charter. If you cannot state the problem in one or two sentences, stop and agree it with the sponsor first. Every requirement should trace back to this need.
  2. Identify stakeholders. List everyone who will use, pay for, support, approve or be affected by the result. Use your stakeholder management analysis to decide who needs an interview, who joins workshops and who just reviews. A missed stakeholder is a missed requirement.
  3. Plan the approach. Decide which techniques you will use, who will attend, where requirements will be recorded, how they will be prioritised and who signs them off.
  4. Elicit requirements. Run interviews, workshops, observation sessions and document reviews. Ask about problems and goals, not just features, and ask "why?" until you reach the real need.
  5. Analyse and model. Remove duplicates, split compound statements, resolve conflicts and fill gaps. Process maps and prototypes expose gaps that a list of sentences hides.
  6. Document. Write each requirement in a consistent format with an ID, type, description, source, owner, priority and acceptance criterion.
  7. Prioritise. Agree what is essential for the first release and what can wait, using a method such as MoSCoW.
  8. Validate and sign off. Walk the stakeholders through the requirements, confirm they are correct and complete enough, and record formal approval. Sign-off does not make them permanent; it makes later changes visible and controlled.
  9. Baseline and trace. Freeze the approved set as the requirements baseline and link each requirement to its source, design, deliverable and test in a traceability matrix.
  10. Manage change. Route new or changed requirements through change control, assess their impact on scope, time, cost and risk, and update the baseline only once approved.
Tip

If stakeholders keep proposing solutions ("we need a dashboard"), ask what decision the solution would help them make. The answer is usually the real requirement, and there may be a simpler way to meet it.

Requirements gathering techniques

No single technique finds everything: observation shows what people actually do, workshops resolve conflicts and prototypes catch misunderstandings early. Combine at least two or three.

TechniqueBest forWatch out for
InterviewsSenior stakeholders, experts, sensitive topicsTime-consuming; one person's view may not represent the group
Facilitated workshopsAgreeing requirements across groups and resolving conflicts quicklyDominant voices; needs a skilled facilitator and clear agenda
Observation (job shadowing)Understanding current processes and workaroundsPeople behave differently when watched; takes time
Surveys and questionnairesLarge or dispersed user groups, testing how widespread a need isShallow answers; poor questions give poor data
Document analysisExisting systems, policies, contracts, regulationsDocuments may be out of date
Prototyping and wireframesUser interfaces and reports, where people know it when they see itStakeholders may mistake a prototype for a near-finished product
User stories and story mappingAgile teams building in incrementsStories without acceptance criteria are too vague to test
Process mappingFinding gaps between the current and future processCan drift into redesign before the need is agreed

A gap analysis template is a quick way to structure the current-state versus future-state conversation, and a feasibility study can test whether demanding requirements are realistic before you commit to them.

Questions to ask stakeholders

  • What problem are you trying to solve, and how do you handle it today?
  • What would a good outcome look like in six months?
  • What happens if this requirement is not met? Is there a workaround?
  • Are there rules, policies or regulations that apply?
  • How will you know the requirement has been met?

How to write good requirements

A requirement that can be read two ways will be built one way and tested the other. The requirements engineering standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 lists characteristics of a well-formed individual requirement, including that it is necessary, appropriate, unambiguous, complete, singular (one requirement per statement), feasible, verifiable, correct and conforming to an agreed format. In practice, check every requirement against these questions:

  • Is it needed to meet the business need, or is it a nice-to-have dressed up as a must?
  • Could two people read it differently? Remove words such as fast, easy, flexible, user-friendly and appropriate.
  • Does it state one thing? Split statements joined by "and" or "or".
  • Can it be tested or inspected, with a clear pass or fail?
Vague requirementImproved requirement
The system must be fast.Invoice search returns results within two seconds for 95% of searches with 50 concurrent users.
The system should be user-friendly.A new accounts payable clerk can log and code a standard invoice unaided after a one-hour induction.
Managers can approve and reject invoices and add comments.Split into three: approve an invoice; reject an invoice with a mandatory reason; add a comment visible to the submitter.
Use the best available security.Payment detail changes require approval by a second authorised user, and every change is logged with user, date and time.

Many teams use a consistent sentence pattern. Traditional specifications use "The system shall..." statements. Agile teams use user stories in the form "As a [role], I want [capability] so that [benefit]", each with acceptance criteria, often written as "Given [context], when [action], then [result]". Whatever format you choose, use it consistently. Our user story template gives you the fields to start with.

Prioritising requirements with MoSCoW

Almost every project has more requirements than time or money. MoSCoW, which came out of the DSDM agile method, sorts requirements into four groups so that the most important ones are protected:

CategoryMeaningTest
Must haveEssential; the release is not viable without itWould we cancel or delay go-live if this were missing?
Should haveImportant but not vital; a workaround existsPainful to leave out, but the solution still works
Could haveDesirable; included if time and budget allowSmall impact if left out
Won't have this timeAgreed as out of this releaseRecorded so it is not lost or reopened informally

The Agile Business Consortium, which maintains DSDM, advises keeping Must haves to no more than around 60% of the effort so that there is contingency to absorb problems. If every stakeholder insists everything is a Must, ask the cancellation question in the table and look for a workaround. Alternatives include value versus effort scoring and the Kano model.

Worked example: requirements for an invoice-processing project

This illustrative example follows a mid-sized company replacing paper-based supplier invoice handling with an automated system. The names and numbers are made up to show the method.

Business need: accounts payable keys around 2,000 paper and emailed invoices a month by hand, approvals are chased by email and late payments are damaging supplier relationships. Objective: cut processing cost per invoice by a third and pay 95% of approved invoices on time within 12 months of go-live.

High-level requirements (in the charter): HL1 suppliers can submit invoices electronically; HL2 invoices are matched and routed for approval without manual keying; HL3 finance can see the status of any invoice; HL4 the change is in place before the financial year-end.

After interviews with accounts payable, two workshops with approvers and a review of the payments policy, the business analyst breaks the high-level requirements down. An extract:

The supplier portal, a popular idea in the workshops, is recorded as a Won't have so it is not lost, and it is not silently added to scope.

Tip

Trace in both directions. Forward tracing shows that every requirement is built and tested. Backward tracing shows that every piece of work exists because of a requirement, which is how you spot gold-plating.

The requirements document and traceability matrix

What a requirements document contains

Predictive projects usually collect requirements in a business requirements document (BRD) or requirements specification. Keep it as short as the project allows; a requirements document nobody reads protects nobody. A typical structure:

  • Purpose, background and business need
  • Objectives and success measures
  • Scope summary, including what is out of scope
  • Stakeholders and user groups
  • Assumptions, constraints and dependencies
  • Requirements by type, each with ID, description, priority, acceptance criterion, source and owner
  • Approval and sign-off record, with version history

Our free business requirements document template includes these sections with prioritised requirements, acceptance criteria and a sign-off page, and the project scope template turns the result into a scope statement.

The requirements traceability matrix

A requirements traceability matrix (RTM) is a table that links each requirement to where it came from and where it goes: its source, business objective, design element, WBS deliverable, test cases and current status. It answers three questions at any point in the project: Is every requirement being delivered? Is every requirement being tested? What is affected if this requirement changes? A spreadsheet is enough for small projects.

Requirements in Agile and hybrid projects

Agile teams still need requirements; they just avoid detailing them all up front. Requirements live in the product backlog, usually as epics broken into user stories, owned and ordered by the product owner. Detail is added through backlog refinement shortly before the work is planned, when the team knows most about it.

AspectPredictive (plan-driven)Agile (adaptive)
When detail is definedMostly before design and buildJust in time, ahead of each iteration
Main formatRequirements document with "shall" statementsProduct backlog of user stories with acceptance criteria
ChangeFormal change control against a baselineExpected; the product owner reorders the backlog
AcceptanceTesting against the specification, then sign-offAcceptance criteria per story plus the team's Definition of Done

Acceptance criteria are specific to each story, while the Definition of Done applies quality standards to every item, so non-functional requirements such as security or accessibility checks often sit there. Many organisations run a hybrid: high-level requirements, constraints and non-functional requirements are agreed up front for governance and contracts, and functional detail emerges through the backlog. The product backlog template works well for this.

Managing requirement changes

Requirements will change as the business moves and users see early versions. Change is not the problem; uncontrolled change is. Once requirements are approved, treat them as part of the project baseline.

  1. Log the proposed change with its reason and who raised it, using a change request template.
  2. Use the traceability matrix to find every design element, deliverable and test affected.
  3. Assess the impact on scope, schedule, cost, quality and risk.
  4. Take it to whoever holds the authority to decide, often the sponsor or a change control board.
  5. If approved, update the requirements, baseline, plan and traceability matrix, and tell the team and stakeholders. If rejected, record the decision.

In Agile projects the product owner can reorder the backlog freely, but changes to agreed release goals, budgets or contracts still go through governance. Read our guide to scope creep for ways to spot and stop unapproved requirement growth.

Common project requirements mistakes

  • Solutions disguised as requirements. "We need a new CRM" is a solution. Ask what problem it solves and record that.
  • Talking only to managers. The people who do the work know the exceptions and workarounds. Observe them.
  • Forgetting non-functional and transition requirements. Performance, security, data migration and training are the usual late surprises.
  • Vague words. Fast, easy, robust and flexible cannot be tested. Replace them with numbers or observable behaviour.
  • Everything is a Must. If nothing can be dropped, nothing is prioritised. Use MoSCoW with the cancellation test.
  • No owner or source. When nobody can explain why a requirement exists, it cannot be challenged or clarified.
  • No change control. Requirements added in corridors and emails quietly grow the scope.

Frequently asked questions

What are project requirements?

Project requirements are the conditions and capabilities a project's result must meet to satisfy the business need and stakeholders, such as features, performance levels, rules and deadlines.

What are the main types of project requirements?

Business, stakeholder, solution (split into functional and non-functional), transition and project requirements. Technical requirements are often listed separately.

What is an example of a project requirement?

"Invoices that match a purchase order within 2% are approved automatically" is a functional requirement. "Search results appear within two seconds for 95% of searches" is non-functional. "Train 40 users before go-live" is a transition requirement.

What are high-level project requirements?

Broad statements of what the result must achieve, captured at initiation in the business case or project charter. They are short, written for sponsors, and later broken down into detailed functional, non-functional and technical requirements.

What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?

Functional requirements describe what the solution does, such as approving an invoice. Non-functional requirements describe how well it does it, such as speed, availability, security and usability.

How do you gather project requirements?

Confirm the business need, identify stakeholders, plan your approach, then elicit requirements through interviews, workshops, observation, document analysis and prototypes. Analyse, document, prioritise and validate them, then baseline and trace them.

Who is responsible for project requirements?

The project manager is accountable for the requirements process. Business analysts or product owners usually elicit and document requirements, the sponsor approves them, and users, experts and IT provide the content.

What is a requirements traceability matrix?

A table linking each requirement to its source, design, WBS deliverable, test cases and status. It shows every requirement is delivered and tested.

What is MoSCoW prioritisation?

A method from DSDM that sorts requirements into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time, so the essential requirements are protected if time or budget runs short.

Are project requirements the same as project scope?

No. Requirements say what the result must do. Scope defines the work and deliverables needed to meet those requirements, including what is out of scope. Requirements feed the scope statement.

How are project requirements handled in Agile?

As epics and user stories in a product backlog owned by the product owner, refined just before the work is planned, each with acceptance criteria. Common quality standards sit in the Definition of Done.

Why do project requirements fail?

Common causes are missing stakeholders, vague wording, solutions recorded instead of needs, forgotten non-functional or transition requirements, weak prioritisation, and changes that bypass change control.
