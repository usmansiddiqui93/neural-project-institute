Project Risk Management: Process, Steps and Examples

Project risk management is the ongoing process of planning for, identifying, analysing, responding to and monitoring uncertain events that could affect a project's objectives, for better or worse. This guide walks you through the process step by step, gives you scoring scales you can copy, shows how to size a contingency reserve with expected monetary value, and follows one worked example from first workshop to closure.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 30 September 2026
  • Last updated 25 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • Project risk management is a continuous loop, not a one-off workshop: plan, identify, analyse, respond, implement and monitor.
  • Define probability and impact scales in real units (days, pounds, % of budget) before anyone scores a risk.
  • Every significant risk needs a named owner, a response, a trigger and a review date, or it is just a worry on a list.
  • Expected monetary value turns a ranked list into a defensible contingency reserve.
  • Manage opportunities as deliberately as threats; they rarely appear by accident.
  • Scale the method to the project: a small team needs a short register and a monthly review, not a 40-page plan.

What is project risk management?

Project risk management is the systematic process of identifying, analysing, responding to and monitoring project risks throughout the life of a project. Its aim is not to remove all uncertainty, which is impossible, but to make uncertainty visible early so the team can decide deliberately what to do about it while options are still cheap.

A project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it happens, would have a positive or negative effect on at least one objective such as scope, schedule, cost or quality. PMI uses this definition in the PMBOK Guide. Risk management deals with three related things:

  • Threats: risks with a negative effect, such as a key supplier delivering three weeks late.
  • Opportunities: risks with a positive effect, such as a new test tool halving regression testing time.
  • Issues: risks (or surprises) that have already happened and now need resolving. They move out of the risk register and into the issue log.

In the eighth edition of the PMBOK Guide, published by PMI in November 2025, Risk is one of seven performance domains, alongside Governance, Scope, Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders and Resources. The guide describes its risk processes as a continuous loop that the team adapts to the project rather than a fixed sequence.

Why project risk management matters

Most project problems were visible as risks before they became issues. Risk management is the habit of saying them out loud, writing them down and acting on them.

  • Cheaper responses. A risk spotted in planning can often be avoided by changing the plan. The same risk discovered during delivery usually costs rework, overtime or a delay.
  • Realistic estimates and reserves. Analysing risks tells you how much contingency time and money the plan really needs, instead of adding a flat 10% and hoping.
  • Better decisions by sponsors. A ranked risk list shows decision-makers what they are accepting when they approve a scope, date or budget, and reporting top risks in every status report means they hear about trouble early.
  • Captured upside. Looking for opportunities finds savings and shortcuts that teams focused only on threats miss.

The project risk management process in 7 steps

The steps below follow the risk processes in the PMBOK Guide. On a real project they overlap and repeat: you will identify new risks in month five and re-analyse old ones whenever something changes.

Step 1: Plan risk management

Decide how risk will be handled on this project before you start listing risks. Record the answers in the risk section of the project management plan, or in a separate risk management plan on larger projects.

  • Roles: who facilitates, who owns risks, who approves the use of contingency.
  • Scales and thresholds: what each score means in days and money, and which scores go to the sponsor.
  • Rhythm and tools: how often the register is reviewed, where risks are reported and where they are recorded.

Step 2: Identify risks

Find as many relevant risks as you reasonably can, from as many viewpoints as possible. Good sources are the project charter, assumptions and constraints, the work breakdown structure, lessons learned from similar projects and specialists' expert judgment.

Write each risk in cause, event and effect form: Because of [a fact that is true now], there is a risk that [an uncertain event], which would lead to [an effect on an objective]. This stops vague entries such as 'resourcing' and makes the next steps much easier.

Step 3: Perform qualitative risk analysis

Score each risk for probability and impact using your agreed scales, multiply them and rank the results in a probability-impact matrix. Qualitative analysis is quick and works on every project. It tells you where to spend your attention first.

Also note proximity: a medium risk that could happen next week often needs action before a high risk that cannot happen until the final quarter.

Step 4: Perform quantitative risk analysis (where justified)

For larger or high-stakes projects, put numbers on the combined effect of the top risks. Common techniques are expected monetary value, decision trees, sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo simulation. Small projects can usually rely on expected monetary value alone.

Step 5: Plan risk responses

For each risk above your threshold, choose a strategy (see the response table below), agree specific actions, assign an owner, set a trigger and decide what the contingency plan is if the response does not work. Add the cost and effort of responses to the plan and budget; a response with no time or money behind it will not happen.

Step 6: Implement risk responses

Carry out the agreed actions. This is where many projects fail: the register shows a sensible mitigation, yet nobody was given time to do it. Put response actions into the schedule as tasks with owners and dates.

Step 7: Monitor risks

Review the register on a fixed rhythm and whenever something significant changes. At each review, re-score open risks, check triggers, confirm actions are on track, close risks that can no longer happen, add new ones and move any that have occurred to the issue log. Report the top risks and the remaining contingency to the sponsor.

Tip

A quick test of whether your process is working: pick any high risk in the register and ask its owner what they did about it in the last two weeks. If the answer is 'nothing', the register is a record, not a management tool.

How to score risks: probability and impact scales

Scores only mean something if everyone uses the same definitions. Agree scales like these in step 1, adjusted to the size of your project. The examples below are for a project with a budget of £400,000 and a duration of nine months.

Expected monetary value and contingency reserve

Expected monetary value (EMV) is the probability of a risk multiplied by its financial impact. Threats have negative impacts and opportunities positive ones, but it is simpler to keep threats as costs and opportunities as savings, as below. Adding up the EMVs of your identified risks gives a reasoned starting point for the contingency reserve.

RiskProbabilityImpact if it occursEMV
R1 Fit-out finishes late, needing extra time in the old building40%£30,000 cost£12,000
R2 Network cabling fails certification and must be redone20%£15,000 cost£3,000
R3 Lease signature delayed by the landlord's lawyers25%£20,000 cost£5,000
O1 Existing furniture can be reused instead of bought50%£10,000 saving-£5,000
Total£15,000

On this basis the project would hold a contingency reserve of about £15,000. You will not spend exactly that: if R1 happens it costs £30,000, and if not, nothing. EMV works because across a set of risks some occur and some do not. On large projects, use Monte Carlo simulation, which shows the range of outcomes and lets the sponsor choose a confidence level.

  • Use EMV to compare responses: a £4,000 mitigation that cuts R1's probability from 40% to 15% reduces its EMV from £12,000 to £4,500, a saving of £7,500 for a £4,000 spend.
  • Keep contingency reserve inside the cost baseline and management reserve outside it, as described in our project budget guide.
  • Release contingency as risks close, and report the remaining balance with the top risks.

Risk response strategies for threats and opportunities

PMI describes five strategies for threats and five for opportunities. The table summarises them with examples.

Threat strategyWhat it meansExample
AvoidChange the plan so the threat cannot happen or cannot affect the objectiveDrop an unproven component and use a tested one
MitigateReduce the probability, the impact or bothRun a full-volume rehearsal before a data migration
TransferMove the financial impact or ownership to a third partyInsurance, a fixed-price contract or a performance bond
AcceptTake no action now; actively accept by holding a reserve, or passively by doing nothingAccept a small risk of rain delaying an outdoor survey
EscalateRefer the risk to someone with the authority to act, when it is outside the project's scopeA company-wide hiring freeze that threatens several projects
Opportunity strategyWhat it meansExample
ExploitAct to make sure the opportunity happensAssign your best developer to finish a module early
EnhanceIncrease its probability or benefitAsk a supplier for early samples of a cheaper component
SharePartner with a third party better able to capture itShare a test environment with another project and split the cost
AcceptTake advantage if it happens, without acting nowUse a price fall if one occurs
EscalateRefer it to a higher level when the benefit lies outside the projectA tool that would help the whole programme

How to choose a response

  1. If the risk is outside the project's authority, escalate it and record who accepted it.
  2. Ask whether the plan can change to avoid it cheaply; avoidance is often best during planning.
  3. Otherwise compare mitigation and transfer options: does the response cost less than the exposure it removes?
  4. Accept what is left, with a contingency plan and trigger for high risks, and log any secondary risks.

Worked example: risk management on an office relocation

A professional services firm is moving 120 staff to a new office. The project has a £400,000 budget, a nine-month schedule and a fixed move weekend agreed with the landlord of the old building. Here is how the team applies the process.

Plan and identify

In week two the project manager runs a 90-minute workshop with facilities, IT, HR, finance and the fit-out contractor, using the scales above. Four of the 22 risks identified are shown here.

IDRisk statementPIScoreOwner
R1Because the fit-out contractor is also working on two other sites, there is a risk that fit-out finishes 2 to 4 weeks late, which would force us to extend the old lease at about £30,0003412 HighFacilities manager
R2Because the building's cabling is new and untested, there is a risk that it fails certification, which would cost about £15,000 and a week to redo236 MediumIT lead
R3Because the landlord's legal team is short-staffed, there is a risk that the lease is signed three weeks late, delaying the start of fit-out248 MediumProperty director
O1Because most desks are less than three years old, there is an opportunity to reuse them and save about £10,000326 MediumProject manager

Plan responses

  • R1, mitigate and transfer: add weekly site inspections, include a 50% completion milestone in the contract with liquidated damages for late handover, and agree in advance a two-week extension option on the old lease. Trigger: the 50% milestone is missed by more than five working days.
  • R2, transfer and mitigate: the cabling contract requires the installer to fix failed runs at its own cost, and the first floor is tested a month early so problems surface while there is time.
  • R3, escalate: the landlord relationship sits with the property director, who agrees to chase weekly; the project records the risk and its owner but does not try to manage it directly.
  • O1, enhance: run a furniture condition survey in month two, not month seven, so reuse can be designed into the floor plan.

The responses cost about £4,000 in extra inspections and earlier testing. The team adds them to the schedule as tasks and to the budget, and sets the contingency reserve at £15,000 using the EMV table above.

Monitor and close

ReviewWhat changedRegister update
Month 3Lease signed on time; furniture survey confirms 80% of desks reusableR3 closed. O1 now likely (P4), saving confirmed into the budget
Month 5Contractor meets the 50% milestone two days earlyR1 probability reduced to 2; score 8 (residual risk). Contingency for R1 partly released
Month 7First-floor cabling test finds four failed runs; installer fixes them at no costR2 closed. A new risk added: move weekend clashes with month-end close (P3, I3)
Month 9Move completes on the planned weekendRemaining risks closed; lessons recorded

The point is the rhythm: specific risks, shared scales, owners, funded responses and monthly reviews.

How to run a risk identification workshop

Workshops bring different viewpoints together. A good one takes 60 to 120 minutes.

  1. Invite the right people. Include the core team, a sponsor representative, key suppliers and someone who has run a similar project. Six to ten people is a workable group.
  2. Set the scene (10 minutes). Restate the objectives, scope, key dates and the scoring scales, so everyone assesses against the same targets.
  3. Generate risks silently first (15 minutes). Ask each person to write risks on sticky notes or a shared board before discussion. This stops the loudest voice setting the agenda.
  4. Walk the plan (30 minutes). Go through the WBS, the schedule's key dependencies and the list of assumptions, asking 'what could go wrong or better here?'
  5. Run a pre-mortem (15 minutes). Imagine it is the end of the project and it has failed, or succeeded beyond expectations. Ask what happened.
  6. Group and rewrite (15 minutes). Merge duplicates and rewrite each risk in cause, event and effect form.
  7. Score quickly and assign owners (15 minutes). Agree rough scores and a provisional owner; detailed response planning can follow afterwards.

Prompt categories help coverage: schedule, cost, scope, technical, people, suppliers, regulation and stakeholders. Ask explicitly about opportunities, or you will get none.

Risk appetite, tolerance and thresholds

These three terms describe how much risk the organisation is prepared to take. They decide which risks the project manager can accept and which must go to the sponsor.

TermLevelExample
Risk appetiteStrategic: the broad attitude to risk'We will take moderate technical risk to be first to market, but we have very low appetite for safety or data protection risk.'
Risk toleranceThe acceptable variation around an objective'The launch may move by up to two weeks; the budget may vary by 5%.'
Risk thresholdThe measurable trigger for escalation'Any single risk with a cost impact over £20,000 goes to the steering group.'

Ask the sponsor to state these in the planning step; otherwise the project manager has to guess what is acceptable. Clear thresholds are part of good project governance.

Scaling risk management to your project

The right amount of process depends on size, complexity and how much is at stake. These are our suggestions, not rules from any standard.

Project typeWhat you needReview rhythm
Small (a few people, under three months)A one-page register with 5 to 15 risks, a simple 1 to 5 matrix and named ownersEvery fortnight or at each status meeting
Medium (one team, several months)A risk section in the project plan, defined scales, a register, EMV for the top risks and a contingency reserveWeekly for high risks; full review monthly
Large or high-stakesA separate risk management plan, a risk lead, quantitative analysis, management reserve and steering group reportingWeekly risk meeting; quarterly deep review
Agile teamsRisks raised in refinement, planning and retrospectives; spikes to reduce technical uncertainty; risk items in the backlogEvery sprint

Short increments and early feedback reduce many risks, but risks outside the team, such as suppliers, funding or regulation, still need an owner.

Common project risk management mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Treating risk as a one-off workshopThe register goes stale within weeksPut a risk review on every project meeting agenda
Vague risks like 'budget' or 'resources'Nobody can score or act on themUse cause, event and effect statements
Scores without defined scalesTwo people score the same risk differentlyDefine scales in days and money before scoring
Responses with no owner, date or budgetNothing happens until the risk becomes an issueSchedule response actions as tasks
Confusing risks and issuesCurrent problems crowd out future onesMove occurred risks to the issue log

Frequently asked questions

What are the steps of project risk management?

The usual steps are: plan risk management, identify risks, perform qualitative risk analysis, perform quantitative risk analysis where justified, plan risk responses, implement responses and monitor risks. They form a loop that repeats throughout the project rather than a one-time sequence.

What are the 5 steps of risk management?

A common five-step version is identify, assess, plan, implement and communicate, which is how PRINCE2 describes its risk management procedure. Other sources use identify, analyse, evaluate, treat and monitor. They cover the same ground as PMI's longer list.

What is a project risk management plan?

It describes how risk will be managed on a specific project: roles, scoring scales, thresholds, risk appetite, review frequency, reporting and tools. On small projects it can be a page in the project management plan; large projects often have a separate document.

What is the difference between a risk and an issue?

A risk is uncertain and in the future; an issue has already happened and needs action now. When a risk occurs it becomes an issue and moves from the risk register to the issue log.

Who is responsible for project risk management?

The project manager leads the process and keeps the register up to date. Each risk has an owner who monitors it and makes sure the response is carried out. The sponsor sets risk appetite, approves major responses and owns risks escalated beyond the project.

How do you calculate a risk score?

Multiply the probability score by the impact score, usually on 1 to 5 scales, to get a number from 1 to 25. A risk that is likely (4) with a high impact (4) scores 16. Compare the score with agreed bands to decide the action needed.

What are the 4 Ts of risk management?

The 4 Ts are tolerate, treat, transfer and terminate, a shorthand used in some UK organisations. They map to PMI's accept, mitigate, transfer and avoid. Some versions add a fifth T, take (the opportunity).

What is the difference between risk appetite and risk tolerance?

Risk appetite is the broad amount of risk an organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its goals. Risk tolerance is the acceptable variation around a specific objective, such as a budget that may vary by no more than 5%.

What is the difference between qualitative and quantitative risk analysis?

Qualitative analysis ranks risks on simple scales to set priorities. Quantitative analysis puts numbers on the combined effect of risks on cost or schedule, for example with expected monetary value or Monte Carlo simulation.

Do Agile projects need risk management?

Yes. Short iterations and frequent feedback reduce many delivery risks, but external risks such as suppliers, funding and regulation still need owners. Many Agile teams discuss risks in planning and retrospectives and use spikes to reduce technical uncertainty.

What software can I use for project risk management?

A spreadsheet such as our risk register template works for most projects. Project management tools add custom risk fields, links to tasks and dashboards; compare them on our project management tools page.
