Statement of Work (SOW): What to Include, With Example

A statement of work (SOW) is a document that describes the work a supplier will perform for a buyer: what will be delivered, by when, to what standard, how it will be accepted and how it will be paid for. It is usually signed alongside, or under, a contract or master service agreement. This guide explains what to include, which type of SOW to use, how to write one step by step, and walks through a complete example.

  • Last updated 25 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • An SOW turns a commercial agreement into a precise description of work, deliverables, timing, acceptance and payment.
  • The most important parts are usually the scope boundaries (including exclusions), testable acceptance criteria and the buyer's own responsibilities.
  • Choose the SOW type to match how well you can define the work: design/detail, level of effort (time and materials) or performance-based.
  • Link payments to accepted deliverables or milestones, and make sure the numbers in every table reconcile.
  • Once signed, the SOW is a baseline: changes go through a written change request that states the cost and schedule impact.
  • For Agile work, fix the budget, time box and ways of working, and let the backlog flex within them.

What is a statement of work?

A statement of work (SOW) is a written description of the work a supplier agrees to perform for a buyer. It defines the purpose, scope, deliverables, schedule, standards, acceptance criteria, responsibilities and payment terms for a specific piece of work. When signed, or incorporated into a contract, it becomes the agreed baseline both parties deliver and measure against.

In simple terms, the contract or master service agreement sets the legal rules of the relationship, and the SOW says exactly what is being bought this time. One supplier relationship can have many SOWs, one for each project or phase.

SOWs are used wherever one party does work for another:

  • Outsourced projects: agencies, consultancies, IT suppliers, construction subcontractors and freelancers.
  • Professional services: implementation, training, audits, research and managed services start-up work.
  • Public-sector procurement: governments describe the work they are buying in an SOW or one of its performance-based variants.
  • Internal services: some organisations use an internal SOW when one department charges another for work.

In project management, the SOW sits inside project procurement management. PMI's process-based PMBOK Guide editions describe a *procurement statement of work* that the buyer prepares while planning procurement, so that potential suppliers can decide whether they can deliver and price the work.

Why a statement of work matters

Most supplier disputes are not about bad faith. They come from two parties reading the same vague sentence in different ways. A good SOW removes that ambiguity before money changes hands. It gives you:

  • A shared definition of done: both parties know what will be delivered and how it will be judged.
  • A scope baseline: anything outside the SOW is a change, which protects the buyer from surprise costs and the supplier from unpaid extra work; see scope creep.
  • A link between money and results: payments are tied to accepted deliverables or milestones, not just elapsed time.
  • Clear responsibilities: what the supplier does, what the buyer must provide, and what happens if either side is late.
  • A basis for closure: at the end you can check every deliverable against the SOW and formally close the work; see project closure.
Tip

Write the SOW for a reader who was not in the room. If a new project manager on either side could pick it up in six months and understand exactly what was agreed, it is specific enough.

What to include in a statement of work

There is no single mandatory format, but most good SOWs cover the same ground. The table lists the sections, what to write in each, and the mistake to avoid.

SectionWhat to writeCommon mistake
1. Background and purposeWhy the work is needed and the business outcome it supportsSkipping it, so the supplier cannot make sensible judgement calls
2. ObjectivesMeasurable results the work should achieveObjectives that cannot be measured, such as 'improve the website'
3. Scope of workThe activities the supplier will perform and the boundaries of the workDescribing activities but not limits (volumes, locations, systems)
4. Exclusions (out of scope)Work that is explicitly not includedLeaving it out, which invites assumptions
5. DeliverablesEach output, its format and quantity, with an IDMixing deliverables with activities ('attend meetings' is not a deliverable)
6. Acceptance criteria and processHow each deliverable is reviewed, tested and signed off; review windows; rework cyclesSubjective criteria such as 'to the client's satisfaction'
7. Schedule and milestonesStart and end dates, milestone dates, period of performanceDates that ignore the buyer's own review time
8. Location, tools and resourcesWhere work happens, who provides equipment, systems access and licencesAssuming access to buildings or systems that takes weeks to arrange
9. Standards and requirementsQuality, security, accessibility, regulatory and brand standards that applyReferring to 'industry standards' without naming them
10. Roles and responsibilitiesKey personnel, buyer responsibilities, decision makers and escalationListing only the supplier's duties
11. Reporting and governanceStatus reports, meetings, risk and issue reportingNo agreed reporting rhythm, so problems surface late
12. Pricing and payment termsPricing model, milestone payments, invoicing, expensesPayment tables that do not add up to the total price
13. Assumptions and dependenciesWhat both parties are assuming, and what the work depends onHidden assumptions that later become disputes
14. Change controlHow changes are requested, assessed, approved and pricedNo process, so changes are agreed verbally
15. SignaturesAuthorised signatories for both parties and the dateSigned by someone without authority to commit budget

Sections that are often missed

Competitor guides and generic templates tend to stop at scope, timeline and cost. In practice, the clauses below cause a large share of real-world disputes, so check that your SOW covers them:

  • Buyer responsibilities: content, data, approvals, subject-matter experts and access the buyer must provide, with dates. If the buyer is late, the schedule should move.
  • Key personnel: named or role-based people the supplier commits, and whether they can be replaced without consent.
  • Handover and intellectual property: what the buyer receives at the end (source files, documentation, credentials, training). Ownership terms usually sit in the main contract, but the SOW should list the handover items.
  • Order of precedence: which document wins if the SOW, the contract and any attachments conflict. This is usually stated in the master agreement; check it rather than assume it.
  • Warranty or hypercare period: how long defects are fixed at no extra cost after acceptance.

Types of statement of work

SOWs are commonly grouped into three types, depending on how much the buyer specifies about the method. Some sources list more (functional, hybrid), but these are variations on the same three ideas.

TypeWhat it specifiesTypical pricingBest whenRisk mainly sits with
Design or detail SOWExactly how the work must be done: methods, materials, specificationsFixed price or unit ratesThe buyer knows precisely what it wants and how, e.g. construction to drawingsBuyer (if the design is wrong, the supplier still delivered what was specified)
Level of effort / time and materials SOWThe time, people or materials to be provided, and the type of workDay or hourly rates, often with a capScope is uncertain, or the buyer needs skilled capacity, e.g. support or discovery workBuyer (cost grows with effort unless capped)
Performance-based SOWThe results and performance standards required; the supplier chooses the methodFixed price or outcome-based, sometimes with incentivesThe outcome is clear and measurable but suppliers may know better methodsSupplier (must achieve the outcome)

Design or detail SOW

A design SOW describes the method and specification in detail. It suits work where the buyer has the expertise and the design is already fixed, such as building to architect's drawings or manufacturing to a technical specification. The supplier's job is to follow it; acceptance checks conformance to the specification.

Level of effort and time-and-materials SOW

A level of effort SOW buys a quantity of time or service, such as 'two senior developers for 12 weeks' or 'up to 40 days of consultancy'. It is flexible and quick to agree, which makes it common for discovery, support and staff augmentation. Protect the buyer with a not-to-exceed cap, regular timesheets and a clear description of the type of work expected.

Performance-based SOW, PWS and SOO

A performance-based SOW states what must be achieved and how performance will be measured, then lets the supplier decide how. US federal procurement promotes this approach through two related documents: a performance work statement (PWS), which describes required results with measurable performance standards, and a statement of objectives (SOO), a shorter statement of high-level goals from which bidders propose their own PWS or SOW. Performance standards in these documents often look like service levels; see service level agreements.

How to choose the right type

  • If you can draw it or specify it exactly, and you want it done your way, use a design SOW.
  • If you cannot yet define the output but know the skills and time you need, use a level of effort SOW with a cap and a review point.
  • If you can measure the outcome but want suppliers to propose the method, use a performance-based SOW.
  • If the work has a defined phase followed by an uncertain one, split it: a fixed-price SOW for discovery, then a second SOW once the scope is known.

Statement of work vs related documents

The SOW is one of several documents that describe a project. The differences matter, because each one is owned, approved and changed differently.

DocumentPurposeWho usually writes itLegally binding?
Statement of workDefines the work, deliverables, schedule, acceptance and payment for one engagementBuyer, supplier or bothUsually, once signed or incorporated into a contract
Master service agreement (MSA)Sets the general legal terms for a relationship: liability, confidentiality, IP, terminationBoth parties' legal teamsYes
Project scope statementDescribes the product and project scope, exclusions, constraints and assumptionsProject managerNo, it is an internal planning document
Project charterFormally authorises a project and the project manager internallySponsor with the project managerNo
RFP or RFQInvites suppliers to propose or quote, often with a draft SOW attachedBuyerNo
ProposalThe supplier's offer: approach, team and priceSupplierNot on its own; it may be incorporated into the contract
Service level agreement (SLA)Defines ongoing service performance standards and remediesBoth partiesUsually, as part of a service contract

SOW vs scope of work

The scope of work is one section of the SOW: the description of the activities and boundaries. The statement of work is the whole document, which adds deliverables, acceptance, schedule, responsibilities and payment. On an internal project, your project scope statement plays a similar role to the scope section, without the commercial terms.

MSA vs SOW

An MSA is signed once and governs every piece of work between two parties. Each new engagement then gets its own SOW, which refers back to the MSA for legal terms. This saves renegotiating liability or confidentiality every time. You can have an SOW without an MSA, but then the SOW (or a short contract around it) must carry the essential legal terms itself. Always take legal advice on the contractual terms; this guide covers the project-management content of the SOW.

How to write a statement of work step by step

The steps below work whether you are the buyer drafting an SOW for tender or the supplier drafting one for a client to sign. Each step names the planning artefact that feeds it.

  1. Confirm the purpose and objectives. Write two or three sentences on why the work is needed and how success will be measured. Use your business case or project objectives.
  2. Gather requirements. List what the output must do and the standards it must meet. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves; see project requirements.
  3. Break the work down. Build a work breakdown structure for the supplier's part of the work. Each lowest-level element should map to a deliverable or work package in the SOW.
  4. Define each deliverable. Give every deliverable an ID, description, format, quantity and owner. Keep activities and deliverables separate.
  5. Write acceptance criteria for every deliverable. State the test, who performs it, the review window and how many rework cycles are included.
  6. Draw the scope boundary. List exclusions, volumes and limits (for example 'up to 300 pages', 'two rounds of revisions', 'UK sites only').
  7. Set the schedule and milestones. Add start and end dates, milestone dates and the buyer's review periods. Check dependencies on the buyer; see project milestones.
  8. Agree roles, reporting and responsibilities. Name decision makers, the supplier's key personnel and the buyer's obligations. A RACI matrix attached to the SOW removes a lot of doubt.
  9. Price the work and set payment terms. Choose the pricing model, base it on a proper cost estimate, link payments to accepted milestones and check that every figure reconciles to the total.
  10. Record assumptions, risks and change control. Write down assumptions and dependencies, note key risks in your risk register and describe how changes will be handled.
  11. Review, test and sign. Ask someone from each side who did not draft it to read it and list every sentence they could interpret two ways. Fix those, take legal review, then have authorised signatories sign.
Tip

Draft the acceptance criteria before you price the work. Suppliers price risk, and vague acceptance criteria are one of the biggest risks they face. Clear criteria usually mean sharper, more comparable quotes.

Statement of work example

The example below is a condensed, fictional SOW for a website redesign. The company names are invented. It shows the level of detail that prevents arguments later; a real SOW would add the legal references to the MSA.

1. Purpose and objectives

Northfield Housing Association (the buyer) is replacing its 10-year-old website. The new site must let tenants report repairs and pay rent online, meet the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 at level AA, and cut repair-related phone calls. Brightline Digital Ltd (the supplier) will design, build and launch the site.

  • Objective 1: launch the new site by 30 June.
  • Objective 2: all page templates pass an independent WCAG 2.2 AA audit with no critical issues.
  • Objective 3: online repair reporting live at launch, integrated with the buyer's housing management system.

2. Scope and exclusions

In scopeOut of scope
Discovery workshops (up to 4) and sitemapWriting new content (the buyer provides all copy)
Design of up to 8 page templates, with 2 rounds of revisions eachPhotography and video production
Build on the buyer's chosen content management systemChanges to the housing management system itself
Migration of up to 300 existing pagesMigration of pages beyond 300 (available as a change)
Repair-reporting form integrated via the existing APIHosting costs and domain renewals
Training for up to 10 staff editors (2 sessions)Ongoing support after the warranty period (separate SOW)

3. Deliverables and acceptance criteria

IDDeliverableAcceptance criteria
D1Discovery report and sitemapBuyer's project lead approves in writing within 5 working days of receipt
D2Design system and 8 page templatesAll templates approved after no more than 2 revision rounds; colour contrast meets WCAG 2.2 AA
D3Built site on stagingAll 42 user acceptance test scripts pass; no open severity 1 or 2 defects
D4Migrated content300 pages migrated; random sample of 30 pages checked with zero broken internal links
D5Live siteSite live on production domain; repair form creates a job in the housing system in 10 of 10 test submissions
D6Training and handover pack2 training sessions delivered; admin guide, credentials and source files handed over

4. Milestones and payments

The fixed price is £48,000 excluding VAT. Each payment is invoiced only after the linked deliverables are accepted.

MilestoneLinked deliverablesTarget datePaymentAmount
M1 Discovery completeD114 February15%£7,200
M2 Design approvedD228 March25%£12,000
M3 Build ready for testingD316 May30%£14,400
M4 Go-live and acceptanceD4, D5, D630 June20%£9,600
M5 End of 30-day warrantyAll defects fixed30 July10%£4,800
Total100%£48,000

5. Responsibilities, assumptions and change control

  • The buyer provides final copy for all new pages by 2 May, and API credentials for the housing system by 21 March. If either is late, affected milestone dates move by the same number of working days.
  • The buyer reviews each deliverable within 5 working days. If no response is received within 10 working days, the supplier may escalate to the buyer's sponsor.
  • The supplier provides a named project manager and lead developer, who will not be replaced without the buyer's written agreement.
  • Status reports are sent every Friday; a steering meeting is held every four weeks.
  • Changes are requested in writing using the change request form. The supplier responds within 5 working days with cost, schedule and risk impact. No change work starts until both parties sign the change.

Notice what makes this example robust: every deliverable has a testable criterion, every payment depends on accepted deliverables, the payment table adds up, and the buyer's obligations have dates and consequences. Writing acceptance criteria that work

Acceptance criteria decide whether the supplier gets paid and whether the buyer gets what it needs. The test is simple: could two reasonable people disagree about whether the criterion has been met? If yes, rewrite it.

VagueTestable
The website will be fastKey pages load within the agreed performance budget, measured with a named tool on a stated connection profile
Training to the client's satisfactionTwo 3-hour sessions delivered to up to 10 staff; at least 8 attendees complete the editing exercise unaided
Report of high qualityReport covers the 6 sections in Annex A, cites data sources and is approved by the project lead after one revision round
System fully testedAll agreed test scripts executed and passed; no open severity 1 or 2 defects; test log delivered
Migrate the contentUp to 300 listed pages migrated with formatting preserved; sample of 30 pages has no broken links

Alongside the criteria, define the acceptance procedure:

  • who reviews and who signs off;
  • the review window (for example 5 working days);
  • how defects are classified and which severities block acceptance;
  • how many rework cycles are included in the price;
  • what happens if the buyer does not respond (escalation, or in some contracts deemed acceptance, which needs careful legal drafting).

Acceptance criteria are part of quality management; see project quality management for how to plan reviews and tests.

Managing changes to a signed SOW

Once signed, the SOW is a baseline. Changes are normal, but they should never be agreed in a corridor or a chat message. Use a simple, written change control process:

  1. The requester describes the change in writing, using a change request template.
  2. The supplier assesses the impact on cost, schedule, quality and risk, and states any assumptions.
  3. Both parties review the impact; the buyer's project manager checks it against budget and priorities.
  4. Authorised people on both sides approve or reject the change.
  5. The approved change is recorded as a numbered SOW amendment, and the plan, budget and payment schedule are updated.

Worked example: a change request

In the website example, the buyer discovers 120 more pages that must be migrated. The SOW includes 300, so this is out of scope.

ItemCalculationResult
Migration effort120 pages x 0.3 hours per page36 hours
Checking and project managementFixed allowance4 hours
Total effort36 + 440 hours
Cost40 hours x £75 per hour (rate card in the MSA)£3,000
ScheduleMigration capacity of 20 hours per week; one week overlaps user testingGo-live moves by 1 week

The buyer now has a clear choice: approve £3,000 and a one-week delay, ask the supplier to migrate the extra pages after launch, or archive the pages instead. Without the 300-page limit in the original SOW, this would have become an argument rather than a decision.

Writing an SOW for Agile work

Classic SOWs assume scope can be fixed up front. With Agile delivery, the detailed scope is expected to change as the team learns. You can still write a sound SOW by fixing different things:

ApproachWhat is fixedWhat flexesGood for
Capacity-based (team for a period)Team size, roles, duration, rate, ways of workingBacklog content and orderProduct development where the buyer's product owner sets priorities
Fixed budget, flexible scopeBudget and end date; a prioritised initial backlogLower-priority items drop if neededProjects with a hard deadline and a clear minimum viable product
Phase-by-phase SOWsOne short SOW per phase or releaseScope of later phasesUncertain work where trust is still being built
Outcome-basedThe measurable outcome and a shared definition of doneMethod and backlogMature relationships with clear, measurable goals

In an Agile SOW, the acceptance criteria often refer to a shared Definition of Done and to sprint reviews, and the buyer's responsibilities include providing a product owner with authority to make decisions. State how the backlog is changed (usually by the product owner, without a formal change request) and what still needs one (budget, team size, end date).

SOW quality test: check before you sign

Run this checklist before either party signs. Each 'no' is a likely future dispute.

CheckBuyer asksSupplier asks
ScopeIs everything we need in scope, with volumes and limits?Are exclusions and limits written down?
DeliverablesDoes every deliverable have an ID and a format?Are deliverables separate from activities?
AcceptanceCan we test every criterion objectively?Is there a review window and a limit on rework cycles?
ScheduleDo the dates meet our deadline?Do the dates allow for the buyer's review time and dependencies?
ResponsibilitiesIs the supplier's team named or defined?Are the buyer's obligations dated, with consequences if late?
MoneyAre payments tied to accepted deliverables?Do the payment figures add up to the total price?
ChangeCan we change priorities without renegotiating everything?Is unapproved extra work clearly not required?
DocumentsDoes the SOW reference the right MSA or contract?Is the order of precedence clear?

This check is also a good moment to confirm stakeholders on both sides have seen the SOW; see project stakeholder management.

Common statement of work mistakes

  • Vague verbs: 'support', 'assist' and 'optimise' without a measurable output. Replace them with deliverables and quantities.
  • No exclusions: if it is not written as out of scope, someone will assume it is in.
  • Subjective acceptance: 'to the client's satisfaction' gives the supplier no target and the buyer no protection.
  • Forgetting the buyer's obligations: late content, data or approvals are a common cause of delay; dates and consequences belong in the SOW.
  • Payments not linked to acceptance: paying by calendar date rewards time spent, not results.
  • Numbers that do not reconcile: check that milestone payments, cost breakdowns and totals match.
  • Copying a template without editing it: irrelevant clauses confuse readers and can contradict the contract.
  • Duplicating or contradicting contract terms: keep legal terms in the MSA or contract and refer to them.
  • No change process: verbal changes are the start of scope creep.
  • Ignoring constraints: budget, regulatory and resource limits should shape the SOW; see project constraints.

Frequently asked questions

What is a statement of work?

A statement of work (SOW) is a document that describes the work a supplier will perform for a buyer: the purpose, scope, deliverables, schedule, standards, acceptance criteria, responsibilities and payment terms. Once signed or attached to a contract, it is the baseline both parties deliver and measure against.

What should a statement of work include?

At minimum: purpose and objectives, scope of work, exclusions, deliverables, acceptance criteria and process, schedule and milestones, location and resources, standards, roles and responsibilities (including the buyer's), reporting, pricing and payment terms, assumptions, change control and signatures.

Is a statement of work legally binding?

Often, yes. An SOW is usually signed by both parties or incorporated into a contract or master service agreement, which makes its terms enforceable. The exact position depends on the wording and the governing law, so take legal advice on important agreements.

Who writes the statement of work?

Either party can. Buyers often draft the SOW to include with a request for proposal or quote, while agencies and consultancies often draft it for the client to review. The best SOWs are reviewed and agreed by both sides before signature.

What is the difference between a statement of work and a scope of work?

The scope of work is a section within the SOW that describes the activities and boundaries of the work. The statement of work is the full document, which also covers deliverables, acceptance, schedule, responsibilities and payment.

What is the difference between an MSA and an SOW?

A master service agreement sets the general legal terms for a relationship and is signed once. Each SOW describes one specific engagement under that MSA: its scope, deliverables, schedule and price. One MSA can have many SOWs.

Can you have an SOW without an MSA?

Yes. For one-off work, an SOW can stand alone or sit within a short contract, but it then needs to include essential legal terms such as liability, confidentiality, intellectual property and termination.

What is the difference between an SOW and a project charter?

A project charter authorises a project internally and gives the project manager authority to use resources. An SOW defines work that one party will do for another, usually as part of a commercial agreement. A supplier's SOW can be an input to the buyer's charter.

What are the three types of statement of work?

The three common types are the design or detail SOW (specifies how the work is done), the level of effort or time-and-materials SOW (buys a quantity of time or service) and the performance-based SOW (specifies outcomes and lets the supplier choose the method).

What is a performance work statement?

A performance work statement (PWS) is a performance-based document, used particularly in US government procurement, that describes the required results and measurable performance standards rather than the method of doing the work.

How long should a statement of work be?

Long enough to remove ambiguity and no longer. A small freelance job may need two or three pages; a complex IT or construction engagement can run to dozens of pages with annexes for requirements, acceptance tests and pricing.

How do you change a statement of work after it is signed?

Through a written change request. The supplier assesses cost, schedule and risk impact, authorised people on both sides approve it, and the change is recorded as a numbered amendment to the SOW.

Can you use a statement of work for Agile projects?

Yes. Instead of fixing detailed scope, an Agile SOW usually fixes team capacity, duration, budget and ways of working, refers to a shared Definition of Done, and lets the product owner reprioritise the backlog within those limits.

Who signs the statement of work?

Authorised signatories from both parties: people with the authority to commit budget and resources. On the buyer side this is often the sponsor or a procurement lead; on the supplier side an account director or company officer.
