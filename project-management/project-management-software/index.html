What Is Project Management Software? Types and Uses

Project management software is an application that lets a team plan, assign, schedule, track and report on project work in one shared place, instead of across spreadsheets, email threads and meetings. This guide explains how it works, the main types, what it does well and badly, what it costs and how to set up your first project, with worked examples throughout.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 4 October 2026
  • Last updated 25 October 2026
  • 21 min read

Key takeaways

  • Project management software turns a project into structured data (tasks, owners, dates, dependencies and statuses) that everyone sees and updates in one place.
  • The same data appears in many views: lists, boards, Gantt charts, calendars, workload charts and dashboards.
  • Types range from simple task apps to team collaboration tools, full project management, work management platforms and portfolio (PPM) suites.
  • The main benefits are one source of truth, earlier warning of slippage and far less time spent chasing updates and building reports; the main costs are licences, set-up and the discipline to keep it current.
  • In our independent ranking, Wrike is the strongest all-in-one platform; monday.com, Asana and ClickUp follow, and lighter tools such as Trello and Basecamp suit simpler needs.

What is project management software?

Project management software is a digital tool, usually cloud-based, that helps teams plan projects, break them into tasks, assign owners and due dates, link dependent work, track progress and report status. It keeps the plan, the conversation and the files together, so everyone works from the same current information.

The PMBOK Guide from PMI describes a project as a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. That temporary, one-off nature is what makes projects hard to run from memory: the team, the deadlines and the risks are different every time. Software gives each project a consistent structure and a single home.

You will also see vendors describe their products as work management platforms. The difference is mostly one of scope. Project management software focuses on projects with a start, an end and a defined outcome. Work management software also covers ongoing work, such as request queues, recurring operations and approvals. Most leading products now do both.

Software is only one kind of project management tool. Techniques such as the critical path method and documents such as a risk register matter just as much. For the full picture, read our guide to the types of project management tools.

How project management software works

Under the surface, almost every project management application stores the same kinds of objects. Once you understand them, any tool becomes easier to learn, and you can compare products on a like-for-like basis.

The building blocks

Building blockWhat it isExample
Workspace or spaceThe top-level area for a team, department or clientMarketing team space
Project or folderA container for one project's work, with its own dates and ownerQ1 product webinar
TaskA single piece of work with an owner, a due date and a statusWrite landing page copy
Subtask or checklistSmaller steps inside a taskDraft, review, final edit
MilestoneA zero-duration checkpoint marking an important eventRegistration page live
DependencyA link saying one task cannot start or finish until another doesDesign cannot start until copy is approved
Custom fieldExtra data you add to tasksPriority, budget, channel, effort in hours
Status and workflowThe stages a task moves throughTo do, In progress, In review, Done
ViewA way of displaying the same tasksList, board, Gantt, calendar, workload
Dashboard and reportLive charts and summaries built from task dataOverdue tasks by owner, progress by milestone

A project in software is essentially a work breakdown structure turned into tasks, with owners, dates and dependencies attached. The software then does the bookkeeping: it recalculates dates when something moves, notifies the people affected and rolls progress up to the project and portfolio level.

One plan, many views

The most useful idea to grasp is that views are not separate documents. When a designer drags a card from In progress to In review on a Kanban board, the same task updates on the Gantt chart, the workload chart and the manager's dashboard. Team members can work in a list or board, the project manager in a Gantt or workload view and stakeholders on a dashboard, without anyone maintaining separate copies.

The daily loop

Once a project is set up, the software supports a simple repeating cycle:

  1. Plan: the project manager creates tasks, dates and dependencies, often from a template.
  2. Assign: each task gets one accountable owner; the software notifies them.
  3. Update: owners change status, log comments or time and attach files where the work happens.
  4. Alert: the tool flags overdue tasks, blocked dependencies and approaching milestones.
  5. Report: dashboards and status reports refresh automatically from the latest data.

Worked example: one project from request to close

Here is how a typical project runs through project management software. The team and project are fictional, but the steps reflect how most all-in-one platforms work.

The scenario: an eight-person marketing team is asked to run a product webinar in six weeks. It involves a landing page, three emails, social posts, slides, a speaker briefing and follow-up content.

  1. Request. The product manager fills in a request form in the tool, giving the goal, date, audience and budget. It lands in the team's intake queue, not someone's inbox.
  2. Approval and set-up. The marketing lead approves the request. One click creates a project from the team's "Webinar" template, with 28 standard tasks, owners by role and relative due dates counted back from the webinar date.
  3. Scheduling. The project manager checks the Gantt view. Dependencies are already in the template, for example slides cannot be designed until the speaker outline is agreed. The critical path shows the speaker outline is the task most likely to delay everything.
  4. Resourcing. The workload view shows the designer is booked at 130% in week three because of another campaign. The project manager moves two non-critical social graphics to a freelancer added as a guest user, and the designer drops to 95%.
  5. Execution. Team members work from the board view and their personal "My work" list.
  6. Review and approval. Slides and landing page designs go through built-in proofing: reviewers mark up the file and approve it, and the task moves on automatically.
  7. Reporting. Each Friday the stakeholder dashboard shows tasks completed, overdue items and milestone status. The status report is generated from live data.
  8. Change. In week four the webinar date moves forward by two days. The project manager shifts the milestone, dependent tasks move with it and every owner affected is notified of their new date.
  9. Close. After the webinar the team records lessons learned on the project, archives it and updates the template: two tasks are added and one is removed. The next webinar starts from a better plan.
Tip

You do not need every feature from this example on day one. Many teams start with tasks, owners, dates and a board, then add templates, dependencies, workload views and dashboards as the need appears.

Types of project management software

Our guide to the types of project management tools groups software by product category. Here we group it by how much of your work it covers, which method it supports and where it runs.

By scope: the software ladder

Think of project management software as a ladder. Each rung covers more of the organisation's work and adds more structure, but also more set-up and cost.

RungWhat it coversTypical featuresExamples from our reviews
1. Task and to-do appsPersonal or small-group task listsLists, due dates, simple boardsTrello for simple boards
2. Team collaboration toolsShared projects with messaging at the centreTo-dos, message boards, schedules, filesBasecamp, Notion
3. Project management softwarePlanning and tracking individual projectsGantt charts, dependencies, milestones, reportsSmartsheet, Microsoft Planner (paid plans)
4. Work management platformsProjects plus requests, operations and cross-team workMultiple views, automation, workload, dashboards, intake formsWrike, monday.com, Asana, ClickUp
5. Portfolio management (PPM)Many projects, programmes and resources across the organisationPortfolio dashboards, capacity planning, financials, governanceHigher tiers of Wrike, Smartsheet and similar enterprise platforms

The rungs overlap (Wrike, for example, spans three to five depending on the plan). Climb only as far as your work requires: a three-person team on rung five will spend more time administering the tool than running projects.

By delivery method

  • Predictive (waterfall) software emphasises Gantt charts, baselines, dependencies and the critical path. It suits projects with a fixed sequence, such as construction and events.
  • Agile software emphasises backlogs, sprints, story points, boards and burndown charts. Jira is the best-known example; see our guide to Scrum software tools.
  • Hybrid software supports both, so a team can plan phases on a Gantt chart and run delivery in sprints. Most work management platforms, including Wrike, monday.com and ClickUp, fall here.

By deployment and industry

Most project management software is now cloud software (SaaS) used in a browser or mobile app; desktop and self-hosted options survive for specialist needs such as very large engineering schedules or strict data-residency rules. Industry-focused products add what general platforms lack: issue tracking and releases for software teams, time, billing and profitability for agencies (often called professional services automation, or PSA), drawings and site records for construction, and proofing and request intake for creative teams. A specialist tool is worth it when your workflows cannot be modelled in a general platform without heavy customisation.

Project management software vs related software

Project management software overlaps with other systems you may already use. Knowing the boundaries helps you avoid paying twice.

SoftwareBuilt forHow it relates to project management software

Chat and messaging (Slack, Teams)Real-time conversationGood for quick questions; decisions and tasks should be captured in the project tool, often via an integration
CRMCustomer relationships and sales pipelinesA won deal often triggers a delivery project in the PM tool
ERP and finance systemsAccounting, procurement and payrollHolds the official numbers; PM software tracks project-level budgets and time
PSABillable services businessesCombines project management with time, billing and profitability
PPMPortfolio selection, funding and governanceSits above project management; many work management platforms now include PPM features on higher tiers
Tip

If your team keeps a spreadsheet next to the project tool to track the same tasks, one of them is redundant. Decide which is the source of truth and retire the other.

Benefits of project management software

The benefits come from replacing manual coordination with shared, structured data:

  • One source of truth. Everyone sees the same tasks, dates and owners, so there are no competing versions of the plan.
  • Clear ownership. Every task has a named owner and a due date.
  • Earlier warning. Overdue tasks, blocked dependencies and overloaded people show up as they happen, not at the next status meeting.
  • Less reporting effort. Dashboards and status reports build themselves from live data.
  • Better resource decisions. Workload views show who has capacity before you commit to new work.
  • Smoother collaboration with outsiders. Guest access lets clients and suppliers see and approve their part without seeing everything.
  • An audit trail. Comments, approvals and changes are recorded against each task.

Do you need project management software? A quick self-assessment

Score one point for each statement that is true for your team today:

  1. You run more than two projects at the same time.
  2. More than five people contribute to a typical project.
  3. Tasks depend on each other, and one delay regularly causes others.
  4. The same people work on several projects at once.
  5. You spend more than an hour a week chasing updates.
  6. Status reports are built by hand from several sources.
  7. Files and decisions are hard to find after a few weeks.
  8. Clients, suppliers or other departments need visibility of progress.
  9. You repeat similar projects and rebuild the plan each time.
  10. Leaders ask questions about progress that nobody can answer quickly.
ScoreWhat it suggestsNext step
0 to 2A spreadsheet or simple board is probably enoughUse a free project tracker template or a Kanban board template
3 to 5You will gain from a team toolTry the free plan of a simple, visual tool and add a timeline view when needed
6 to 10Manual coordination is costing you real timeShortlist all-in-one platforms with Gantt charts, workload views and dashboards

Templates stay useful after you move to software. A project plan template or Gantt chart template is a good way to sketch the structure before building it in a tool.

How to choose project management software

Choosing well is a project in itself. In short:

  1. Start from problems, not features. List what is going wrong today, such as missed deadlines, overloaded people or slow reporting.
  2. Decide your rung on the ladder. Match the type of software to the scope of work it needs to cover.
  3. Turn problems into must-haves. Keep the list short and check which plan tier includes each one.
  4. Trial two or three tools on real work. Use a live project for two to four weeks with the people who will use the tool daily.
  5. Compare the full cost. Include the right tier, guests, training, integrations and admin time.

How to set up your first project, step by step

Whichever tool you choose, the first project sets the pattern for everything after it. These steps work in almost any platform.

  1. Agree the outcome and the end date. Write one sentence describing what done looks like, plus the deadline and the sponsor.
  2. Break the work down. List the deliverables, then the tasks needed to produce each. Aim for tasks of roughly half a day to a few days; anything smaller belongs in a checklist.
  3. Create the project structure. Make one project (or folder) and, if it is large, group tasks by phase or deliverable.
  4. Set statuses. Keep the workflow short: To do, In progress, In review, Done. Add a Blocked status only if you will act on it.
  5. Add only the custom fields you will report on. Priority and effort are common; resist adding fields nobody will fill in.
  6. Assign one owner and a due date to every task. Shared ownership usually means no ownership.
  7. Link dependencies and add milestones. Connect tasks that genuinely depend on each other, then check the Gantt view for the critical path.
  8. Check workload. Use the workload or capacity view to spot people booked over 100% and rebalance before work starts. Our guide to capacity planning explains how.
  9. Build one dashboard. Include progress by milestone, overdue tasks and upcoming deadlines. Share it with stakeholders instead of sending reports.
  10. Agree team habits. Decide when updates happen (for example, before the weekly meeting), where questions go (comments on the task) and what gets escalated.
  11. Save it as a template. After the project closes, turn the improved plan into a template for next time.
Tip

Keep risks visible too. Some platforms have a built-in risk or issue item type; otherwise, link a risk register to the project and review it at the same meeting as the schedule.

Rolling out to a whole department needs more planning: champions, role-based training and a date when the old way stops. A training plan template helps you organise it.

Common mistakes when adopting project management software

  • Copying the old spreadsheet column for column. Software works differently. Rebuild the plan around tasks, owners, dependencies and statuses instead of recreating every column.
  • Over-customising early. Dozens of statuses, fields and automations make the tool hard to use. Start lean and add only what people ask for.
  • Running two systems in parallel. If status still lives in a spreadsheet or slide deck, people will update that and ignore the tool.
  • Tasks that are too big or too small. A task called "Website" tells nobody anything; fifty tasks for one email is noise. Size tasks so progress is visible every few days.
  • Notification overload. Default settings can bury people in alerts. Show the team how to tune notifications in the first week.
  • No archive policy. Finished projects clutter searches and dashboards. Archive them on close and keep the template.

Frequently asked questions

What is project management software?

Project management software is an application, usually cloud-based, that helps teams plan projects, break them into tasks, assign owners and due dates, link dependencies, track progress and report status in one shared place.

What is an example of project management software?

Well-known examples include Wrike, monday.com, Asana, ClickUp, Smartsheet, Jira, Microsoft Planner, Notion, Trello and Basecamp. In our independent ranking, Wrike comes first as the most complete all-in-one platform.

What are the main types of project management software?

By scope, the main types are task and to-do apps, team collaboration tools, project management software, work management platforms and portfolio management (PPM) suites. Software can also be grouped by method (Agile, predictive or hybrid), deployment (cloud, desktop or self-hosted) and industry.

What is the best project management software?

It depends on your needs. Our ranking places Wrike first for all-in-one depth, followed by monday.com for ease of use, Asana for cross-team coordination and ClickUp for value. Jira suits software teams, and Trello or Basecamp suit simpler needs.

Is there free project management software?

Yes. Wrike, monday.com, Asana, ClickUp, Jira, Notion, Trello and Basecamp all have free plans with limits on users, projects or features. Smartsheet offers a free trial instead. Free plans usually lack Gantt dependencies, dashboards and workload views.

How much does project management software cost?

Most tools charge per user per month, with paid plans for the leading tools starting at roughly $5 to $11 per user per month in our review data (checked 5 October 2026). Some, such as Basecamp, charge a flat monthly price, and enterprise plans are usually quote-based.

Can Excel be used as project management software?

Excel and other spreadsheets work for small, simple projects. They struggle when tasks depend on each other, several people edit the plan or stakeholders want live reporting. That is usually the point to move to dedicated software.

What is the easiest project management software to use?

Trello and monday.com are among the easiest to pick up, and Basecamp is simple for communication-centred projects. Feature-rich platforms such as Wrike take longer to learn but cover far more as your needs grow.

Is Jira project management software?

Yes. Jira is project management software designed mainly for software development and IT teams, with backlogs, sprints, boards and issue tracking. Other teams can use it, but general-purpose platforms are often easier for non-technical work.

Do small businesses need project management software?

Not always. If you run one or two simple projects with a few people, a spreadsheet or free board may be enough. Once you juggle several projects, share people between them or spend hours chasing updates, software usually pays for itself.

Does project management software include AI?

Most leading platforms now include AI features such as summaries, drafting, task suggestions and risk alerts. Availability and usage limits depend on the plan, so check what is included and treat AI output as a draft. Our ranking of AI project management tools compares what each platform offers.
