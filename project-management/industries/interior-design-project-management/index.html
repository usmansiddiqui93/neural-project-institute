Interior Design Project Management: Phases, FF&E, Tools

Interior design project management is how a designer or studio runs a project from the client brief through concept, design, documentation, procurement and installation to the final reveal, while controlling budget, programme, quality and client approvals. Its defining challenge is managing hundreds of individual items, from furniture and lighting to fabrics and finishes, each with its own approval, price, lead time and delivery. This guide gives you the seven phases, a step-by-step process, FF&E procurement methods, illustrative worked examples for order dates, budgets and fees, KPIs and software.

Key takeaways

  • Interior design projects are product-heavy: hundreds of FF&E items each need selection, approval, ordering, tracking and installation.
  • Work backwards from the date each item is needed on site to set a must-order-by date, and review the FF&E schedule against those dates every week.
  • Control three budgets separately: the studio fee, the FF&E budget and the building works budget.
  • Written sign-offs at the end of each phase and a priced change log protect the client's budget and the studio's fee.
  • Track hours against fee by phase; it is the quickest way to see whether a fixed-fee project is still profitable.
  • Use specialist FF&E tools for product data and purchasing, and a work management platform such as Wrike when you need a multi-project view with approvals and resourcing.

What is interior design project management?

Interior design project management is the planning, coordination and control of an interior project's scope, design approvals, FF&E procurement, budget, programme and installation, from the first client meeting to handover. It keeps the client, designers, suppliers and trades aligned so the finished space matches the approved design, budget and date.

On a small residential job the principal designer often runs the project personally. In a larger studio a project designer or dedicated interior design project manager handles day-to-day delivery, supported by an FF&E or procurement coordinator, while the principal keeps design direction and the client relationship.

Be clear about the boundary with construction. Unless appointed to do so, an interior designer does not normally manage the building contract or certify payment to a contractor; you coordinate the design, specify and procure products, and inspect the result. Write that boundary into your appointment. On larger fit-outs a contractor runs the works; see our guide to construction project management for how that side is controlled.

What makes interior design projects different

Interior projects use the same building blocks as any project, but six features change how you manage them.

  • Product-heavy scope. A single home or office can involve hundreds of furniture, fixtures and equipment (FF&E) items, each with its own supplier, price and lead time.
  • Lead times set the programme. Bespoke joinery, upholstery, made-to-measure curtains and imported lighting can take many weeks, so ordering dates drive the schedule more than design tasks do.
  • Approvals are subjective. Decisions depend on the client's taste, so you need clear sign-off of samples, boards and drawings before anything is ordered.
  • Money often flows through the studio. Many studios buy on the client's behalf, collect deposits and apply a markup, which means they carry financial and supplier risk.
  • Trades work in a tight sequence, often in an occupied home or a live workplace.
  • Regulation applies to products, not just buildings. Fire performance of upholstery and fabrics, accessibility and building rules all matter, especially on commercial and hospitality projects.

The 7 phases of an interior design project

Studios name their stages differently, but most projects follow this sequence. Close each phase with a written client sign-off before you start the next one; it is one of the most reliable ways to limit rework.

1. Discovery and briefFirst meeting, site visit, measured survey, needs, style, budget and timescaleDesign brief, measured survey, fee proposal, signed appointmentBrief and appointment agreed
2. Concept designMood boards, space planning options, initial palette and lighting ideasConcept presentation, preferred layout, outline budgetConcept and layout approved
3. Design developmentDetailed layouts, lighting, joinery, finishes and FF&E selections with pricesSample boards, draft FF&E schedule, budget estimateSelections and budget approved
4. DocumentationDrawings and specifications for contractors, joiners and suppliersDrawing package, specifications, tender or quote requestsDocumentation issued
5. ProcurementQuotes, client approvals, deposits, purchase orders, order trackingPurchase orders, delivery schedule, updated FF&E scheduleEach order approved in writing

The BIID Interior Design Project Book, published by RIBA Publishing with the British Institute of Interior Design, sets out a designer's responsibilities at each stage and reflects the 2020 Plan of Work. How to manage an interior design project step by step

This ten-step process turns the phases into actions. On commercial projects, add formal tendering and contractor management.

  1. Qualify the enquiry and agree the brief. Confirm the client's goals, rooms in scope, budget range, decision-makers and deadline. Capture it in a creative brief template or project brief template.
  2. Survey and set the scope. Measure the space, photograph existing conditions and list what stays, what goes and what is new. Note anything that needs a contractor, building control or landlord consent.
  3. Write the fee proposal and appointment. Define deliverables by phase, the number of design revisions included, how procurement works, payment terms and how extra work is charged.
  4. Build the programme backwards. Start from the handover or install date, then place lead times, trade work and client approval deadlines. A project schedule template or Gantt chart template makes the sequence visible to the client.
  5. Set the three budgets. Separate the studio fee, the FF&E budget and the works budget, and include a contingency for each spend budget.
  6. Design, present and get sign-off. Present a limited number of options, agree a decision date for every presentation and record approvals in writing.
  7. Open the FF&E schedule. Give every item a code, specification, supplier, price, lead time and must-order-by date.
  8. Procure and track. Collect quotes, get client approval and deposits, raise purchase orders and confirm delivery dates. Review the schedule weekly.
  9. Coordinate site works and installation. Share a simple weekly plan with trades, check deliveries on arrival and plan the install day room by room.
  10. Snag, hand over and review. Close snags, hand over care guides and warranties, issue the final account and hold a short lessons-learned review.
Tip

Put the must-order-by date for every long-lead item into the programme as a milestone, with the client approval deadline one week before it. Late approvals then show up as programme risks, not surprises.

Running FF&E procurement

FF&E stands for furniture, fixtures and equipment: the movable and installed items the design team specifies, orders and installs. Procurement is where much of an interior project's time and money is won or lost. Our guide to project procurement management covers the general principles.

What goes in an FF&E schedule

The FF&E schedule (sometimes called the specification schedule) is the master record of every item. Use one row per item with at least these fields:

FieldWhy it matters
Item code and roomLinks the item to drawings and the install plan (for example LR-03, living room sofa)
Description and specificationModel, dimensions, finish, fabric, colour, quantity
Supplier and quote referenceWho supplies it and on what terms
Unit cost, total cost and client priceShows cost, any markup and the price the client approved
Lead time and must-order-by dateProtects the install date
Approval status and dateProves the client signed off before you ordered
Order statusQuote, approved, deposit paid, ordered, shipped, delivered, inspected, installed
Delivery address and dateSite, receiving warehouse or supplier collection
Compliance notesFire rating, certificates, care and warranty information

A project tracker template works as a starting FF&E schedule. Many studios also show order status on a board using a Kanban board template, with one card per item moving from left to right.

Worked example: must-order-by dates

Illustrative example. A residential living and kitchen project has its install week starting Monday 1 March 2027. Kitchen joinery is needed on site earlier, on 1 February, and wallpaper and lighting by 15 February so decorators and electricians can finish. The must-order-by date is the needed-on-site date minus lead time, shipping and a buffer.

ItemNeeded on siteLead timeShippingBufferMust order by
Imported pendant lightsMon 15 Feb 202710 weeks2 weeks2 weeksMon 9 Nov 2026
Bespoke sofaMon 1 Mar 202712 weeks1 week2 weeksMon 16 Nov 2026
Kitchen joineryMon 1 Feb 20278 weeksIncluded2 weeksMon 23 Nov 2026
WallpaperMon 15 Feb 20274 weeks1 week1 weekMon 4 Jan 2027
Made-to-measure curtainsMon 1 Mar 20276 weeksIncluded1 weekMon 11 Jan 2027
Dining chairs (stock)Mon 1 Mar 20273 weeks1 week1 weekMon 25 Jan 2027

Sorted by must-order-by date, the pendant lights come first, not the sofa, even though the sofa has the longest lead time. Because the lights are needed earlier and ship from abroad, they become the critical item, and the client must approve them within days. That is the critical path method applied to products: the item with the least time to spare drives the programme.

Give bespoke and imported items a larger buffer than stock items, enough to cover inspection and, if needed, a replacement.

Procurement models

Agree in the appointment who buys what. Each model changes your risk, cash flow and workload.

ModelHow it worksStudio riskGood for
Studio buys on client's behalfStudio raises orders, collects deposits from the client, may apply a markup or pass on trade discountsHighest: supplier errors, damage and payment timingFull-service residential and hospitality
Client buys directStudio specifies; client orders and pays suppliers directlyLower financial risk, but less control over timingBudget-conscious clients, design-only appointments
Contractor supplies and installsItems sit in the building contract; contractor procuresLow procurement risk; coordination still neededFixtures, sanitaryware, built-in joinery
Owner-furnished, contractor-installed (OFCI)Client or studio supplies; contractor fitsInterface risk: who checks, stores and accepts the itemCommercial fit-outs, lighting, equipment

Whichever model you use, issue a request for quotation with the full specification, ask for lead time and delivery terms in writing, and never order before the client has approved the item and paid any deposit you require.

Receiving, inspection and storage

Inspect and photograph every delivery against the specification before signing for it, and record the date on the FF&E schedule as evidence for any supplier claim. When the site is not ready, use a receiving warehouse so items are checked, stored and delivered together, and confirm who insures them until handover.

Budget, fees and studio profitability

Most interior projects carry three budgets, and mixing them is a common cause of disputes. Our guide to building a project budget explains the general method.

BudgetWhat it coversWho controls it
Studio feeDesign time, procurement management, site visits and project managementStudio, against its own hours
FF&E budgetFurniture, lighting, soft furnishings, accessories, art, delivery and installationStudio on the client's behalf
Works budgetBuilding, joinery, electrical, plumbing, decoration and flooringContractor, monitored by the studio or client

Worked example: FF&E budget tracker

Illustrative example. The client approved an FF&E budget of £60,000, including £6,000 (10%) contingency. Midway through procurement, the tracker shows committed orders plus forecasts for items still to buy. Variance is committed plus forecast minus approved budget, so a positive figure is an overrun.

LineApproved budgetCommitted + forecastVariance
Furniture£26,000£27,400+£1,400
Lighting£9,000£8,200-£800
Window treatments£7,000£7,600+£600
Rugs and accessories£6,000£6,000£0
Delivery, storage and installation£6,000£6,900+£900
Subtotal£54,000£56,100+£2,100
Contingency£6,000£3,900 remaining£2,100 drawn
Total£60,000£60,000Within budget

The project is still within budget, but 35% of the contingency has gone before the most uncertain costs (installation and late changes) have landed. That is the moment to tell the client, agree whether to rebalance lines (for example, the lighting saving offsets part of the furniture overrun) and stop discretionary upgrades. A budget template or project cost estimate template gives you the structure.

Fee structures

The fee structure changes how you track time and scope. Rates vary widely by country and project type, so set yours from your own costs.

StructureHow it worksWhat to watch
Fixed fee by phaseA set fee for defined deliverables at each phaseScope creep and extra revisions erode margin; define what is included
Hourly or day rateClient pays for time recordedClients may resist open-ended cost; give estimates and report hours regularly
Percentage of project costFee is a percentage of the works and/or FF&E costAgree what the percentage applies to and how changes affect it
Cost-plus or markup on FF&EStudio earns a margin on products it buysBe transparent about markup and trade discounts in the appointment
HybridFor example a fixed design fee plus hourly procurement and site timeMake the boundary between fixed and variable work explicit

Worked example: hours against fee

Illustrative example. A studio agrees a fixed design fee of £18,000 and uses a target rate of £75 per hour, which gives a budget of 240 hours. It allocates the hours by phase and checks progress at the end of design development.

PhaseShare of feeHours budgetHours usedStatus
Concept design15%3644+8 hours
Design development30%7290+18 hours
Documentation20%48Not started
Procurement20%48Not started
Installation and handover15%36Not started

The studio treats each phase's share of the fee as earned when that phase is signed off. With concept and design development complete, 45% of the fee is earned (£8,100) for 134 hours used, so the effective rate is £8,100 ÷ 134 hours ≈ £60 per hour against the £75 target. The calculation assumes fee is earned only at phase sign-off; studios that bill monthly or by percentage complete will see different interim figures. The causes were two extra rounds of concept revisions and a late layout change. The studio can charge the extra revisions as additional services if the appointment allows, tighten the remaining phases, or accept the lower margin and fix its next fee proposal. Without hours tracking, nobody would notice until close-out.

Managing client approvals and changes

Slow decisions and late changes are among the commonest threats to an interior programme. Manage them with structure, not chasing.

  • Present fewer options. Two or three well-considered choices lead to faster decisions than ten.
  • Tie every presentation to a decision date that is linked to a must-order-by date, and tell the client what happens if it slips.
  • Record approvals in writing, ideally on the sample board, drawing or FF&E line itself, with the date.
  • Price every change before it is approved. Show the cost, the lead time impact and any additional design fee.
  • Keep one change log that updates the FF&E schedule, budget and programme together.

Worked example: a change log entry

Illustrative example. After approving the sofa fabric, the client asks for a performance velvet in a different colour.

FieldEntry
ChangeCR-07: living room sofa fabric changed to performance velvet
Cost impact+£850 fabric uplift; +£150 for new samples and courier
Time impactLead time +3 weeks: ordered on the original must-order-by date of 16 November 2026, the sofa now arrives on 8 March 2027, after install week; with its two-week buffer it is fitted on 22 March 2027
Fee impact2 hours sourcing and re-presentation, chargeable as an additional service under the appointment
DecisionClient approved in writing on 12 November 2026; schedule, budget and programme updated

Installation, handover and the reveal

The install is where all the threads meet, so plan it like a small project of its own.

  1. Confirm readiness. Works complete, decoration dry, site protected, access arranged.
  2. Schedule deliveries in install order, largest pieces first, room by room.
  3. Assign an install lead who checks items against the FF&E schedule and records snags.
  4. Style and photograph the space for the client and, with permission, your portfolio.
  5. Snag and hand over. Walk the space with the client, agree the snag list and dates, and hand over care guides, warranties and supplier contacts.
  6. Close out. Issue the final account, release retained deposits, archive the project file and run a short review using a lessons learned template.

Regulations and compliance to plan for

Certificates and approvals take time, so plan them from the start. This is a summary, not legal advice; check the rules where you work.

  • Furniture fire safety (UK, domestic). Upholstered furniture supplied for homes is covered by the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988. Amendments in force since 30 October 2025 removed certain baby and young children's products from scope and dropped the display-label requirement (the permanent label still applies). A wider reform, including a new test approach, was still under consultation in 2026, so check the current position before you specify.
  • Non-domestic seating (UK). Contract furniture for offices, hotels and public spaces is commonly specified to BS 7176, which sets resistance to ignition for upholstered furniture in non-domestic settings according to the hazard level of the use.
  • Construction safety. In Great Britain, the CDM Regulations 2015 apply to construction work, including domestic projects; for a domestic client, the client's duties normally pass to the contractor or principal contractor. Our construction project management guide explains the duty holders.
  • Building rules, consents and accessibility. Structural, electrical, plumbing and fire protection changes may need building control approval; listed buildings and landlords add consents, and commercial spaces must meet accessibility requirements.
  • Outside the UK. Local building, fire and accessibility codes and product flammability standards apply; specify to the rules of the project's location, not the supplier's.

Store certificates and test reports on the FF&E line for each item, so the close-out pack is ready at handover.

Software for interior design project management

Most studios combine three kinds of tool:

  • Specialist interior design software for product libraries, FF&E specifications, client approvals, purchase orders, deposits and invoicing.
  • CAD and 3D tools for drawings, layouts and visuals.
  • A general work management platform when the studio runs many projects at once, needs to plan designers' time across projects, or works on commercial fit-outs with larger teams and external partners.

Look for Gantt views, custom fields for item data, approval workflows, workload views and client sharing. Wrike is ranked first in our project management tool reviews and suits studios that need a portfolio view, structured request intake and built-in proofing and approvals for design files; read our Wrike review for plans and pricing. monday.com and Asana are also strong choices, particularly for teams that want quick setup and visual boards.

Smaller residential studios may find a specialist interior design tool covers most of their needs. Whatever you trial, run one real past project through it, including an FF&E schedule, a change and a client approval.

Frequently asked questions

What is interior design project management?

It is the planning, coordination and control of an interior project from brief and concept through documentation, procurement and installation to handover, while managing budget, programme, quality and client approvals.

What does an interior design project manager do?

They run day-to-day delivery: the programme, budgets, FF&E schedule, supplier orders, client approvals, coordination with contractors and trades, site visits, snagging and reporting to the principal designer and client.

What are the phases of an interior design project?

Discovery and brief, concept design, design development, documentation, procurement, construction and installation, and handover and reveal. Close each phase with a written client sign-off.

What does FF&E mean?

FF&E stands for furniture, fixtures and equipment: the movable and installed items, such as furniture, lighting, soft furnishings and accessories, that the design team specifies, orders and installs.

What is an FF&E schedule?

It is the master list of every item on the project, with its code, room, specification, supplier, cost, client price, lead time, must-order-by date, approval status, order status and delivery details.

How long does an interior design project take?

It depends on scope. A single room with stock furniture may take a few months; a full house or fit-out with building works and bespoke items takes much longer. Lead times for bespoke and imported items usually set the duration.

How do you calculate a must-order-by date?

Take the date the item is needed on site and subtract its lead time, shipping time and a buffer for inspection and problems. Set the client approval deadline before that date and track it weekly.

How do interior designers charge for their work?

Common structures are a fixed fee by phase, hourly or day rates, a percentage of project cost, a markup on products bought for the client, or a hybrid. Whichever you use, define what is included and how extra work is charged.

How do you handle client changes?

Log the change, show its cost, lead time and fee impact, get written approval, then update the FF&E schedule, budget and programme together. Never re-order before the change is approved.

What is the difference between Cat A and Cat B fit-out?

Cat A is a landlord's base fit-out of an office, with floors, ceilings, basic lighting and services. Cat B is the tenant's fit-out designed around its own needs, such as partitions, meeting rooms, kitchens and furniture, often led by an interior designer.

What software do interior designers use for project management?

Specialist interior design tools for FF&E and purchasing, CAD and 3D tools for drawings and visuals, and a work management platform such as Wrike for multi-project planning, approvals and resourcing.

Can you use Kanban for interior design projects?

Yes. A Kanban board with one card per FF&E item and columns such as quote, approved, ordered, shipped, delivered and installed is an easy way to see procurement status at a glance.
