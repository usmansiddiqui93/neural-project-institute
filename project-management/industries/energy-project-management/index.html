Energy Project Management: Phases, Roles, Controls and Tools

Energy project management is the planning, control and delivery of projects that produce, transmit or store energy, from wind, solar and battery storage to power stations, grid connections, pipelines and oil and gas facilities. These projects are capital-intensive, long-lived, high-hazard and heavily regulated, so success depends on disciplined front-end planning, formal investment gates, strong contracts and rigorous safety management. This guide explains the life cycle, contracting, project controls, HSE, permitting, commissioning and tools, with worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • Energy project management runs on stage-gates: each gate is an investment decision, and the final investment decision (FID) commits most of the capital.
  • Time and money spent on front-end definition (FEED or development) is the strongest protection against cost and schedule overruns in construction.
  • Permits, grid connection dates and long-lead equipment such as transformers often decide the schedule more than construction itself.
  • Safety performance is reported first on almost every energy project; measure it with rates per hours worked, not raw counts.
  • Earned value, probabilistic (P50/P90) estimates and a live risk register give owners and lenders an honest forecast.
  • Specialist scheduling, cost and HSE systems sit alongside a work management platform such as Wrike for the owner's team and development portfolio.

What is energy project management?

Energy project management is the discipline of taking an energy asset from idea to safe, reliable operation: proving the business case, securing land, permits and grid access, defining the design, contracting and building it, and commissioning it into service. It applies standard project methods with added focus on investment gates, safety, regulation and long-term asset performance.

The term covers very different project types. A 20 MW rooftop solar programme and a multi-billion offshore development share the same principles but differ hugely in scale, risk and governance.

What makes energy projects different

Large capital, long life

Assets may operate for decades, and decisions made in the first few percent of spending fix most of the lifetime cost. Owners therefore invest heavily in feasibility, design and estimating before committing to build. Business cases are usually tested with discounted cash flow measures such as net present value.

Permits and consents

Planning permission, environmental impact assessment, marine or water licences, land rights and conditions attached to consents can take longer than construction. Depending on project size and country, consent may come from a local authority or a national process.

Grid connection

Most power projects cannot earn revenue until the network operator connects them. Connection dates, reinforcement works and technical compliance tests often sit on the critical path.

High-hazard work

Electrical, lifting, working at height, confined space, marine and process hazards mean permit-to-work systems, safety cases and strict contractor management.

Financing and lenders

Many projects, particularly renewables, are project-financed. Lenders and their technical advisers review the schedule, contracts, estimates and risks before financial close and monitor progress during construction.

Supply chain, sites and weather

Transformers, turbines, switchgear and specialist vessels can have long lead times. Offshore, remote or mountainous sites add logistics and seasonal weather windows.

The energy project life cycle

Large oil, gas and power companies commonly use a stage-gate model with names such as Appraise, Select, Define, Execute and Operate. Renewable developers usually follow a development-led sequence. The two map onto each other closely.

Capital project stage-gateRenewables development sequenceMain workGate decision or milestone
AppraiseSite origination and feasibilityIdentify opportunity, screening studies, early land and grid checksIs the opportunity worth funding further?
SelectDevelopmentCompare concepts, land agreements, surveys, consent application, grid offerPreferred concept selected; consent and grid secured
Define (FEED)Pre-construction and financingFront-end engineering design, procurement strategy, tenders, financingFinal investment decision (FID) or financial close
ExecuteConstructionDetailed design, procurement, construction and installationMechanical completion
CommissionCommissioning and energisationTesting, energisation, performance testsCommercial operation date (COD) and takeover
OperateOperations and maintenanceO&M, warranty period, performance monitoringBusiness case benefits realised
DecommissionRepowering or decommissioningRemove or replace the asset and restore the siteSite restored or repowered

The early stages are often called front-end loading (FEL), usually split into FEL 1 (Appraise), FEL 2 (Select) and FEL 3 (Define). A feasibility study supports the first gate; the business case is refreshed at every gate.

Tip

Protect the front end. Rushing FEED or development to hit an investment date usually moves the problems into construction, where changes cost far more and contractors price the uncertainty.

Front-end loading, FEED and cost estimate classes

Front-end engineering design (FEED) defines the facility well enough to produce a reliable estimate and schedule, tender the main contracts and support the final investment decision. Typical FEED outputs include the design basis, layouts, single-line diagrams, equipment lists, long-lead item specifications, the execution plan, the control estimate and the risk register.

As definition improves, the estimate becomes more accurate. AACE International's estimate classification for the process industries (recommended practice 18R-97) is widely used to describe this. The ranges below are AACE's indicative figures; actual accuracy depends on the project's risk.

AACE classProject definitionTypical useIndicative accuracy rangeTypical gate
Class 50% to 2%Screening, conceptLow −20% to −50%; high +30% to +100%Appraise
Class 41% to 15%Study or feasibilityLow −15% to −30%; high +20% to +50%Select
Class 310% to 40%Budget authorisationLow −10% to −20%; high +10% to +30%FID
Class 230% to 75%Control or bidLow −5% to −15%; high +5% to +20%Execute (control baseline)
Class 165% to 100%Check estimate or bidLow −3% to −10%; high +3% to +15%Execute (lump-sum tenders, changes)

The practical lesson: an estimate prepared at Appraise is not a budget. Present early numbers as ranges, state the estimate class and move to a single control budget only at FID. Build estimates with a project cost estimate template structured by your work breakdown structure so each class refines the same lines.

Step by step: delivering a utility-scale renewables project

The sequence below reflects how a typical onshore solar, wind or battery storage project moves from site to operation. Timings vary widely by country, size and grid conditions; development commonly takes longer than construction.

  1. Originate the site. Screen land for resource (sun or wind), grid capacity nearby, environmental constraints and access. Agree options or leases with landowners.
  2. Apply for grid connection. Submit the application early; the offer sets your connection date, capacity and cost of reinforcement works.
  3. Run surveys and design for consent. Ecology, landscape, heritage, noise, flood and ground surveys feed the layout and the environmental assessment.
  4. Engage stakeholders and submit the consent application. Consult the community and statutory bodies before submission, then manage questions and conditions.
  5. Prepare the business case and financing. Model revenue (power purchase agreement, merchant or support scheme), costs and returns. Use a business case template and confirm the financing route.
  6. Develop the design and procurement strategy. Choose EPC or multi-contract, prepare specifications and issue tenders using an RFQ template. Order long-lead items such as transformers early if needed.
  7. Reach FID or financial close. Lenders' technical advisers review contracts, schedule, estimates and risks. Baseline the schedule and budget.
  8. Discharge pre-construction consent conditions. Many consents require approved plans (traffic, environment, drainage) before work starts.
  9. Construct. Civil works, foundations or piling, installation of panels, turbines or battery containers, cabling and substation works. Manage HSE, progress, quality and change weekly.
  10. Commission and connect. Test systems, energise with the grid operator, complete compliance tests and reach the commercial operation date.
  11. Hand over to operations. Close out punch lists, deliver the handover dossier and as-built drawings and start the warranty and O&M period.

Large oil and gas or thermal projects follow the same logic with far longer engineering, fabrication and commissioning phases. For the construction phase in more depth, see our guide to construction project management.

Contracting strategies: EPC, EPCM and multi-contract

The contracting strategy decides who carries design, interface and cost risk. It is chosen during Select or development and shapes the owner's team, the controls you need and the financing terms.

How to choose

  • Choose EPC when lenders want price and date certainty, the technology is mature and you have limited in-house engineering capacity. Expect a higher price for the risk transfer.
  • Choose multi-contract when you have an experienced owner's team and can manage interfaces; it can lower cost but you carry the gaps between packages.
  • Choose EPCM or reimbursable when scope is still evolving or the project is too large for any contractor to price at fixed cost.
  • Whatever you choose, write an interface register that lists every physical and contractual interface between packages, with an owner for each.

Project controls: schedule, cost and earned value

The integrated schedule

Energy schedules are built with the critical path method and usually have several levels: a level 1 milestone summary for the board and lenders, a level 2 or 3 schedule by area and discipline, and detailed contractor schedules. Permits, grid dates and long-lead equipment should appear as logic-linked activities, not just notes. A Gantt chart template is fine for an early development plan; large projects move to enterprise scheduling tools.

Worked example: earned value on a battery storage project

A battery storage project has a construction budget at completion (BAC) of £40 million over 14 months. At the end of month 8 the controls team reports the figures below. The numbers are illustrative.

Tip

SPI drifts towards 1.0 as a project nears its end even if it is late, because all planned work is eventually earned. On energy projects always check the critical path and key milestone dates alongside SPI.

P50 and P90: probabilistic estimates

Owners and lenders rarely accept a single-point estimate. A Monte Carlo simulation runs the schedule or cost model thousands of times with ranges for uncertain items and produces confidence levels.

  • For cost and schedule, P50 is the value with a 50% chance of not being exceeded and P90 the value with a 90% chance of not being exceeded. Many owners set the budget near P50 and hold contingency up to a higher confidence level.
  • For energy yield, the language is reversed: a P90 annual output is the level expected to be exceeded in 90% of years. Lenders commonly size debt on conservative yield cases such as P90.
  • Example: a wind farm's cost model gives P50 £118m and P90 £131m. The board approves a budget of £118m plus £13m of contingency held by the owner, released only through formal change control.

Health, safety and environment

On energy projects, safety is usually the first item in every report and meeting. A robust HSE system typically includes:

  • Safety management system covering hazard identification, risk assessment, method statements, training and competence.
  • Permit to work for hazardous activities such as electrical isolation, hot work, confined spaces and lifting, with clear isolation and handback procedures.
  • Safety case or equivalent where regulation requires it, for example on major hazard sites.
  • Contractor HSE management: prequalification, site inductions, supervision ratios and joint inspections.
  • Environmental controls: ecology protection, pollution prevention, waste and the conditions attached to consents.
  • Legal duties by jurisdiction. In Great Britain, for example, the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 place duties on clients, designers and contractors on construction projects, including energy construction.

Worked example: TRIR and LTIF

Safety performance is compared using rates per hours worked so that projects of different sizes can be compared. Two common measures are the total recordable incident rate (TRIR), usually calculated per 200,000 hours (roughly 100 full-time workers for a year), and the lost time injury frequency (LTIF), usually calculated per 1,000,000 hours. Check which basis your organisation and clients use.

Input or measureValueCalculation
Hours worked to date1,200,000From contractor timesheets
Recordable incidents3Incident log
Lost time injuries1Incident log
TRIR0.503 × 200,000 ÷ 1,200,000
LTIF0.831 × 1,000,000 ÷ 1,200,000

Lagging rates show what has already happened. Balance them with leading indicators such as safety observations, inspections completed, near misses reported and permit-to-work audits.

Commissioning and handover

Commissioning proves the asset works safely and as designed. Plan it from FEED, not from the end of construction.

  1. Mechanical completion. Systems are installed and checked against drawings; walkdowns produce a punch list split by priority.
  2. Pre-commissioning. Inspections and tests without energy or process fluids: cable tests, insulation resistance, loop checks.
  3. Commissioning. Systems are energised or started one by one under permit-to-work control and tested against design.
  4. Grid energisation and compliance testing. Coordinated with the network operator to show the plant meets connection requirements.
  5. Performance and reliability tests. Output, efficiency or availability is measured against contract guarantees.
  6. Commercial operation date (COD) and takeover. The owner accepts the asset; warranties and the defects period start.
  7. Handover and close-out. As-built drawings, operating manuals, spares, training records and the handover dossier go to operations; lessons are captured as part of project closure.
Tip

Track commissioning by system, not by area. A system completion dashboard (systems mechanically complete, pre-commissioned, commissioned and handed over) shows readiness far better than percentage of construction complete.

Skills and certifications

Energy project managers need a blend of commercial and contract knowledge, enough engineering understanding to challenge designs, strong HSE leadership and project controls skills. Many come from engineering, construction or project controls backgrounds; our guides to engineering project management and infrastructure project management cover adjacent sectors.

  • Project management: the PMP certification is widely recognised by owners and contractors.
  • Cost and controls: AACE International offers certifications such as the Certified Cost Professional (CCP) and Certified Estimating Professional (CEP).
  • Safety: industry or national safety qualifications and site-specific training are commonly expected, for example offshore survival and electrical safety training where relevant.
  • Sector knowledge: grid codes, consenting processes and the technology you are delivering matter as much as general methods.

Start with the method, then add sector depth: master stage-gates, estimating classes, earned value and commissioning, and the rest of the energy toolkit builds on them.

Frequently asked questions

What is energy project management?

It is planning and delivering projects that generate, transmit or store energy, from wind, solar and battery storage to power plants, grid infrastructure and oil and gas facilities, through investment gates, construction and commissioning.

What does an energy project manager do?

They lead the project through its gates: coordinating development or design, contracts, budget, schedule, HSE, permits and stakeholders, and reporting progress and forecasts to the owner, board and lenders.

What are the phases of an energy project?

Commonly Appraise, Select, Define, Execute, Commission and Operate. Renewable developers describe them as origination, development, financing, construction, commissioning and operation.

What is FEED in energy project management?

Front-end engineering design: early engineering that defines the facility well enough to produce a reliable estimate and schedule, tender the main contracts and support the investment decision.

What is FID?

The final investment decision: the gate at which the owner, and lenders for project-financed projects, commit the capital to build. It usually follows FEED or development.

What is the difference between EPC and EPCM?

Under EPC one contractor designs, procures and builds, usually for a fixed price, and carries most delivery risk. Under EPCM a firm manages the work for the owner, who holds the contracts and most of the risk.

What is commissioning?

The staged testing that proves an energy asset is installed correctly and works safely as designed, from pre-commissioning checks to energisation and performance tests, before takeover and commercial operation.

What do P50 and P90 mean?

For cost or schedule, P90 is the value with a 90% chance of not being exceeded. For energy yield, P90 is the annual output expected to be exceeded in 90% of years.

How long does a renewable energy project take?

It varies by country, size and grid conditions. Development (land, permits, grid and financing) commonly takes longer than construction, so plan development as carefully as the build.

How does renewable energy project management differ from oil and gas?

Renewables projects are usually smaller and quicker to build and are often driven by permitting, grid connection and financing. Oil and gas projects are larger, more engineering-heavy and carry subsurface uncertainty.

Which KPIs matter in energy project management?

Safety rates such as TRIR and LTIF, leading HSE indicators, CPI, schedule float, estimate at completion, consents status, commissioning progress and, once operating, availability.

Is Agile used in energy project management?

Mainly for digital, software, trading and smart-grid work. Physical construction uses stage-gates, critical path scheduling and earned value, sometimes with Lean practices on site.

What certifications help in energy project management?

The PMP is widely recognised, AACE certifications such as CCP suit cost and controls roles, and industry safety training is commonly expected.
