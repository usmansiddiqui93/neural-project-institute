Agency Project Management: Process, KPIs and Examples

Agency project management is how creative, digital, marketing and media agencies plan, deliver and control client work while protecting profit. It combines ordinary project control with commercial disciplines that in-house teams rarely need: estimating and quoting, tracking time against fees, managing scope against a contract and sharing one team across many clients. This guide walks through the process from pitch to invoice, with worked examples for estimating, capacity, budget burn and retainers.

Key takeaways

  • Agency project management adds commercial control (estimates, time tracking, margin) to ordinary delivery.
  • The statement of work's assumptions and exclusions are your main defence against scope creep.
  • Estimate from your own past hours, price at both cost and billable rates, and tie contingency to named risks.
  • Plan capacity on real available hours, not headcount multiplied by a full working week.
  • Compare budget spent with work accepted every week; the gap is your earliest warning of an overrun.
  • Match the delivery method to the contract: fixed fee, time and materials and retainers each need a different approach.

What is agency project management?

Agency project management is the planning, delivery and commercial control of client work inside an agency. It covers scoping and estimating, booking people across accounts, running delivery, managing client feedback and change, tracking time and budget against the agreed fee, and invoicing. Success means a happy client and a profitable project, not just on-time delivery.

Agencies are a type of professional services firm, so many of the same measures apply. The table shows how agency work differs from an in-house project.

AspectAgency projectIn-house project
Who paysAn external client, under a contractAn internal budget holder
What is soldTime and expertise, often as a fixed feeNothing; the organisation funds its own work
ScopeDefined by a statement of work or retainer agreementDefined by an internal brief or charter
TeamShared across several clients at onceUsually dedicated to the organisation's priorities
ChangeExtra work is a commercial event that needs pricingExtra work is a priority or capacity decision
Success measureClient satisfaction plus marginBenefits delivered within budget

For the wider services view, see professional services project management.

What makes agency project management different

  • Time is the product. Profit depends on how many hours the work actually takes compared with what the client pays.
  • Contracts define scope. A statement of work (SOW) or retainer agreement sets what is included; anything else is a change.
  • Many clients, one team. The same designers and developers serve several accounts, so resourcing is a daily negotiation.
  • The client relationship. Project managers balance delivery against keeping the client happy and the account growing.
  • Client dependencies. Content, feedback and approvals from the client often sit on the critical path.
  • Pitching. Some work starts before a contract exists, so new-business effort must be planned and capped.

The handoffs that break most projects

Two handoffs cause most trouble. The first is sales to delivery: the person who sold the work knows what was promised, but the delivery team only sees the SOW. Hold an internal handover meeting where the account lead walks through promises, risks and the client's personality. The second is delivery to invoice: work is finished but time is missing, changes are unbilled or acceptance was never confirmed. Close timesheets and confirm sign-off before the invoice goes out.

Tip

Write the assumptions into the SOW: number of concepts, rounds of amends, what the client must supply and by when. These are what you will point to when scope starts to drift.

Account manager vs project manager

The split between account management and project management is a common source of friction. As a rule of thumb, the account manager owns why and what (the relationship, the client's goals, upselling and renewals) and the project manager owns how and when (plan, budget, resources and delivery). Both should agree changes before they reach the team. A RACI matrix agreed at the start of each account avoids gaps and double handling.

Producers and traffic managers

In creative and production agencies the project manager is often called a producer, especially where the work includes shoots, video or third-party suppliers. A traffic manager schedules work through the studio across all accounts. In smaller agencies one person often does all three jobs.

Pricing models and how they shape delivery

How the work is sold decides who carries the risk of overruns, and therefore how tightly you must control scope.

Pricing modelHow it worksWho carries overrun riskDelivery approach that fits
Fixed feeOne price for defined deliverablesAgencyPhased plan, tight SOW, formal change requests
Time and materialsClient pays for hours used at agreed ratesClientAgile sprints or Kanban, with regular budget reports
RetainerMonthly fee for a set amount of hours or deliverablesShared; agency if over-servicing is absorbedKanban with a monthly cap and rollover rules
Value-basedPrice linked to the value or result for the clientAgency, with upside if results are strongClear success measures and a controlled scope

The commercial model and the delivery method must match. Selling a fixed price and then running open-ended sprints is a common way agencies lose money.

How to scope an agency project

Before you price anything, make the scope concrete enough that two people would read it the same way. A good SOW covers:

  • Deliverables with quantities, for example "five page templates" rather than "a website".
  • Acceptance criteria: what "done" means for each deliverable.
  • Assumptions: number of concepts, rounds of amends, browsers and devices supported, and what the client supplies (copy, images, access, brand guidelines) and by when.
  • Exclusions: what is not included, stated in the main document, not hidden in an appendix.
  • Client responsibilities and review times, for example "feedback within three working days".
  • Change process: how new requests are priced and approved.
  • Timeline and payment milestones.

Examples of exclusions that hold up: "Copywriting is excluded; the client will supply final copy for all pages by week 3" and "Two rounds of amends are included per design stage; further rounds are charged at the rates in schedule 2."

Agree the SOW with the people who will deliver the work, not only the person who sold it. Designers and developers spot unrealistic assumptions quickly.

How to estimate an agency project: worked example

This example estimates a five-page marketing website. All figures are illustrative. Cost rates are what each hour costs the agency (salary plus overheads); billable rates are what the client is charged.

  1. List the deliverables from the draft SOW: discovery workshop, sitemap and wireframes, visual design for five templates, copy editing, build, testing and launch.
  2. Estimate hours per role from past projects, not from the client's budget. Look up the actual hours on your last two similar websites.
  3. Add project management time. Many agencies use a percentage of delivery hours; this example uses 15%.
  4. Price at both cost and billable rates so you can see the margin.
  5. Add contingency tied to named risks, not a blanket uplift.
  6. Sense-check against a comparable past project and investigate any large difference.
RoleHoursCost rateCostBillable rateFee
Strategist12£50£600£120£1,440
UX designer20£40£800£100£2,000
Visual designer30£40£1,200£100£3,000
Copywriter (editing)15£35£525£90£1,350
Developer60£45£2,700£110£6,600
QA tester10£30£300£80£800
Project manager (15% of 147 h)22£45£990£100£2,200
Subtotal169£7,115£17,390
Contingency: extra design round (risk: new stakeholder joins reviews)8£40£320£100£800
Contingency: CRM form integration (risk: unknown API)6£45£270£110£660
Total183£7,705£18,850

The quote is £18,850 for 183 hours, with a gross margin on time of about 59% ((18,850 − 7,705) ÷ 18,850). If the comparable project took 230 hours, stop and find out why before sending the price: either this scope is genuinely smaller or the estimate is optimistic.

How to run an agency project kick-off

A good kick-off turns the signed SOW into shared expectations. Hold two meetings: an internal one first, then one with the client.

Internal kick-off

  • The account lead walks through what was sold, the client's goals, known sensitivities and any promises made in the pitch.
  • The project manager presents the hours budget per role, the draft timeline and the main risks.
  • The team challenges the estimate and assumptions before the client sees the plan.
  • Agree who may talk to the client about what, so feedback and requests reach the project manager.

Client kick-off

  • Confirm objectives, deliverables, success measures and the people who will approve work.
  • Walk through the timeline, highlighting the dates when the client must supply content or give feedback.
  • Explain the change process and how many rounds of amends are included, in plain language.
  • Agree the status update format, frequency and channel.

Send short written notes the same day. They become the reference point if expectations drift later.

Capacity planning and resourcing

Most agency schedules fail because they assume everyone has a full week to give. Start with real available hours.

Worked example: available hours and utilisation

ItemHours per week
Contracted hours37.5
Less: holiday and leave (averaged)−3.0
Less: internal meetings and training−3.5
Less: admin and email−2.0
Less: new business support−1.0
Available for client work28.0

If this designer logs 23 billable hours, billable utilisation is 23 ÷ 28 = 82% of available hours, or 23 ÷ 37.5 = 61% of contracted hours. Both definitions are used in practice, so pick one, write it down and use it consistently. Schedule client work against the 28 hours, not the 37.5.

How to book people

  1. Convert each project's estimate into hours per role per week.
  2. Book named people against named deliverables, not just roles.
  3. Hold one resource plan for the whole agency, reviewed at a short meeting early each week for this week and next.
  4. Add tentative bookings for likely wins, with a date to confirm or release them.
  5. Leave some unbooked capacity for small client requests and amends, so they do not push planned work back.

Running the work week to week

A steady weekly rhythm catches most problems while they are still small.

WhenWhatWho
MondayResourcing check for this week and next; resolve clashesProject managers and resource manager
DailyShort stand-up or board review; everyone logs time for the dayDelivery team
MidweekBudget burn against accepted work for each live projectProject manager
Thursday or FridayClient status update: progress, decisions needed, risks, budgetProject manager and account manager
FridayTimesheets closed; change log and risk log reviewedEveryone; project manager reviews

Keep one source of truth for status: each piece of work shows its stage, owner, next action and any risk to the date. A short weekly project status report with budget burn keeps the client and the account manager informed without extra meetings.

Managing change requests and scope creep

Scope creep in agencies rarely arrives as a formal request. It comes as "one quick tweak" in a review call or an extra page mentioned in an email. Use a friendly but consistent process:

  1. Log every new request, however small, in a change log.
  2. Check it against the SOW. If it is in scope, schedule it. If not, it is a change.
  3. Set a threshold. For example, anything that takes more than two hours is quoted before work starts; smaller items are logged and reviewed monthly.
  4. Price it at the original rates and state the effect on the timeline.
  5. Get written approval from the authorised client contact before starting.
  6. Update the plan, budget and invoice schedule.

A change request template makes this quick. Our guides to scope creep and change control cover the wider process.

Retainer project management

Retainers suit always-on work such as content, social, SEO and website support. They are usually run as a Kanban flow with a monthly cap on hours or deliverables. The main risk is over-servicing: doing more than the fee covers because each request seems small. Track it monthly.

Month 14038−2£94.74

In this illustrative example the agency's planned blended rate is £90 an hour, but the effective rate has fallen to £70 to £75 because 23 extra hours went unbilled. The fix is a conversation, not a complaint: show the client the trend, agree which requests to drop or move, or propose a larger retainer. Also agree rollover rules for unused hours at the start.

Worked example: a budget-burn check

The website above has a budget of 183 hours. Halfway through the schedule, 120 hours have been logged (66% of budget), but the client has accepted only the discovery output and wireframes, which you weight as 50% of the work. A simple forecast is hours used ÷ share complete: 120 ÷ 0.50 = 240 hours, which is 57 hours (31%) over budget.

What to do next: find the cause (extra design rounds, slow approvals or an underestimate), stop any unapproved work, raise change requests for out-of-scope items, and tell the account manager this week rather than at the end. This is a simplified form of earned value management.

Agency project management software

Agencies need project plans, resource scheduling, time tracking, budgets against estimates, client-facing proofing and approvals, and reporting. Tools fall into three broad groups:

  • Work management platforms for planning, resourcing, proofing and reporting across many clients.
  • Simple client collaboration tools for small agencies that mainly need organised communication with clients.
  • Agency management or PSA (professional services automation) systems for quoting, time, invoicing and financial reporting, sometimes used alongside a work management platform.

Wrike is a strong all-in-one option for agencies that want proofing and approvals, workload and resource management, time tracking and client collaboration in one place, and it ranks first in our project management software rankings. Basecamp suits small agencies that want calm client communication with flat pricing. monday.com and Asana are popular for visual workflows and cross-team task tracking, and ClickUp offers a wide feature set at lower prices.

Whatever you choose, test it with one real project: can you go from estimate to resource plan to time logged to budget report without exporting to a spreadsheet?

Frequently asked questions

What is agency project management?

It is how agencies plan, deliver and control client projects while tracking time, budget and scope against the client contract to protect profit.

What does a project manager do in an agency?

They scope and estimate work, plan timelines, book resources, manage client feedback and approvals, track budget burn, handle change requests and make sure time is logged and invoiced.

How is agency project management different from in-house project management?

Agencies sell time, so estimates, timesheets, contracts and margin matter much more. One team also serves many clients, so resourcing and prioritisation are constant.

What is the difference between an account manager and a project manager in an agency?

Account managers own the relationship, the client's goals and commercial growth. Project managers own delivery: plan, budget, resources and timelines on specific pieces of work.

What is a producer in an agency?

A producer is the agency equivalent of a project manager, a title common in creative, video and production work. They manage budgets, schedules, suppliers and the team from brief to delivery.

How do you calculate utilisation in an agency?

Divide billable hours by available hours (contracted hours minus leave and agreed non-client time) or by contracted hours. For example, 23 billable hours out of 28 available is 82%. Use one definition consistently.

What is over-servicing?

Over-servicing is work delivered beyond what the client pays for, usually on retainers and fixed fees. It shows up as write-offs and a falling effective hourly rate.

How do you handle scope creep in agency project management?

Write clear assumptions and exclusions into the SOW, log every new request, price changes before starting work and get written approval from the client.

What KPIs matter in agency project management?

Billable utilisation, budget burn against work accepted, write-offs, project margin, estimate accuracy, on-time delivery and client health.

How does retainer work fit agency project management?

Retainers suit Kanban-style flow with a monthly cap on hours or deliverables, tracked monthly against the fee, with agreed rules for unused or extra hours.

Which methodology is best for agency project management?

Match it to the contract: phased Waterfall for fixed-price deliverables, Scrum for team-based digital builds, Kanban for retainers and hybrid for fixed milestones with Agile delivery inside.

What templates help with agency project management?

A proposal, cost estimate, creative brief, Gantt chart, change request, RAID log and lessons learned template cover the full life cycle.

What software is used for agency project management?

A work management platform such as Wrike for planning, proofing and resourcing, often paired with an agency management or finance system. Small agencies may prefer simpler tools such as Basecamp.

Why do agencies lose money on projects?

Mostly through optimistic estimates, unbilled extra rounds of amends, over-servicing retainers, unlogged time and work that slips because of late client feedback.
