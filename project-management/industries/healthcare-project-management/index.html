Healthcare Project Management: Phases, Roles and Examples

Healthcare project management is the planning and delivery of change in hospitals, clinics and health systems, such as electronic health record (EHR) rollouts, new clinical pathways, facility upgrades and quality improvement work. It uses the same core methods as any project, but patient safety, health data regulation, services that run around the clock and scarce clinician time change the priorities. This guide covers the life cycle, clinical safety, roles, methods, two worked examples, KPIs, templates and software.

Key takeaways

  • Healthcare project management puts patient safety, clinical governance and data protection at the centre of planning.
  • Keep a project risk register and a clinical hazard log, cross-referenced, and make clinical safety sign-off a formal gate.
  • Most large programmes are stage-gated overall, with Lean, PDSA or Agile cycles used inside workstreams.
  • Clinician engagement, training readiness and a controlled go-live decide success more than the plan itself.
  • Plan training and backfill as real costs: releasing staff from wards is often the biggest hidden effort.
  • Keep patient-identifiable data out of project tools unless the vendor's compliance position has been confirmed.

What is healthcare project management?

Healthcare project management is the application of project management methods to temporary initiatives in health and care organisations, with patient safety, clinical governance and health data protection built into every stage. A healthcare project manager coordinates clinical, operational, IT, estates and supplier teams to deliver change without disrupting care.

It happens in acute hospitals, health systems and NHS trusts, primary care networks, community and mental health services, public health bodies and the suppliers that build health technology. Projects range from a single-ward improvement to multi-year, multi-site programmes.

Clinical systems and digitalEHR or electronic prescribing rollout, patient portal, lab system replacementClinical safety, data migration and downtime
Clinical pathway redesignSame-day emergency care, a new stroke pathway, virtual wardsClinician buy-in and unintended effects on other services
Quality improvementReducing falls, improving discharge times, cutting medication errorsMeasurement and sustaining the change

What makes healthcare projects different

A late office move is an inconvenience; a badly planned change to a medication workflow can harm patients. That one difference shifts the emphasis towards clinical risk, testing, training and controlled go-lives.

Patient safety and clinical risk

Changes to clinical systems or pathways can introduce new hazards, such as a wrong default dose or a missed alert. They need a structured clinical risk assessment and sign-off by an appropriately qualified clinician.

Health data is heavily regulated

Health records are among the most sensitive data an organisation holds. Privacy law, information governance rules and supplier contracts shape what systems you can use and how data moves during the project.

Services cannot stop

Wards, emergency departments and many clinics run 24 hours a day, every day. Cutovers, downtime and training must fit around rotas, bank holidays and seasonal peaks such as winter.

Clinician time is scarce

Doctors, nurses, pharmacists and allied health professionals are the essential subject matter experts, but they can only be released from clinical duties with notice and backfill.

Many stakeholders hold a veto

Clinical leads, nursing and medical directors, information governance, IT, estates, infection prevention, finance, staff side representatives and sometimes patients and the public all need to be engaged. Our guide to stakeholder management covers the techniques.

Inspection and accreditation

Regulators and accrediting bodies, such as the Care Quality Commission (CQC) in England or The Joint Commission in the US, expect organisations to show that changes were governed and safe. Good project records double as evidence.

Clinical safety and information governance

This is the part of healthcare project management with no real equivalent in most other sectors. Treat it as a workstream with its own owner, plan and gates, not as a checklist at the end.

Project risks vs clinical hazards

A project risk threatens the project's objectives, such as a late go-live or a budget overrun. A clinical hazard is a potential source of harm to a patient, such as a drug interaction alert that fails to fire. Keep them in separate logs, owned by different people, but cross-reference them: a delayed test cycle (project risk) may leave a hazard unmitigated.

Project risk registerClinical hazard log
Concerned withScope, time, cost, quality, benefitsPotential harm to patients
Owned byProject managerClinical safety lead or Clinical Safety Officer
Scored byProbability and impact on the projectLikelihood and severity of harm
Closed whenThe risk passes or is managedThe hazard is mitigated to an acceptable level and signed off
TemplateRisk register templateOrganisation's clinical safety format

An example hazard log entry for an electronic prescribing project might read: Hazard: a prescriber selects the wrong patient from a similar-name list. Cause: patient search shows names without date of birth. Effect: a medicine is prescribed to the wrong patient. Controls: show date of birth and hospital number in search results, wristband scanning at administration, training on patient identification. Residual risk: assessed and accepted by the Clinical Safety Officer before go-live.

Standards and rules to know

  • England: health IT is governed by the clinical risk management standards DCB0129 (for manufacturers) and DCB0160 (for organisations deploying and using systems), each requiring a named Clinical Safety Officer. NHS England ran a consultation on revisions to both standards from June to September 2026, so check the current versions before you plan.
  • England, buying digital tools: NHS organisations use the Digital Technology Assessment Criteria (DTAC) to assess digital health technologies. NHS England published an updated DTAC form and guidance in March 2026.
  • UK and EU data protection: health data is special category data under UK GDPR and GDPR, so new systems or new uses of patient data normally need a data protection impact assessment (DPIA).
  • United States: HIPAA governs protected health information (PHI). A vendor that creates, receives, maintains or transmits PHI on your behalf is a business associate and needs a business associate agreement (BAA).
  • Medical device software: some clinical software is regulated as a medical device (by the MHRA in the UK or the FDA in the US), which adds manufacturer obligations. Confirm the classification with the supplier.
Tip

Rules change and vary by country and setting. Ask your clinical safety and information governance leads which standards apply before the plan is baselined, and put their approvals in the schedule as gates.

Healthcare project life cycle

Most healthcare organisations use a staged, governance-heavy life cycle, even when individual workstreams use Agile methods. A typical clinical system project runs through seven phases.

PhaseWhat happensTypical gate or output
1. Case for changeProblem defined with clinical and operational data; options appraised; funding soughtApproved business case
2. InitiationSponsor, clinical lead and governance confirmed; scope, budget and benefits agreedProject charter or project initiation document
3. Clinical designCurrent and future workflows mapped with clinicians; configuration specifiedSigned-off workflows; initial hazard assessment
4. Build and testConfiguration, integrations (lab, pharmacy, imaging), data migration; system, integration and user acceptance testingTest exit report; hazard log updated
5. Training and readinessRole-based training, super users, cutover rehearsals, go/no-go criteria agreedGo/no-go decision; clinical safety sign-off
6. Go-live and stabilisationCutover, command centre, at-the-elbow support, rapid issue triageStabilisation exit criteria met
7. Optimisation and closureFixes and enhancements, benefits tracking, handover to business as usual, lessons learnedClosure report; benefits review

How this maps to the standard project phases

If you know the classic phases of initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing, phases 1 and 2 are initiation, phase 3 is detailed planning and design, phases 4 to 6 are execution, and phase 7 is closure. The healthcare version makes clinical design, safety sign-off and stabilisation visible as their own stages. Smaller quality improvement projects use a much lighter cycle, covered in the second worked example below.

How to run a healthcare project step by step

Use these steps for any healthcare project and scale the governance to its size and clinical risk.

  1. Define the problem with data. Describe the clinical or operational problem and its current measures, for example average time from discharge decision to departure.
  2. Secure an executive sponsor and a clinical lead. The sponsor owns benefits and funding; the clinical lead owns clinical decisions and brings peers with them.
  3. Agree governance early. Confirm the project board, clinical safety lead, information governance contact and how decisions are escalated.
  4. Write the charter. Purpose, scope, out of scope, benefits, budget (including backfill), milestones and gates. Our project charter template covers the essentials.
  5. Map stakeholders and staff groups. List every role affected, how they will be engaged and when they need training.
  6. Plan around the clinical calendar. Add rota changes, resident doctor rotation dates, bank holidays and winter pressures before scheduling go-lives.
  7. Design with clinicians, not for them. Run short, focused design sessions and let clinical leads present decisions to their peers.
  8. Run safety and governance alongside delivery. Keep the hazard log, DPIA and test evidence up to date as the design changes.
  9. Decide go-live on evidence. Use pre-agreed go/no-go criteria, and rehearse cutover and rollback.
  10. Support, stabilise, then close. Provide at-the-elbow support, triage issues daily, measure benefits and capture lessons learned before the next site goes live.

Worked example: rolling out electronic prescribing across a hospital

A 600-bed hospital is replacing paper drug charts with an electronic prescribing and medicines administration (EPMA) system across 30 inpatient wards. The chief nurse is the sponsor, a consultant physician is the clinical lead, a pharmacist is the Clinical Safety Officer and a project manager coordinates the programme. The figures below are illustrative.

Choosing the rollout approach

A single big-bang go-live would demand training every staff group at once and leave no room to learn. The project board chooses a phased rollout: two pilot wards first, then waves of seven wards every three weeks. The trade-off is a period where patients moving between wards switch between paper and electronic charts, which is logged as a clinical hazard with specific controls for transfers.

Sizing training and backfill

At 7.5-hour shifts, 3,680 hours is about 490 shifts of staff time. Most of it needs backfill from bank or agency staff, so the project manager costs it into the budget and agrees release dates with ward managers ward by ward. Super users (two per ward per shift pattern) receive extra training and are rostered as supernumerary during their ward's first week live. A training plan template keeps the sessions, attendance and completion by ward in one place.

Go/no-go criteria for each wave

CriterionThreshold agreed by the project boardOwner
Staff trainedAt least 90% of each ward's rostered staff trained, and every shift covered by a trained super userTraining lead
Clinical hazardsNo open hazards rated high; all medium hazards have controls in placeClinical Safety Officer
TestingAll critical and high defects fixed and retestedTest lead
Devices and infrastructureBedside devices, scanners and Wi-Fi tested on every bayIT lead
Downtime proceduresPaper backup charts and downtime process available and briefedWard managers
SupportCommand centre and at-the-elbow rota confirmed for the first two weeksProject manager
Operational pressureHospital not at its highest escalation level on the planned dateSite operations

Go-live and stabilisation

  1. Pharmacists transcribe current paper charts into the system for each ward the day before go-live, with a second check.
  2. The ward goes live at a planned time agreed with the ward manager, avoiding medicine rounds.
  3. A command centre runs daily huddles, logs issues and tracks themes; urgent clinical issues go straight to the Clinical Safety Officer.
  4. Super users and floor walkers provide at-the-elbow support on every shift for the first week.
  5. The project board reviews stabilisation exit criteria (for example, issue volumes falling and no unresolved safety incidents) before releasing support and starting the next wave.

Lessons from the pilot wards, such as needing more devices on drug rounds, are fed into the next wave's plan. This is the main advantage of phasing: each wave is safer than the last.

Worked example: a quality improvement project with PDSA cycles

Not every healthcare project is a large programme. Ward-level improvements usually use the Model for Improvement, which asks three questions (what are we trying to accomplish, how will we know a change is an improvement, what changes can we make) and then tests changes in small Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles.

Illustrative aim: increase the share of patients discharged before noon on one medical ward over six months. Measures: an outcome measure (percentage of discharges before noon), a process measure (percentage of patients with a planned discharge date set within 24 hours of admission) and a balancing measure (readmissions within 30 days), all plotted weekly on a run chart.

CycleChange testedWhat was learnedDecision
PDSA 1Next-day discharges identified at the afternoon board round for one consultant team for one weekWorked well, but medicines to take home were still lateAdapt: involve pharmacy
PDSA 2Pharmacy prepares medicines the evening before for identified patientsFewer morning delays; transport bookings now the bottleneckAdapt: add transport
PDSA 3Transport booked the day before for identified patientsMost planned patients left before noonAdopt for this team
PDSA 4Process extended to all consultant teams on the wardSome teams' board rounds are later in the dayAdapt timing, then spread

The project management is light: a one-page charter, a simple action log, a run chart and a short weekly huddle. The discipline is in measuring, testing small and only spreading what works. For the wider toolkit behind this kind of work, see our guide to Lean project management.

A RACI matrix is especially useful in healthcare because clinical, technical and governance responsibilities overlap. Be explicit about who approves each clinical workflow and who signs off safety.

What does a healthcare project manager do?

A healthcare project manager turns a clinical or operational objective into a governed plan and keeps it moving. Typical duties include building the schedule and budget, coordinating clinical, IT, estates and supplier teams, running the project board, managing risks and issues alongside the clinical hazard log, planning training and backfill, leading go-live readiness and command centre arrangements, and reporting benefits.

Skills, qualifications and how to get into the role

  • Project skills: planning, risk management, stakeholder management and clear reporting.
  • Healthcare knowledge: how clinical teams work, clinical governance, patient flow and information governance.
  • Credibility with clinicians: listening, translating between clinical and technical language, and respecting clinical time.
  • Certification: many employers ask for a recognised qualification such as the PMP certification or PRINCE2; see our project management certification guide for options.
  • Routes in: clinicians often move in through improvement or digital roles; project managers from other sectors often start as project officers or in health IT, learning the clinical context on the job.

Methodologies that fit healthcare projects

Choose the method by the type of change. Many programmes combine them.

ApproachBest forNotes
Stage-gate or WaterfallCapital builds, major system procurements, regulated go-livesClear gates suit boards and auditors; plan safety and testing gates explicitly
PRINCE2Public health systems such as the NHSWidely used in UK public healthcare; project boards and management by exception fit trust governance
LeanPatient flow, waiting times, theatre utilisationValue stream mapping and waste removal are well established in hospitals
PDSA and the Model for ImprovementWard-level changes, pathways, clinical audit actionsSmall tests of change measured on run charts
Six SigmaReducing variation, for example in lab turnaround or medication errorsOften combined with Lean
Agile or ScrumDigital health apps, patient portals, in-house softwareBuild clinical safety review into the definition of done
HybridEHR and EPMA programmesStage-gated overall, with iterative configuration and test cycles inside the build phase
Tip

Whatever method you choose, make clinical safety sign-off and information governance approval explicit gates in the plan, not tasks someone remembers at the end.

Essential documents and templates

Scale the document set to the project. A ward-level improvement might need a charter and a run chart; an EHR programme needs everything below.

  • Business case and project charter: the problem, clinical and financial benefits, scope and sponsor.
  • Work breakdown structure: clinical design, build, integration, data migration, training, go-live and support workstreams.
  • Schedule: planned around rotas, holidays and seasonal peaks, with gates shown as milestones.
  • Risk register and clinical hazard log: separate but cross-referenced.
  • DPIA and data sharing agreements: owned by information governance.
  • Communication plan: tailored messages for consultants, nurses, administrative staff and patients.
  • Training plan: role-based training, super user schedule and completion tracking.
  • Go/no-go checklist and cutover plan: including rollback and downtime procedures.
  • Change request log: formal change control once clinical design is signed off.
  • Closure report and benefits review: what was delivered, lessons learned and who owns benefits from here.

Common challenges and how to handle them

  • Clinician engagement. Involve clinicians from the start, fund backfill, keep sessions short and let clinical leads present decisions to peers.
  • Local variation. Every ward wants its own workflow. Agree a design principle (standardise unless there is a safety reason not to) and route requests through change control.
  • Fixed go-live dates. Contract or legacy system end dates often fix the deadline. Protect it with go/no-go criteria and a rehearsed rollback plan, not by cutting testing.
  • Data migration quality. Start data cleansing early and run several trial loads with clinical validation.
  • Operational pressure. Avoid go-lives in known peak periods and agree what escalation level would trigger a postponement.
  • Change fatigue. Coordinate with other programmes through the PMO so wards are not hit by several changes at once.
  • Declaring success at go-live. Keep measuring adoption and outcomes for months, and budget for optimisation.

Software for healthcare project management

Healthcare teams typically use a general work management platform for planning alongside specialist systems: EHR suppliers' implementation and testing tools, IT service management for post go-live tickets, and incident reporting systems for patient safety events. When choosing a project tool, look for:

  • Gantt charts, dependencies and baselines for gated plans.
  • Portfolio dashboards so a PMO can see every project and shared clinical resources.
  • Request forms for intake of improvement ideas and change requests.
  • Strong access controls, audit trails and single sign-on.
  • A clear data position: keep patient-identifiable data out of the project tool, and if it could ever be stored there, confirm the vendor's compliance commitments (for example a HIPAA BAA in the US, or a DPIA in the UK) before you buy.

Wrike is a strong all-round option for health system PMOs because it combines Gantt planning, portfolio views, request forms and enterprise security controls in one platform.

Frequently asked questions

What is healthcare project management?

Healthcare project management is the planning and delivery of projects such as EHR rollouts, clinical pathway changes, facility upgrades and quality improvement work, with patient safety, clinical governance and health data protection built into every stage.

What does a healthcare project manager do?

They plan and control scope, schedule, budget and risk, coordinate clinical, IT, estates and supplier teams, run governance gates, plan training and backfill, lead go-live readiness and support, and report benefits.

What qualifications do you need for healthcare project management?

Most roles ask for project management experience plus a recognised qualification such as PMP, CAPM or PRINCE2. A clinical, health IT or healthcare operations background is a strong advantage but not always required.

Can you become a healthcare project manager without a clinical background?

Yes. Many healthcare project managers come from IT, operations or other sectors. Strong clinical leads on the project provide the clinical expertise, and you learn the context through the work. Respect for clinical time and governance matters more than a clinical qualification.

What are examples of healthcare projects?

EHR and electronic prescribing rollouts, patient portals, new clinical pathways, virtual wards, theatre and ward refurbishments, quality improvement projects such as reducing falls, service mergers and preparation for inspections.

What are the phases of healthcare project management?

A typical life cycle is case for change, initiation, clinical design, build and test, training and readiness, go-live and stabilisation, then optimisation and closure.

Which methodology is best for healthcare projects?

Large system and building projects usually use stage-gate or PRINCE2, improvement work uses Lean or PDSA cycles, and digital products often use Agile. Many programmes combine them.

How is healthcare project management different from other project management?

It adds clinical safety assessment, clinician-led design, 24/7 service continuity, health data regulation and inspection readiness to the usual concerns of scope, time and cost.

What is a Clinical Safety Officer?

In England, a Clinical Safety Officer is a suitably qualified and registered clinician who is responsible for clinical risk management of a health IT system under the DCB0129 and DCB0160 standards, including the hazard log and safety case.

Is Agile used in healthcare?

Yes, especially for digital health products and in-house software. Clinical safety review and governance approvals still need to be built into each release, for example through the definition of done.

What KPIs are used in healthcare projects?

Gate adherence, budget variance, training completion, open clinical hazards, post go-live incidents, adoption, and clinical or flow outcomes such as length of stay and waiting times.

Is project management software HIPAA compliant?

It depends on the vendor, plan and contract. If protected health information could be stored in the tool, confirm the vendor will sign a business associate agreement and configure the required controls. The safest approach is to keep patient data out of project tools.
