Engineering Project Management: Phases, Roles and Methods

Engineering project management is the planning and control of projects whose main output is an engineered product, system or facility, taking it from requirements through design, build, verification and handover. It combines ordinary project control of scope, time, cost and risk with technical control: requirements traceability, design reviews, configuration and change control, and testing. This guide explains the phases and gates, a step-by-step process, worked examples, roles, methods, KPIs and tools.

Key takeaways

  • Engineering project management pairs project control (scope, schedule, cost, risk) with technical control (requirements, design reviews, configuration and verification).
  • Design reviews and gates are the main decision points; change becomes much more expensive after design freeze.
  • Trace every requirement to a design element and a test, so you always know what is proven and what is not.
  • Plan for test failures and long-lead parts explicitly; they decide the end date more often than design tasks do.
  • Hybrid approaches work best: iterate on software and early prototypes inside a gated plan for hardware.
  • Keep design data in CAD and PLM, and use a work management platform such as Wrike for schedules, resources and approvals.

What is engineering project management?

Engineering project management is the discipline of planning, organising and controlling a project that designs and delivers an engineered output, such as a machine, a product, a control system or a plant. It covers the usual project concerns of scope, schedule, cost, risk and people, plus the technical controls that prove the design works: requirements, design reviews, configuration management and verification.

The work is usually led by a project manager working alongside a chief or lead engineer. The project manager owns the plan, budget and commitments; the lead engineer owns the technical solution.

Types of engineering projects

Project typeTypical outputWhat usually drives the plan
New product developmentA product ready for manufacturePrototype cycles, tooling lead times, certification
Machinery and automationA special-purpose machine, test rig or production lineLong-lead components, factory and site acceptance tests
Aerospace and defence systemsA system or subsystem meeting a specificationFormal design reviews, qualification testing, configuration control
Process plant and capital projectsA plant, skid or facility upgradeEngineering deliverables, procurement, construction and commissioning
Civil and structural engineeringBridges, structures, foundationsDesign checks, approvals and site constraints

Manufacturing-led projects such as line changes and new product introduction are covered in our guide to manufacturing project management.

Engineering project management vs engineering management

The two terms are often confused. Engineering management is the ongoing leadership of an engineering function; engineering project management is the delivery of a specific, time-bound project.

Engineering project managementEngineering management
FocusDeliver one project to scope, time, cost and qualityRun and develop an engineering team or department
Time horizonDefined start and endOngoing
Typical decisionsPlan, budget, risks, suppliers, change, acceptanceHiring, skills, standards, tools, capacity across projects
Success measured byProject objectives met and the output acceptedTeam capability, quality and throughput over time
Typical titleEngineering project manager, technical project managerEngineering manager, head of engineering

In smaller firms one person often does both. If you are making that move from a technical role, our guide to the technical project manager role covers the skills involved.

What makes engineering projects different

In engineering projects the hardest risks are usually technical. A design that cannot be manufactured, a component that fails qualification or a misunderstood requirement can cost far more than a late task. That changes how you plan and control the work.

  • Requirements must be traceable. Every requirement should link to a design element and to a test that proves it.
  • Change is expensive late. Prototypes, tooling, certification and procurement commitments make late design changes costly, so design freezes matter.
  • Safety and standards. Many products must meet regulatory or industry standards for areas such as pressure equipment, machinery safety, aerospace, automotive or medical devices.
  • Multiple disciplines. Mechanical, electrical, software, civil and process engineers must integrate their work through clearly defined interfaces.
  • Technology maturity. New technology carries uncertainty that no amount of scheduling removes.
  • Long-lead procurement. Castings, motors, specialist electronics and certified materials can take months to arrive and often sit on the critical path.
  • Test and rework cycles. New designs rarely pass every test first time, and retest loops are real work that needs to be in the plan.

Technology readiness levels (TRLs)

Many engineering organisations use technology readiness levels, a nine-point scale developed by NASA, to judge how mature a technology is. NASA describes TRL 1 as basic research, TRL 6 as a fully functional prototype or representative model, and TRL 9 as technology proven in operation. You can use the scale to decide how much schedule and cost risk to carry:

TRL bandWhat it meansPlanning implication (our advice)
TRL 1–3Principles and proof of conceptTreat as research; fund in short, capped stages rather than a fixed delivery plan
TRL 4–6Components and prototypes tested in increasingly realistic conditionsPlan explicit prototype and test iterations with go/no-go points
TRL 7–9Demonstrated, qualified and proven in operationSuitable for a committed delivery schedule with normal contingency
Tip

If a project needs a technology below about TRL 6 to meet its specification, raise it as a top risk at the first gate and agree a fallback design. Committing to a fixed delivery date on immature technology is one of the most common causes of engineering overruns.

Engineering project phases and design reviews

Life cycle names vary by sector, but most engineering projects follow the same logic: decide, define, design, build, prove and hand over. Each phase ends with a review where the sponsor and technical authority decide whether the project is ready to move on.

PhaseWhat happensTypical gate or review
1. Concept and feasibilityDefine the need, explore options, assess technical and commercial feasibilityConcept review / gate 1
2. Requirements definitionCapture stakeholder needs and turn them into verifiable system requirementsSystem requirements review (SRR)
3. Preliminary designSystem architecture, major trade-offs, early analysis, long-lead item identificationPreliminary design review (PDR)
4. Detailed designFull models, drawings, calculations, software design and specificationsCritical design review (CDR) and design freeze
5. Build or fabricationProcure parts, manufacture prototypes or construct the facilityTest readiness or production readiness review
6. Integration, verification and validationTest subsystems and the whole system against requirements and user needsFactory or site acceptance tests, qualification
7. Handover and supportDocumentation, training, commissioning and transfer to operations or productionFinal acceptance and project closure

The international standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 (current edition 2023) describes system life cycle processes in more detail and is a useful reference if your organisation is formalising its own life cycle.

PDR, CDR and design freeze

The preliminary design review (PDR) checks that the chosen architecture can meet the requirements within cost and schedule, and that the risks are understood. The critical design review (CDR) checks that the detailed design is complete enough to build and test. Passing CDR usually triggers a design freeze: from then on, every change goes through formal engineering change control because it can affect parts already ordered, tooling and test plans.

The V-model

The V-model, widely used in systems engineering, pairs each level of design on the left of the V with a matching level of testing on the right. It is a simple way to make sure verification is planned at the same time as design, not afterwards.

Left side: define and designRight side: matching verification
User needs and concept of operationsValidation and user acceptance
System requirementsSystem verification tests
Subsystem designSubsystem integration tests
Component designComponent and unit tests

How to manage an engineering project step by step

The steps below follow a typical engineering project from need to closure. Scale the formality to the size and risk of the project.

  1. Clarify the need and success criteria. Agree the problem, the performance targets, the budget envelope and the date the output is needed. Record them in a project charter.
  2. Appoint the project manager and technical authority. Name one person accountable for delivery and one for the technical solution, and agree how they make joint decisions.
  3. Assess feasibility and technology maturity. Compare options, check TRLs and identify the riskiest assumptions to test first.
  4. Write verifiable requirements. Each requirement should be measurable and have a verification method (test, analysis, inspection or demonstration). A business requirements document template is a good starting structure; see our guide to project requirements.
  5. Build the product-based work breakdown structure. Break the system into subsystems and deliverables, then add the work to design, buy, build and test each one using a WBS template.
  6. Identify long-lead items and interfaces. List every part with a long lead time and every interface between disciplines or suppliers, each with an owner.
  7. Estimate with ranges. Estimate effort by discipline and add allowances for prototype iterations and retests; record assumptions in a project cost estimate.
  8. Build an integrated schedule. Link design, procurement, build and test activities, find the critical path and set a baseline. A Gantt chart template or critical path template works for smaller projects.
  9. Set up risk, change and configuration control. Open a risk register, agree a change board and decide how drawings and part numbers are released and versioned.
  10. Run design reviews as real gates. Circulate review packs in advance, record actions, and only pass the gate when the entry criteria are met.
  11. Control delivery each period. Track progress with earned value or milestone completion, review risks, and process engineering changes through the change request route.
  12. Verify, validate and hand over. Run the test plan, close non-conformances, complete documentation and training, and obtain formal acceptance.
  13. Close and learn. Archive the configuration baseline, release the team and capture lessons learned for the next project.

Worked example: an automated test rig project

To make the techniques concrete, follow a single example. An engineering firm is designing and building an automated test rig for a customer's electric motors. It has a budget at completion of £600,000, a 12-month schedule, and mechanical, electrical and software workstreams. All figures are illustrative.

Step 1: build a requirements traceability matrix

The team writes each requirement so it can be verified, then links it to the design element that satisfies it and the test that proves it. An extract:

Req IDRequirementDesign elementVerificationStatus
R-001Rig shall test motors up to 50 kgFrame drawing TR-100; lifting fixture TR-140Load test VT-07Passed
R-002Rig shall measure torque to ±0.5%Torque transducer spec E-220; calibration procedureCalibration test VT-12Open
R-003Guarding shall prevent access while the motor runsInterlocked guard TR-300; safety PLC logic SW-40Interlock test VT-15Failed, retest planned
R-004Test cycle shall complete in under 6 minutesTest sequence software SW-10Timed demonstration VT-20Not started

At any review, the matrix shows how many requirements are proven, which are at risk and which have no test yet. A requirement with no verification method is a gap you want to find at PDR, not at acceptance.

Step 2: measure progress with earned value

At the end of month 6, the plan said half the work would be done. The project controls figures are:

MeasureValueMeaning
Budget at completion (BAC)£600,000Total approved budget
Planned value (PV)£300,000Budgeted cost of the work scheduled to date
Earned value (EV)£255,000Budgeted cost of the work actually completed
Actual cost (AC)£290,000What the completed work actually cost
Cost variance (EV − AC)−£35,000Over budget for the work done
Schedule variance (EV − PV)−£45,000Behind plan
Cost performance index (EV ÷ AC)0.88£0.88 of planned work for every £1 spent
Schedule performance index (EV ÷ PV)0.85Progressing at 85% of the planned rate

If cost performance stays at this level, the estimate at completion is BAC ÷ CPI = £600,000 ÷ 0.879 ≈ £682,000. If both cost and schedule pressure persist, a more cautious forecast is AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI) = £290,000 + £345,000 ÷ 0.747 ≈ £752,000. To finish on the original budget, the to-complete performance index would need to be (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) = £345,000 ÷ £310,000 ≈ 1.11, meaning every remaining pound must buy £1.11 of work. That is unlikely without changing scope or approach, so the project manager takes the forecast to the sponsor now rather than hoping to recover.

Step 3: assess an engineering change

After the critical design review, the customer asks for the rig to handle motors up to 65 kg instead of 50 kg. Before anyone agrees, the team assesses the impact across every discipline:

AreaImpactExtra costSchedule effect
MechanicalStiffen frame, redesign lifting fixture, re-issue 9 drawings£14,000+3 weeks of design
ElectricalLarger drive and cabling; drive is a long-lead item£9,500+6 weeks lead time (critical path)
SoftwareUpdate load limits, safety logic and test sequences£4,000+1 week, runs in parallel
VerificationRepeat load and interlock tests at the new rating£3,500+1 week
DocumentationUpdate risk assessment, manuals and traceability matrix£1,500Runs in parallel
Total£32,500About +7 weeks to completion

The change board presents three options to the customer: accept the change with the extra cost and date; defer it to a later upgrade; or approve an early order for the larger drive while the design work is confirmed, which cuts about three weeks of the delay for a small cost at risk. The decision, whichever it is, is recorded and the baseline updated.

Step 4: plan for test failures and retests

The verification plan has 40 tests. Based on its previous rigs, the team assumes about four in five will pass first time, so it expects around 8 failures. Each failure typically needs about 3 days of diagnosis and rework and 1 day of retest on the rig. Rework can run in parallel with other tests, but rig time cannot, so the plan adds roughly 8 days of rig time plus a small buffer at the end of the test phase instead of assuming every test passes. Tracking the actual first-time pass rate as testing proceeds shows quickly whether that allowance is enough.

Day to day, the engineering project manager maintains the schedule and forecasts, chairs progress, risk and change meetings, balances engineers' time across projects, chases long-lead suppliers and keeps the customer informed, trading off performance, cost and time with the lead engineer when problems arise.

Methodologies for engineering projects

There is no single best method. Choose based on how well the requirements are understood, how mature the technology is and how much of the product is software.

ApproachBest forHow it works in engineering
Stage-gateProduct development and capital projectsGo, hold or stop decisions at each gate control investment and risk
V-model and systems engineeringComplex and safety-critical systemsEach design level has a matching test level, planned together
WaterfallFacilities and well-understood designsSequential phases with formal sign-offs
Agile and hybridSoftware-heavy products and early prototypingShort iterations inside a gated plan; see iterative and incremental development
Critical path methodAny project with many dependenciesFinds the longest chain of dependent work and the float on everything else
Critical chainMulti-project environments with shared specialistsBuffers protect the end date rather than each task

Combining Agile with gates for hardware

Agile works well for software and early concept work, where feedback is fast and changes are cheap. Hardware still needs gates for long-lead parts, tooling and certification. A practical hybrid is to keep the stage-gate plan and design reviews for the system, run the software team in sprints, and align a few integration points where each hardware build meets a defined software release. Plan both rhythms explicitly rather than letting one team wait on the other.

Common mistakes in engineering project management

  • Treating design reviews as presentations. A review that never says no is not a gate. Set entry criteria and record actions.
  • Writing requirements that cannot be tested. Words such as 'robust' or 'user-friendly' need measurable criteria.
  • Freezing the design on paper only. If changes after CDR do not go through change control, the freeze means nothing.
  • Leaving procurement until the design is complete. Long-lead items need to be identified and, where possible, ordered earlier.
  • Reporting percent complete by feel. Use measurable progress such as drawings released, tests passed or earned value.

Software for engineering project management

Engineering organisations run several systems, each holding part of the picture. The project management platform should coordinate work and decisions, not duplicate engineering data.

When choosing the project platform, look for dependencies and critical path, baselines, workload and resource management across projects, approval workflows, custom fields for items such as part or drawing numbers, and dashboards for sponsors. Wrike fits this coordination role well and leads our tool rankings, with Gantt charts, workload views and cross-project portfolio reporting. Keep design data in your PLM system and link to it from tasks rather than duplicating it.

Skills, qualifications and career path

Most engineering project managers start as engineers and move into project roles by leading work packages, then whole projects. The skills that matter most are a mix of technical and management ability.

  • Technical credibility in at least one discipline, so you can follow design trade-offs and challenge estimates.
  • Planning and project controls: scheduling, estimating, earned value and risk analysis.
  • Commercial awareness: contracts, supplier management and change pricing.
  • Communication and negotiation with customers, suppliers and engineers.
  • Quality and safety awareness relevant to your sector's standards.

Useful qualifications

  • Professional engineering registration, such as Chartered Engineer (CEng) through the Engineering Council in the UK or a Professional Engineer (PE) licence in the US.
  • Systems engineering certification from INCOSE, which offers an entry-level ASEP, the CSEP for experienced practitioners and the ESEP at expert level.
  • Project management certification such as the PMP or PRINCE2.

Frequently asked questions

What is engineering project management?

Engineering project management is the planning and control of projects that design and deliver an engineered product, system or facility. It combines scope, schedule, cost and risk control with technical control of requirements, design reviews, configuration and testing.

What does an engineering project manager do?

They plan and control the project: building the schedule and budget, managing risks, suppliers and change, running progress and design review meetings, balancing engineers' time and reporting to the sponsor and customer, working closely with the lead engineer who owns the technical solution.

What is the difference between engineering management and engineering project management?

Engineering management is the ongoing leadership of an engineering team or function. Engineering project management delivers a specific, time-bound project to agreed scope, time, cost and quality.

Do you need an engineering degree to be an engineering project manager?

Not always, but on technically complex projects most employers expect an engineering degree or equivalent experience, because the role involves judging design trade-offs and technical risk.

What are the phases of an engineering project?

Typically concept and feasibility, requirements definition, preliminary design, detailed design, build or fabrication, integration and verification, and handover. Each ends with a review or gate.

What methodology is best for engineering projects?

It depends on the project. Stage-gate and the V-model suit hardware and safety-critical systems, Waterfall suits well-understood designs, and Agile suits software and early prototyping. Many teams use a hybrid of gates for hardware and sprints for software.

What is a design freeze?

A design freeze is the point, usually after the critical design review, after which the design is baselined and any change must go through formal engineering change control because it can affect parts, tooling and tests.

What is the difference between PDR and CDR?

A preliminary design review (PDR) checks that the chosen architecture can meet the requirements and that the risks are understood. A critical design review (CDR) checks that the detailed design is complete enough to build and test.

What is a requirements traceability matrix?

It is a table linking each requirement to the design elements that satisfy it and the tests that verify it, with the current status. It shows what has been proven and highlights requirements without a test.

What are technology readiness levels?

Technology readiness levels are a nine-point scale, developed by NASA, for judging how mature a technology is, from basic research at TRL 1 to proven operation at TRL 9. Low TRLs signal high schedule and cost risk.

What KPIs should engineering project managers track?

Useful KPIs include cost and schedule performance indices, milestones on time, requirements verified, engineering change rate after design freeze, open design review actions, drawings released on time, first-time test pass rate and non-conformances.

What software do engineering project managers use?

CAD and simulation tools for design, PLM for parts and change records, requirements tools on complex systems, ERP for purchasing, and a work management platform such as Wrike for schedules, resources, approvals and reporting.
