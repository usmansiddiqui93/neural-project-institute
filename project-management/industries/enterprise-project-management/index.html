Enterprise Project Management (EPM): Guide and Examples

Enterprise project management (EPM) is the practice of managing all of an organisation's projects as one coordinated system, with shared governance, standard methods, common tools and a single view of the portfolio and the people who deliver it. It moves the question from "is this project on track?" to "are we running the right projects, and do we have the capacity to finish them?" This guide explains how EPM works, how it differs from a PMO and portfolio management, and how to prioritise, resource, govern and measure projects at enterprise scale, with worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • Enterprise project management treats every project as part of one portfolio with shared governance, standards, data and tools.
  • An enterprise PMO (EPMO) is strategic and organisation-wide; a departmental PMO is mainly tactical and supports delivery.
  • Prioritise with a transparent scoring model, then check capacity before approving anything: capacity is the real constraint.
  • Tier your governance so small projects stay light and large, risky investments get stage gates and assurance.
  • Start simple (inventory, intake, standard status), use one platform as the source of truth, and add resource and benefits management in stages.

What is enterprise project management?

Enterprise project management (EPM) is an organisation-wide approach that selects, prioritises, resources, governs and reports on all projects and programmes as one system. It combines portfolio decisions, standard project methods, enterprise resource management and consistent reporting so that leaders can fund the work that best supports strategy and stop work that no longer does.

Ordinary project management asks how to deliver one project well. EPM sits a level above and answers three questions continuously:

  1. Are we doing the right projects? Every proposal is judged against strategy, value and risk using the same criteria.
  2. Can we deliver them? Demand for people, skills and money is compared with real capacity before work starts.
  3. Are they delivering value? Health, spend and benefits are reported the same way across the portfolio, so problems surface early.

EPM overlaps with portfolio and programme management. For how the three levels fit together, see our guide to program vs project vs portfolio management.

EPM vs traditional project management vs portfolio management

The terms are often used loosely. This comparison shows where each one focuses.

Project managementProject portfolio management (PPM)Enterprise project management (EPM)
ScopeOne projectA set of projects and programmesAll projects, programmes and portfolios across the organisation
Main questionAre we delivering this project on time, on budget and to scope?Which projects should we fund and in what order?How do we select, resource, govern and deliver all projects consistently?
Time horizonThe project's lifePlanning cycles (quarterly or annual)Continuous
Typical ownerProject managerPortfolio manager or portfolio boardEnterprise PMO and executive team
Key outputsDeliverables, status reportsPrioritised portfolio, funding decisionsStandards, governance, enterprise resource view, portfolio reporting, benefits tracking

In practice, EPM includes portfolio management and adds the standards, tools, resource management and capability building that make portfolio decisions possible. Many organisations use "EPM" and "PPM" interchangeably; what matters is that someone owns both the decisions and the system behind them.

EPMO vs PMO: what is the difference?

Enterprise project management is usually run by an enterprise project management office (EPMO). A departmental project management office (PMO) often already exists in IT, engineering or change functions. The two work together, but their remit differs.

Departmental PMOEnterprise PMO (EPMO)
ScopeOne business unit or functionThe whole organisation
FocusTactical: delivery support, methods, reportingStrategic: portfolio selection, capacity, governance, benefits
Reports toHead of the function (for example the CIO)Executive team, COO, CFO or a transformation director
Typical servicesTemplates, schedules, status reports, project supportIntake and prioritisation, portfolio board, enterprise resource view, standards, capability building
Success looks likeProjects in the function delivered wellThe organisation funds the right work and finishes it

Smaller organisations often have one PMO doing both jobs. As the number of projects and departments grows, the strategic role usually separates into an EPMO, with departmental PMOs or Agile PMOs reporting into it.

Core elements of an EPM system

Different sources list the components differently; some, for example, describe seven elements drawn from a PMI paper (risk analysis, structured estimating, project reviews, coaching, escalated issue management, time accounting and an information system). In our experience a working EPM system needs the following nine building blocks.

ElementWhat it means in practice
GovernanceWho approves what, at which thresholds, and how decisions are escalated; see project governance
Standard life cycleCommon phases and stage gates so projects can be compared
Demand intakeOne front door for new ideas and requests, with a standard form and rough sizing
PrioritisationA transparent scoring model and a regular portfolio decision forum
Enterprise resource managementA view of demand and capacity by role and skill; see capacity planning
Standard reportingThe same health indicators and definitions everywhere, rolled up automatically
Risk and issue escalationShared risk scales and escalation routes, including cross-project dependencies
Benefits managementNamed benefit owners and measurement after delivery
Capability and toolsTraining, coaching, templates and one platform that acts as the source of truth

The enterprise project management life cycle

EPM adds a portfolio life cycle around the life cycle of each individual project. The portfolio cycle repeats, usually quarterly, while each project moves through its own phases.

PhaseWhat happensWho decides
1. Strategy and planningStrategic objectives and investment themes set; annual or quarterly budget envelopes agreedExecutive team
2. Demand intakeIdeas and requests captured through a standard form with rough sizingPMO triage
3. Evaluation and prioritisationBusiness cases scored on value, risk, alignment and resource needPortfolio board or investment committee
4. Approval and resourcingSelected projects funded and staffed; capacity checked against other commitmentsPortfolio board, resource managers
5. Delivery and stage gatesProjects run with their chosen method and report through standard dashboards; gates check continued viabilityProject boards and EPMO
6. Portfolio monitoring and rebalancingRegular reviews of health, spend and capacity; projects accelerated, paused or stoppedPortfolio board
7. Closure and benefits trackingProjects closed, benefits measured, lessons fed back into standardsBenefit owners and EPMO

How the portfolio cycle connects to each project

Phases 2 to 4 happen before a project formally starts: they produce the approved project charter and the resources to deliver it. Phase 5 is where the project's own life cycle runs, whether that is a Waterfall plan, Scrum sprints or a hybrid. Stage gates link the two: at each gate the project reports in a standard format and the portfolio board decides whether to continue, change or stop. Phase 7 closes the loop, because benefits are often realised months after the project team has disbanded.

Agile organisations run a similar cycle with lean portfolio practices, funding value streams rather than individual projects.

How to prioritise projects across the enterprise

Prioritisation is the most valuable decision in EPM, and the one most often made by whoever argues loudest. A simple, published scoring model makes it fairer and faster.

  1. Separate mandatory work. Regulatory, legal and safety projects are not optional; ring-fence their capacity first.
  2. Agree four to six criteria linked to strategy, such as strategic alignment, financial value, risk reduction, delivery risk and urgency.
  3. Weight the criteria so they add up to 100%. Get the executive team to agree the weights, not the PMO.
  4. Define each score. Write what a 1, 3 and 5 look like for each criterion so different assessors score consistently.
  5. Score every proposal using the business case. Use financial measures such as net present value or return on investment for the value criterion where you can.
  6. Rank, then check capacity. A ranked list is only a wish list until you compare it with the people available.
  7. Decide and publish. Record what was approved, deferred or rejected and why, then revisit each quarter.

Worked example: scoring five project proposals

Imagine a mid-sized financial services firm with five proposals for next quarter. The portfolio board agreed these weights: strategic alignment 30%, financial value 25%, risk reduction or compliance 20%, delivery risk 15% (5 = low risk) and urgency 10%. Each proposal is scored from 1 to 5.

ProposalAlignment (30%)Value (25%)Risk reduction (20%)Delivery risk (15%)Urgency (10%)Weighted score
E. Customer self-service app551333.70
A. CRM replacement542233.50
B. Regulatory reporting upgrade325453.50 (mandatory)
C. Data platform modernisation443223.30
D. Office relocation system changes231442.55

The weighted score is the sum of each score multiplied by its weight. For proposal E: (5 × 0.30) + (5 × 0.25) + (1 × 0.20) + (3 × 0.15) + (3 × 0.10) = 1.50 + 1.25 + 0.20 + 0.45 + 0.30 = 3.70. Proposal B scores lower on value but is mandatory, so it goes to the top of the queue regardless.

Worked example: checking capacity before you approve

The scarcest skill for these projects is data engineering. The team has 12 people, but about 30% of their time goes to business-as-usual support, leaving roughly 8.4 full-time equivalents (FTE) for projects next quarter.

OrderProposalData engineering FTE neededCumulative FTEFits within 8.4 FTE?
1B. Regulatory reporting (mandatory)2.02.0Yes
2E. Customer self-service app3.55.5Yes
3A. CRM replacement2.58.0Yes
4C. Data platform modernisation4.012.0No: defer or re-sequence

The board approves B, E and A, and defers C to the following quarter, or approves only its discovery phase, which needs far fewer people. Without this check, all four would have started, each would have been understaffed and all four would probably have slipped. Starting fewer projects usually finishes more. For more on balancing demand, see our guide to resource allocation.

Tip

Do the capacity check by role or skill, not by headcount. An organisation can have plenty of people overall and still be blocked by three architects or one security specialist.

Governance tiers: right-sizing control

One of the fastest ways to make EPM unpopular is to apply the same heavy process to every project. Tiering sets lighter or heavier governance according to size and risk. The thresholds below are illustrative; set your own based on your budget authority levels and risk appetite.

TierTypical criteriaApprovalRequired documentsReporting
Tier 3: smallLow cost, under three months, one department, low riskDepartment headOne-page brief, entry in the portfolio trackerMonthly status field update
Tier 2: mediumModerate cost or cross-department, some dependenciesPortfolio boardCharter, light business case, RAID log, scheduleFortnightly standard status report
Tier 1: majorHigh cost, strategic, regulatory or high riskInvestment committee or executive teamFull business case, plan, stage gates, benefits plan, independent assuranceFortnightly or weekly status, monthly board review

Write the tier criteria down and let the EPMO assign the tier at intake. Allow projects to move tiers if their risk or size changes.

What does an enterprise project manager do?

"Enterprise project manager" is used in two ways. Sometimes it means a senior project manager who runs large, cross-functional projects under enterprise standards. More often it describes a portfolio or EPMO role. Typical responsibilities include:

  • Running intake, prioritisation and the portfolio board cycle.
  • Maintaining the portfolio view: health, spend, milestones, risks and dependencies.
  • Working with resource managers to match demand with capacity.
  • Setting and improving standards, templates and tool configuration.
  • Coaching project managers and sponsors.
  • Reporting to executives in plain language, with clear decisions needed.

The role needs strong stakeholder management, financial literacy and the confidence to recommend stopping a project.

Methodologies and frameworks that fit

There is no single EPM method. Most organisations combine a governance framework with project-level methods suited to each type of work.

ApproachBest forNotes
Stage-gate governanceEnterprise-wide control of investmentStandard gates with defined criteria across all projects
PMBOK Guide or PRINCE2 as the base methodConsistent project-level practiceTailor to project size; avoid forcing heavy process on small projects
Portfolio management standardsPortfolio selection and balancingPMI publishes The Standard for Portfolio Management (currently the fourth edition); AXELOS publishes Management of Portfolios (MoP)
SAFe or other scaled AgileLarge technology and product organisationsLean portfolio management with funding by value stream
Hybrid tieringOrganisations with mixed project typesTier projects by size and risk, and let each team keep a delivery method that suits its work

Our advice: standardise what the portfolio needs to compare (gates, health definitions, a small set of mandatory fields) and leave teams free on how they deliver. An Agile team and a construction project can both report progress, forecast, risks and benefits in the same format.

How to implement enterprise project management step by step

  1. Secure an executive sponsor. EPM changes who decides what gets funded. Without a senior sponsor it becomes a reporting exercise.
  2. Build a portfolio inventory. List every active project with its owner, budget, stage, end date and health. Many organisations find far more projects than they expected.
  3. Define a minimal standard. Agree a simple life cycle, health definitions (what red, amber and green mean) and five to ten mandatory fields.
  4. Open one front door. Introduce a single intake form for new work and route all requests through it.
  5. Set up the portfolio forum. Hold a regular portfolio board with a published scoring model and decision log.
  6. Choose one platform. Move the inventory, intake and status reporting into one tool so reports are generated, not collected.
  7. Add resource management. Start with the scarcest roles, then widen.
  8. Introduce governance tiers and stage gates. Apply them to new projects first rather than retrofitting everything.
  9. Track benefits. Assign benefit owners and review benefits after closure.
  10. Review and improve. Each quarter, remove steps that do not change decisions and add what is missing.

A 12-month EPM roadmap

PeriodFocusOutputs
Months 0–3VisibilitySponsor and charter for the EPMO, portfolio inventory, health definitions, intake form, first portfolio board
Months 3–6ControlScoring model, governance tiers, standard status report, one platform live for all Tier 1 and 2 projects
Months 6–12OptimisationCapacity planning for key roles, stage gates, benefits tracking, lessons learned library, first annual review of the framework
Tip

Show value early. Within the first quarter, use the inventory to surface duplicate projects or a capacity clash and get a decision made. That earns the EPMO credibility for the harder changes.

KPIs for enterprise project management

Pick a handful of portfolio measures and define them precisely so every project reports the same way.

KPIHow to calculate itWhat it tells you
Strategic alignmentSpend on projects linked to a strategic objective ÷ total project spendWhether investment follows strategy
Portfolio healthShare of projects rated green, amber and redWhere executive attention is needed
Benefits realisationBenefits realised ÷ benefits planned, for closed projectsWhether projects deliver value, not just outputs
Capacity loadDemand for a role ÷ available capacity for that roleOver-allocation before it causes delays
Schedule and cost performanceAggregate earned value indices (SPI and CPI) or milestone and budget varianceDelivery control across the portfolio
Time to decisionDays from intake submission to approval or rejectionWhether governance is slowing the organisation down
Projects stopped at a gateProjects cancelled or re-scoped at gates when no longer viableA healthy sign that governance works

Avoid measuring the EPMO by the number of templates completed. Measure the decisions it enables and the value the portfolio delivers.

Benefits of enterprise project management

  • Better investment decisions. Projects are compared on the same criteria, so money flows to the work that matters most.
  • Realistic commitments. Capacity checks stop the organisation starting more than it can finish.
  • Earlier warning. Standard health reporting surfaces failing projects while there is still time to act.
  • Visible dependencies. Cross-project risks and handovers are tracked rather than discovered late.
  • Consistent delivery. Shared methods, templates and coaching raise the baseline for every project manager.
  • Proven value. Benefits tracking shows what the portfolio actually achieved.

Enterprise project management software

EPM depends on a single source of truth. Spreadsheet portfolios rarely scale beyond a few dozen projects, because data goes stale and reports have to be assembled by hand. When you compare tools, look for:

  • Portfolio and programme hierarchies with roll-up reporting and dashboards.
  • Enterprise resource management with capacity and demand views by role.
  • Configurable intake forms, approval workflows and stage gates.
  • Support for both Agile boards and Gantt-based plans in the same system.
  • Custom fields and item types so every project reports the same data.
  • Enterprise security: single sign-on, granular permissions, audit logs and data residency options.
  • Integrations with finance, HR, IT service management and development tools.

Wrike, our top-ranked tool, is one of the strongest all-in-one options for EPM. It combines cross-project portfolio views, workload and resource planning, request forms, approvals, custom item types and enterprise security in one platform; advanced resource planning, budgeting and BI sit on its higher, quote-based tiers. Smartsheet suits organisations that prefer spreadsheet-style portfolio planning, and Jira is a natural fit for the software teams within an enterprise portfolio.

Frequently asked questions

What is enterprise project management?

Enterprise project management (EPM) is managing all of an organisation's projects as one coordinated system, with shared governance, standard methods, common tools, enterprise resource management and a single portfolio view.

What is the difference between EPM and project portfolio management?

Portfolio management focuses on selecting, prioritising and balancing projects. EPM is broader: it includes portfolio decisions and adds the standards, tools, resource management, governance and capability building used across every project.

What is the difference between a PMO and an EPMO?

A PMO usually supports delivery in one department and is mainly tactical. An enterprise PMO works across the whole organisation, reports to the executive team and focuses on strategy alignment, prioritisation, capacity and governance.

What does an enterprise project manager do?

They run intake and prioritisation, maintain the portfolio view, balance demand with capacity, set standards, coach project managers and report to executives with clear decisions needed.

What are the elements of enterprise project management?

Governance, a standard life cycle, demand intake, prioritisation, enterprise resource management, standard reporting, risk and issue escalation, benefits management, and capability and tools.

What are the phases of enterprise project management?

Strategy and planning, demand intake, evaluation and prioritisation, approval and resourcing, delivery with stage gates, portfolio monitoring and rebalancing, and closure with benefits tracking.

How do you implement enterprise project management?

Secure an executive sponsor, build a portfolio inventory, agree minimal standards, open one intake route, start a portfolio board, move to one platform, then add resource management, tiered governance and benefits tracking in stages.

What is an example of enterprise project management?

A firm scores all project proposals against agreed weighted criteria, checks the result against the capacity of its scarcest skills, approves only what it can staff and tracks every approved project through the same gates and dashboards.

Which methodology is used in enterprise project management?

Usually a stage-gate governance framework with PMBOK or PRINCE2 practice at project level, portfolio standards such as PMI's Standard for Portfolio Management, and scaled Agile approaches such as SAFe for product teams.

How does Agile fit into enterprise project management?

Agile teams keep their delivery methods while the portfolio funds products or value streams and tracks outcomes, forecasts and risks in a common format.

What KPIs are used in enterprise project management?

Strategic alignment of spend, portfolio health, benefits realisation, capacity load by role, schedule and cost performance, time to decision and projects stopped at gates.

What is enterprise project management software?

Software that provides portfolio views, resource and capacity management, intake and approval workflows and enterprise reporting across all projects. Wrike is a strong all-in-one example.

Is enterprise project management only for large companies?

The full model suits larger organisations, but the core ideas (one project list, one intake route, a scoring model and a capacity check) help any business running several projects at once. See small business project management.
