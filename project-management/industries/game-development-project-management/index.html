Game Development Project Management: A Producer's Guide

Game development project management is the planning, scheduling, budgeting and coordination of a game from the first pitch through pre-production, production, testing, platform certification and launch to live operations. It combines software delivery with creative production, and its defining challenge is that the fun has to be found through prototyping and playtesting rather than written down in advance. This guide covers phases and milestones, producer roles, Agile in studios, a worked schedule and budget, scope control, crunch prevention, KPIs and tools.

Key takeaways

  • Game projects run on a hybrid model: a milestone plan agreed with the publisher or funder on the outside, Scrum for feature teams and Kanban for content pipelines and bug fixing on the inside.
  • Write down exit criteria for every milestone (first playable, vertical slice, alpha, beta, gold) so everyone agrees what 'done' means before the date arrives.
  • Use the vertical slice to measure the real cost of content, then rebuild the production schedule from those numbers rather than from the pitch.
  • Keep a visible, prioritised cut list from day one; cutting early is the main defence against feature creep and crunch.
  • Plan QA, localisation and platform certification as scheduled work with their own lead times, not as a final step.
  • In most studios the project manager is called a producer; the job is to protect the team's ability to make a good game on time and within budget.

What is game development project management?

Game development project management is the discipline of planning, resourcing, scheduling and controlling the work needed to make a video game, from concept to launch and post-launch updates. It coordinates design, engineering, art, animation, audio, writing, QA and marketing so that a playable, fun and stable game ships on the agreed platforms, by the agreed date and within budget.

In most studios the person doing this work is called a producer rather than a project manager. The principles are the same as general project management: define scope, plan, track, manage risk and communicate. What changes is the type of uncertainty, the number of disciplines and the external gates set by publishers and platform holders.

General software projectGame development project
What defines successWorking features that meet requirementsA game players find fun, stable and worth buying or playing
RequirementsMostly known after discoveryDiscovered through prototypes and playtests; some ideas are thrown away
Work typesMainly code, design and testingCode, design, art, animation, audio, writing, cinematics, localisation and QA
Volume of contentModerateOften thousands of assets moving through repeatable pipelines
External gatesClient sign-off, release approvalsPublisher milestones, funding payments, platform certification and store review
Release dateOften flexibleUsually tied to marketing campaigns, events and seasonal windows
After releaseMaintenancePatches, live events, seasons and downloadable content

Two features shape everything else: the fun has to be found through prototypes and playtests, and the content has to be produced at scale through pipelines whose throughput, not headcount, limits what you can make. If you already manage software, our guide to software project management covers the engineering side in more depth; this guide focuses on what is specific to games.

The phases of game development

Studios use slightly different names, but most games move through the same phases. Smaller games compress them; large productions add formal gates between each.

PhaseGoalMain activitiesKey outputs
ConceptDecide whether the idea is worth fundingPitch, target audience and platforms, business model, competitor review, rough scope and budgetPitch deck, concept art, high-level budget
Pre-productionFind the fun and prove the pipelinePrototypes, art style, technical foundations, tools, pipeline set-up, vertical slice, production planFirst playable, vertical slice, game design document, production schedule
ProductionBuild the game at scaleFeature development in sprints, content creation through pipelines, regular playtests and buildsFeature-complete and content-complete builds
AlphaMake the game playable end to endFinish remaining features, fill content gaps, fix major issues, start full test passesAlpha build
BetaPolish and stabiliseBug fixing, balancing, performance, optimisation, localisation testing, external playtestsBeta build, playtest data
Release candidate and certificationPass platform and store requirementsCompliance testing, submission builds, fixes for failures, launch day patch planningRelease candidate, gold master or approved release build
Launch and live operationsSupport players and extend the gamePatches, events, seasons, new content, community management and analyticsUpdate roadmap, live metrics

Mapped to the standard project management phases, concept is initiation, pre-production is planning with real experimentation, production to beta is execution with heavy monitoring, and launch is closure for the development project and the start of an operational phase.

Tip

Do not shorten pre-production to protect the launch date. Problems found in pre-production cost days; the same problems found in production cost months, because by then content has been built on top of them.

Game development milestones explained

Milestones are the backbone of a game schedule and, for many studios, the basis of publisher payments. Definitions vary between studios and publishers, so the table below shows common usage. The most useful habit is to agree written exit criteria for each one.

MilestoneWhat it meansTypical exit criteria to agree
PrototypeRough, often throwaway build that tests one mechanic or ideaQuestion answered (for example 'is traversal fun?'), decision recorded
First playableCore loop playable with placeholder artCore loop can be played for a set time; key controls and camera working
Vertical sliceShort section of the game at near-final qualityRepresentative gameplay, art, audio and UI at target quality; cost per unit of content measured

BetaContent complete; focus on polish and stabilityAll content in; no known critical bugs blocking progress; performance targets close to met

Gold or release buildApproved build for releasePlatform and store approval received; build archived

A vertical slice deserves special attention. It proves three things at once: that the creative vision works, that the pipeline can produce content at final quality, and how long that content really takes. Treat it as a measurement exercise as well as a showcase, and rebuild your production estimates from what it tells you.

Tip

Write each milestone definition into the project plan and, where there is one, the publisher agreement. Include features, content counts, bug thresholds by severity and performance targets. Ambiguous milestone definitions are a common cause of disputes and late payments.

Who manages a game project: producers and project managers

Game studios borrow their job titles from film. The producer usually owns delivery; the creative director owns the vision. Large studios add layers, while small teams combine roles.

RoleMain responsibilities
Executive producerOverall responsibility for the game or portfolio, budget, studio and publisher relationships
ProducerPlans and runs production: schedule, priorities, risks, dependencies, communication and milestone delivery
Associate or assistant producerSupports the producer, often for one discipline or feature team; runs boards, meetings and tracking
Line producer or development managerFocuses on schedule, staffing and cost across the project
Game director or creative directorOwns the creative vision and the player experience; makes final design calls
Discipline leads (design, engineering, art, animation, audio, QA)Estimate and review work in their area, set quality standards and mentor the team
QA testers and QA leadPlan test passes, find and verify bugs, check compliance requirements
External or publisher producerRepresents the publisher, reviews milestones, coordinates marketing, localisation and platform relations

Game producer vs project manager

In most studios they are the same job under different names. Where both exist, the producer usually owns scope and priority trade-offs with the creative director, while project managers run detailed scheduling, tracking and coordination for a team or discipline. Some studios also use the title development manager for this second role. The skills overlap with any project manager's role: planning, risk, communication and negotiation, plus a working understanding of how games are built.

Tip

A good producer protects the team's time. That means shielding developers from unplanned requests, making trade-offs visible early and saying no to scope that the schedule cannot hold.

Agile, Scrum and Kanban in game development

Most studios run a hybrid. The outer plan is milestone-based because funding, marketing and platform gates are fixed. Inside each milestone, teams use iterative methods so they can respond to what playtests reveal.

ApproachWhere it fits in a game projectWhy
Milestone or stage-gate planThe whole project; publisher and funding gatesGives funders and marketing predictable checkpoints
Rapid prototypingConcept and pre-productionCheap experiments to find the fun before investing in content
ScrumFeature and gameplay teams during productionShort sprints with a playable build reviewed at each sprint review
KanbanArt, animation and audio pipelines; bug fixing in beta; live opsWork arrives continuously in many small items; WIP limits keep flow steady

How sprints map to milestones

A milestone of eight to twelve weeks typically contains four to six two-week sprints. Each sprint should end with a playable build and a sprint goal that contributes to the milestone's exit criteria. Use Agile release planning to lay out which features and content each sprint targets, then adjust after every review. A shared definition of done for features (for example 'in the build, tested, no critical bugs, audio hooked up') stops half-finished work piling up.

For the basics of the methods themselves, see what is Scrum and our guide to Kanban boards.

How to manage a game project step by step

The process below works for an indie game or a large production; scale the documentation and the number of gates to the size of the project.

  1. Agree the vision and constraints. Write a one-page pitch: player fantasy, genre, platforms, audience, business model, target release window and budget ceiling. Get sign-off from the funder or studio leadership.
  2. Time-box prototyping. Give the team a fixed number of weeks to test the riskiest mechanics. End each prototype with a decision: keep, change or drop.
  3. Set up the foundations. Choose the engine, version control for large binary assets, build pipeline, bug tracker and task tool. Define naming conventions and folder structures before content production starts.
  4. Build the vertical slice and measure it. Track how long each asset type and each level or mission took to reach final quality. These numbers become your production rates.
  5. Create the production plan. Break the game into features and content (a work breakdown structure works well), estimate using vertical slice data, and build a milestone schedule with dependencies between disciplines.
  6. Prioritise and set up the cut list. Rank every feature and content block by importance to the player experience. Mark what you would cut first if time runs short and agree the list with the creative director.
  7. Plan capacity by discipline. Compare required effort per discipline with available people per month. Fill gaps with hiring, outsourcing or scope changes before production starts. See capacity planning.
  8. Run production in sprints and pipelines. Feature teams work in sprints; content flows through Kanban boards with WIP limits. Hold regular playtests and review a playable build every sprint.
  9. Track milestones and risks weekly. Compare completed features and content against plan, update the risk register and escalate trade-offs early.
  10. Lock features at alpha and content at beta. After each lock, changes go through formal change control with an impact assessment.
  11. Plan certification and launch. Book submission slots, run internal compliance passes, prepare store pages and age ratings, and plan a day-one patch if needed.
  12. Run a post-mortem and hand over to live ops. Capture lessons learned, archive builds and source, and set the post-launch update roadmap.

Worked example: planning an 18-month game

This example shows how a small studio might plan a single-player action game for PC and two consoles, with a publisher funding development against milestones. The figures are illustrative: replace them with your own rates and estimates.

Step 1: lay out the phases and team

PhaseMonthsAverage team sizePerson-months
Concept133
Pre-production (to vertical slice)4624
Production (to alpha)914126
Alpha to beta and release candidate31442
Certification and launch11010
Total18205

Step 2: turn effort into a budget

Assume a fully loaded cost (salary, taxes, benefits, equipment and overheads) of £6,500 per person per month. Staff cost is 205 × £6,500 = £1,332,500. Add outsourced art and audio of £150,000, software licences and tools of £40,000 and external QA and localisation testing of £60,000, giving a subtotal of £1,582,500. A 15% contingency adds £237,375, for a total budget of £1,819,875.

The monthly burn rate in production is 14 × £6,500 = £91,000 in staff costs alone. Tracking burn rate against plan each month is one of the simplest ways to spot trouble: if the team is growing faster than planned or a phase is running long, the burn rate tells you before the budget does. Our guide to project budgets explains contingency and cost baselines in more detail.

Step 3: check content throughput against the schedule

The vertical slice included one level, which took a level team of four people five weeks, or 20 person-weeks. The game needs 12 more levels. Teams usually get faster after the first level, so the producer assumes 20% fewer hours per level: 16 person-weeks each, or 192 person-weeks in total.

Production lasts about 39 weeks. A level team of five gives 195 person-weeks, which only just covers 192. There is almost no buffer, so the producer agrees three options with the creative director in advance: outsource set dressing for four levels, merge two smaller levels into one, or move one level to post-launch content. These sit at the top of the cut list.

Tip

Leave a buffer between your internal release candidate date and the date marketing has announced. Certification failures and resubmissions are common, and you need room to fix and resubmit without moving launch.

Scope control: cut lists and feature creep

Feature creep is the steady addition of 'small' ideas that together overwhelm the schedule. In games it is especially tempting because new ideas often do make the game better. The answer is not to forbid ideas, but to make every addition visible and paid for.

Use MoSCoW to rank features and content

PriorityMeaning in a gameExample
Must haveThe game does not work or is not the pitched game without itCore combat, save system, first ten levels
Should haveImportant to quality; painful but possible to cutPhoto mode, two optional boss fights
Could haveNice additions if time allowsExtra cosmetic outfits, bonus challenge mode

Run the cut list like a budget

  • Keep the cut list in the same tool as the backlog, ordered from first to cut to last to cut.
  • Review it at every milestone and whenever a new feature is proposed.
  • When something new is added, ask what moves down or out to make room.
  • Cut early and cleanly: removing a feature before content is built for it is far cheaper than cutting after.
  • Record cuts and their reasons so the team understands what happened and can bring ideas back for post-launch updates.

How to prevent crunch

Crunch is a prolonged period of excessive overtime to hit a deadline. It harms people's health, increases mistakes and bugs, and often leads to staff leaving after launch. Most crunch is caused by planning decisions made months earlier, so prevention is a project management responsibility.

  1. Measure before you commit. Build production schedules from vertical slice data, not from the pitch.
  2. Plan to around 80% capacity. Leave room for meetings, illness, reviews and the unexpected, and put holidays in the schedule.
  3. Keep the cut list live. Make scope the first lever you pull, not hours.
  4. Protect lock dates. Feature lock at alpha and content lock at beta stop late additions creating late bugs.
  5. Watch leading indicators. Rising bug counts, falling sprint completion, growing WIP and overtime warn you weeks ahead.
  6. Buffer external dates and escalate early. Tell funders about schedule risk while there are still choices.
Tip

If the team is regularly working evenings and weekends, the plan is wrong. Re-plan scope and dates with the creative director and funder instead of asking for more effort.

Content pipelines and asset tracking

A pipeline is the sequence of steps each asset type goes through. Visualising pipelines on Kanban boards shows where work is waiting, which is usually where schedules slip.

Asset typeTypical pipeline stepsCommon bottleneck
CharacterConcept, approval, model, texture, rig, animation, implementation, reviewRigging and animation, which depend on approved models
Environment or levelLayout and greybox, playtest, art pass, lighting, set dressing, performance pass, reviewPlaytest feedback forcing layout changes after the art pass
Audio and dialogueSpec or script, creation or recording, implementation, mix, reviewWaiting for final animation timing or late script changes
  • Set WIP limits per step so work flows instead of piling up in front of busy specialists.
  • Measure throughput (assets finished per week) per pipeline, not just per person.
  • Find the constraint and plan around it, as described in the theory of constraints: if rigging is the bottleneck, adding concept artists only makes the queue longer.
  • Approve concepts and greyboxes before expensive final art starts, so late feedback does not discard finished work.

QA, bug management and certification

Testing in games is continuous, but it peaks between alpha and launch. Agree severity levels early and use them to define milestone bug thresholds.

SeverityTypical definitionExample
Critical (or A)Crash, data loss, progression blocker or certification failureGame crashes when loading chapter 3
Major (or B)Serious issue with a workaround or that badly affects experienceQuest marker points to the wrong place
Minor (or C)Noticeable but does not affect playTexture flicker on a distant building
TrivialCosmetic or very rareTypo in credits

Plan certification as a project in its own right

Console platform holders publish technical requirements that games must meet before release: PlayStation uses its Technical Requirements Checklist (TRC), Xbox uses the Xbox Requirements (XRs) and Nintendo runs Lotcheck. The documents are confidential and available to registered developers, so read them early, assign an owner and build compliance checks into regular test passes. PC and mobile stores have their own review processes too.

  • Book submission dates with the platform or publisher well ahead of launch.
  • Run a full internal compliance pass before the release candidate.
  • Allow time for at least one failure and resubmission.
  • Track age ratings, store assets and legal text as tasks with owners.

Live operations and post-launch

For games with ongoing updates, launch marks a change of operating model. Work becomes a continuous stream of patches, events and new content, so most live teams switch from milestone plans to a regular release cadence with Kanban for fixes and sprints for larger content drops.

  • Set a predictable update rhythm (for example a hotfix lane, monthly patches and seasonal content) and publish a player-facing roadmap only for what is committed.
  • Separate urgent fixes from planned content with swimlanes so incidents do not silently consume the roadmap.
  • Use player data, such as retention, to decide what to build next, and keep paying down technical debt left from launch.

Common mistakes in game production

  • Rushing pre-production. Production starts before the fun or the pipeline is proven, so content is rebuilt later.
  • Scheduling from the pitch. Replace early guesses with vertical slice data.
  • Vague milestone definitions. 'Alpha' means different things to different people unless it is written down.
  • Treating QA as the last phase. Late bugs are slower to fix and eat into certification time.
  • Ignoring cross-discipline dependencies. Animation waiting on rigs, or audio on final timing, creates hidden queues.
  • Underestimating localisation and certification. Both have lead times that cannot be compressed at the end.

Software for game development project management

Studios combine development tools with a production management tool. A typical stack includes:

  • Game engine and build pipeline, with automated builds so a playable version is always available.
  • Version control that handles large binary files, such as art and audio, alongside code.
  • A bug tracker with severity, platform and build fields.
  • A production management tool for backlogs, sprints, milestone timelines, pipeline boards and dashboards across disciplines.
  • Review and approval tools for concept art, cinematics, trailers and store assets.
  • Documentation space for the GDD, pipelines and onboarding.

Many engineering-led studios use Jira for backlogs, sprints and bug tracking. Wrike, the top tool in our rankings, suits studios and publishers that want milestone timelines, Kanban pipeline boards, workload views and proofing for art, video and marketing assets in one platform, which helps when development, marketing and localisation need to share one plan.

Frequently asked questions

What is game development project management?

It is the planning, scheduling, budgeting and coordination of a game from concept and pre-production through production, testing, certification and launch to live operations. It combines software delivery with creative content production and relies on playtesting to find what is fun.

What are the phases of game development?

The usual phases are concept, pre-production, production, alpha, beta, release candidate and certification, and launch with live operations. Studios name and split them differently, so agree definitions for your own project.

What is the difference between a game producer and a project manager?

In most studios they are the same job. Where both exist, the producer owns scope, priorities and trade-offs with the creative director, while project managers or development managers run detailed scheduling, tracking and coordination for a team or discipline.

What is a vertical slice in game development?

A short, playable section of the game at near-final quality in gameplay, art, audio and UI. It proves the vision and the pipeline and shows how long content really takes, so it is used to rebuild production estimates and often to secure funding.

What is the difference between alpha and beta in game development?

Alpha usually means feature complete and playable from start to finish, possibly with placeholder content. Beta usually means content complete, with the focus on bug fixing, polish, balancing and performance. Definitions vary, so write them down with your publisher.

Is Scrum used in game development?

Yes. Many studios run feature teams in Scrum sprints during production, with a playable build at each sprint review. They usually combine it with a milestone plan for publisher gates and Kanban for art pipelines, bug fixing and live ops.

How do you avoid crunch in game development?

Schedule from vertical slice data, plan to around 80% capacity, keep a live cut list, protect feature and content lock dates, buffer external dates and escalate schedule risk early. Treat regular overtime as a sign the plan needs to change.

What is a cut list?

A prioritised list of features and content that the team has agreed to remove first if time or money runs short. Keeping it visible from the start makes scope cuts a planned decision rather than a late crisis.

What is a game design document?

A GDD is a living document describing the game's vision, mechanics, systems, content and technical constraints. Modern teams keep it short and update it as playtests change the design, often in a wiki linked to the backlog.

How long does it take to make a game?

It depends on scope, team size and platforms. Small games can take months, while large productions often take several years. The reliable way to estimate is to measure content costs in a vertical slice and build the schedule from those rates.

How do you budget a game?

Estimate person-months per phase and multiply by a fully loaded monthly cost, then add outsourcing, licences, QA, localisation, marketing assets and a contingency. Track monthly burn rate against plan to catch overruns early.

What software do game studios use for project management?

Studios combine an engine, version control for large files, a bug tracker and a production tool. Jira is common in engineering-led studios; Wrike suits studios and publishers that want timelines, pipeline boards and asset approvals in one place. Compare tools in our rankings.
