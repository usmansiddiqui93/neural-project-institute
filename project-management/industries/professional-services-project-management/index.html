Project Management by Industry guide · Professional services

Professional Services Project Management: A Practical Guide

Professional services project management is the planning, staffing, delivery and commercial control of client engagements in firms that sell expertise, such as consultancies, IT services firms, agencies and accountants. It covers everything internal project management does, plus contracts, billable time, utilisation and margin, because every engagement must satisfy the client and make money for the firm. This guide walks through the engagement life cycle, pricing models, the core numbers with worked examples, roles, KPIs and software.

Key takeaways

  • Professional services project management combines delivery (scope, time, quality) with commercial control (utilisation, realisation and margin).
  • The pricing model decides who carries cost risk: fixed price puts it on the firm, time and materials puts most of it on the client.
  • Track three numbers on every engagement: hours against budget, earned progress against hours spent, and forecast margin.
  • Scope changes are commercial events: log them, size them and price them before doing the work.
  • Resource management across all engagements, not just within one, is where services firms win or lose profit.
  • Use a work management platform for delivery and add PSA or finance software when billing and revenue reporting get complex.

What is professional services project management?

Professional services project management is the discipline of delivering client engagements on time, to scope and to the agreed quality while protecting the firm's margin. It applies standard project management methods to work sold under a contract, where people's time is both the main cost and the product, and where billing depends on delivery.

The category includes management and IT consultancies, software implementation partners, agencies, accounting and law firms, engineering and architecture practices, and the services arms of software vendors. The delivery challenge is the same in each: turn a statement of work into results the client accepts, with the hours you priced.

Why it is different from internal project work

The core toolkit is the same, but six features of services work change what a project manager must watch.

People are the product

In most services firms staff costs are by far the largest cost line. An hour that cannot be billed is margin lost for good, so staffing, time tracking and avoiding idle (bench) time matter as much as the schedule.

Contracts set the rules

A master services agreement (MSA) usually sets the legal terms for the relationship, and each statement of work defines the scope, deliverables, assumptions, price and acceptance criteria for one engagement. If something is not in the SOW, it is not in scope, however reasonable the request sounds.

The pricing model decides who carries risk

Under time and materials the client pays for the hours used, so overruns cost the client. Under fixed price the firm absorbs overruns, so estimating and change control decide profit. The next sections cover this in detail.

Revenue follows delivery

Invoices are raised from approved timesheets or accepted milestones, and finance teams recognise revenue under accounting standards such as IFRS 15 or ASC 606. Late timesheets or a delayed client sign-off delay cash, so the project manager affects the firm's cash flow directly.

Many engagements at once

Consultants often split their week across two or three clients. A delay on one engagement ripples into others, which is why services firms need a firm-wide view of resource allocation.

The relationship is a deliverable

Services firms grow through repeat work and referrals, so responsiveness and no surprises on the invoice matter as much as the outputs.

Stages 1 and 2 are often run by sales or partners, but they set the constraints delivery will live with, so a structured sales-to-delivery hand-over is worth an hour of everyone's time.

Pricing models and what each means for the project manager

The commercial model changes where risk sits and therefore what you must control most tightly. Many engagements mix models, for example a fixed-price discovery phase followed by time and materials delivery.

Choosing a pricing model

  • Choose fixed price when the scope is well understood, you have delivered similar work before and you have historical actuals to estimate from.
  • Choose T&M when the scope is uncertain, the client wants to direct the work week by week, or discovery is still needed.
  • Choose a phased approach when uncertainty is high at the start: a short fixed-price discovery produces the detail you need to price the main phase with confidence.
  • Choose a retainer for ongoing advisory, support or managed services where demand is steady but individual requests are small.

The core numbers: utilisation, realisation and margin

Three measures connect a consultant's week to the firm's profit. Definitions vary between firms (for example, whether training counts as available time), so agree yours and apply them consistently. The formulas below are common working definitions, not a formal standard.

Billable utilisation

Formula: billable hours ÷ available hours × 100. Available hours are contracted hours minus holidays, leave and public holidays.

Example: a consultant has 1,760 available hours in a year and logs 1,320 billable hours. Utilisation is 1,320 ÷ 1,760 = 75%. The other 440 hours went on internal meetings, sales support, training and bench time.

Realisation rate

Formula: amount billed ÷ standard value of hours worked × 100, where standard value is hours worked multiplied by the standard rate.

Example: the team works 400 hours on an engagement at a standard rate of £150 per hour, so the standard value is £60,000. After writing off 40 hours of rework the firm decided not to charge, it bills £54,000. Realisation is 54,000 ÷ 60,000 = 90%.

Project margin

Formula: (revenue − delivery cost) ÷ revenue × 100, where delivery cost is hours worked multiplied by a fully loaded cost rate (salary, benefits and overheads) plus any direct expenses.

Example: the same 400 hours at a loaded cost rate of £85 per hour cost £34,000. Revenue is £54,000, so the margin is £20,000, or 20,000 ÷ 54,000 = 37%.

StepQuestion it answersWhere value leaks
Available hoursHow much time do we have?Unplanned absence, too much internal work
Billable hours (utilisation)How much time went on client work?Bench time, slow staffing, non-billable admin
Billed value (realisation)How much of that time did we charge?Discounts, write-offs, overruns on fixed price
Collected cashHow much did we actually receive?Late invoices, disputes, slow payment
MarginWhat did we keep?Wrong staffing mix, underpricing, scope creep

Utilisation is a firm and resourcing measure; realisation and margin are the measures a project manager influences most directly. A team can be fully utilised and still lose money if the hours go on unpriced scope.

Worked example: a fixed-price engagement health check

An IT services firm sells a fixed-price CRM implementation for £120,000. The estimate is 600 hours of delivery plus 60 hours of contingency (660 hours), at a loaded cost rate of £110 per hour, so the planned cost is £72,600 and the planned margin is 39.5%. The engagement is planned over 14 weeks.

At week 8 the project manager runs the weekly numbers. The team has logged 420 hours. Progress, measured against the baseline work breakdown, is 50% of the in-scope work, which is worth 300 baseline hours. That gives an overall cost performance index of 300 ÷ 420 = 0.71, which looks alarming.

Breaking down the timesheets shows why: 90 of the 420 hours went on integrating a second data source that the client asked for in a workshop. It is not in the SOW. On the in-scope work alone, 300 baseline hours were earned for 330 hours spent, a CPI of 0.91. Using the standard estimate at completion formula, the in-scope work will take about 600 ÷ 0.91 = 660 hours, using all the contingency. The extra integration needs another 60 hours, 150 hours in total.

PlanAt week 8Forecast, absorbing the extra workForecast, with an approved change request
Revenue£120,000£120,000£142,500
Hours660 (budget)420 (actual)810810
Cost at £110 per hour£72,600£46,200£89,100£89,100
Margin39.5%25.8%37.5%

The project manager raises a change request for the integration: 150 hours at the £150 standard rate, £22,500. The client sponsor approves it because the integration has clear value to them. Without the change request, the engagement would have finished at a margin of about 26%; with it, the margin recovers to about 37.5%.

What the project manager does next

  1. Stops further work on the integration until the change request is approved, and tells the client sponsor why on the same day.
  2. Re-plans the remaining in-scope work, since all contingency is now forecast to be used.
  3. Investigates the in-scope CPI of 0.91: slower client approvals were adding rework, so a two-day turnaround for reviews is agreed with the sponsor.
  4. Records the lesson for estimating: workshops with this client type generate add-on requests, so future SOWs should state how new requests will be handled.
  5. Reports the revised forecast and margin to the delivery director in the weekly portfolio review.

How to manage a professional services project step by step

Use these steps for any engagement, scaling the documents to its size and risk.

  1. Review the deal before signature. Check the estimate against historical actuals, test the assumptions and confirm the right people are available. Use effort estimation techniques rather than top-down guesses.
  2. Hold a sales-to-delivery hand-over. Walk through the SOW, the client's priorities, promises made during the sale and known sensitivities.
  3. Build the baseline. Turn the SOW deliverables into a work breakdown, schedule and hours budget per role, with contingency held separately.
  4. Staff the engagement. Request named people through resource management, matching skills and seniority to the pricing assumptions.
  5. Kick off internally, then with the client. Agree governance, status reporting, decision makers, review turnaround times and how changes will be handled.
  6. Track time daily and progress weekly. Daily time entry keeps billing accurate; weekly progress against the baseline shows whether hours are buying progress.
  7. Report honestly. Send a short status report at the agreed cadence: progress, budget, risks, decisions needed and open change requests.
  8. Control scope. Log every new request, size it and agree whether it is in scope, a change request or a later phase.
  9. Secure acceptance. Get written sign-off against the SOW criteria at each milestone, and raise invoices promptly.
  10. Close well. Reconcile hours and invoices, release the team, run a lessons learned review and discuss follow-on needs with the client.

Tip

Report bad news early and with a plan. Clients forgive overruns they were warned about far more readily than surprises on an invoice.

Managing scope and change requests with clients

Scope creep is the most common reason fixed-price engagements lose money. Requests usually arrive informally in workshops, emails or calls, and each one feels too small to raise. A simple, consistent process protects both the margin and the relationship.

  1. Log every request in a change log as soon as it is heard, with who asked and when.
  2. Check it against the SOW. Is it covered by a deliverable or an assumption? If yes, it is in scope and gets planned.
  3. Size it. Estimate hours by role, schedule impact and any effect on other deliverables.
  4. Price it using the contract's rates or change mechanism.
  5. Get written approval from the client sponsor before starting work. Use a change request template so every request looks the same.
  6. Update the baseline: plan, hours budget, revenue forecast and the SOW or contract amendment.
  7. Report it: show approved, pending and rejected changes in every status report.

This is formal change control applied to a commercial contract. A useful habit is to agree a small threshold (for example, requests under a few hours) that the team can absorb as goodwill, logged but not charged. That keeps the relationship flexible while making the cumulative cost visible.

Resource management across engagements

In a services firm, resourcing is a portfolio problem. The right person on the wrong engagement, or a strong consultant sitting idle for two weeks, costs more than most project-level overruns.

Match demand to capacity

Demand comes from signed engagements (hard bookings) and from the sales pipeline (soft or tentative bookings, often weighted by probability of winning). Capacity is available hours by role and skill. A rolling four to twelve week view shows gaps before they become problems. Our guide to capacity planning explains the method.

In this illustrative example the firm can move some senior consultant work to consultants, use the spare consultant time for pre-sales or training, and bring forward hiring or a contractor for solution architecture if the pipeline converts.

Practical rules

  • Keep one resource plan for the whole firm, not one per project manager.
  • Book named people only when work is signed; use role-based placeholders for the pipeline.
  • Use a skills matrix to find people with the right skills, not just the people who are free.
  • Review the plan weekly with sales so staffing and selling stay connected.

Many firms also run a project management office (PMO) to standardise methods, templates, health checks and portfolio reporting. A RACI matrix for each engagement makes the split between the firm and the client explicit, which is especially useful for approvals and acceptance.

What does a professional services project manager do?

A professional services project manager turns a signed SOW into a delivered, accepted and invoiced engagement. Typical responsibilities include building the plan and hours budget, securing the right people, running client governance, tracking time and progress, managing risks and change requests, forecasting margin, securing acceptance and closing the engagement. Compared with an internal project manager, they spend more time on commercial judgement and client relationships.

Useful skills combine core project management skills with commercial awareness: reading a contract, understanding cost rates and margin, negotiating change, and having difficult conversations with clients without damaging trust.

Essential documents and templates

Most engagements need the same core set of documents. Keep them light on small engagements and complete on large or fixed-price ones.

  • Project proposal and cost estimate: solution, approach, effort by role and price.
  • Statement of work: deliverables, assumptions, exclusions, acceptance criteria, timeline and price.
  • Project charter: internal kick-off summary of objectives, team, budget and risks.
  • Project schedule: milestones, dependencies and client review dates.
  • Budget: hours and cost by role, with contingency held separately.
  • RAID log: risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies, including client dependencies.
  • Change request: description, impact, price and approval.
  • Project tracker: tasks, owners and status for the delivery team.
  • Lessons learned: estimating and delivery lessons to feed into the next proposal.

Common challenges and mistakes

  • Underestimated deals. Estimate from historical actuals, involve delivery before signature and price uncertainty explicitly.
  • Optimistic progress reporting. Measure progress by completed deliverables, not hours spent or percentage guesses.
  • Late or inaccurate timesheets. Ask for daily time entry and show the team how it links to billing and forecasting.
  • Vague acceptance criteria. Write measurable criteria into the SOW and secure sign-off milestone by milestone.
  • No feedback loop to sales. Share lessons learned so the next proposal prices the real effort.

Frequently asked questions

What is professional services project management?

It is the planning, staffing, delivery and commercial control of client engagements in firms that sell expertise, such as consultancies, IT services firms, agencies, accountants and law firms. It combines standard project management with contracts, billable time, utilisation and margin.

What does a professional services project manager do?

They turn a signed statement of work into a delivered, accepted and invoiced engagement: building the plan and hours budget, securing staff, running client governance, tracking time and progress, managing risks and change requests, forecasting margin and closing the engagement.

What is PSA software?

Professional services automation (PSA) software manages the business side of delivery: resource planning, time and expenses, rate cards, utilisation, project accounting, billing and profitability reporting.

Do I need PSA software or a project management tool?

Most firms start with a work management platform for planning and delivery. Add PSA or finance software when billing, revenue forecasting and firm-wide resourcing outgrow spreadsheets. Many firms connect the two.

How do you calculate billable utilisation?

Divide billable hours by available hours and multiply by 100. For example, 1,320 billable hours out of 1,760 available hours is 75% utilisation. Agree what counts as available time and apply it consistently.

What is a good utilisation rate for professional services?

There is no universal figure. Targets depend on role and firm: delivery consultants are usually expected to be far more billable than managers, who also sell and lead. Set targets by role and watch the trend rather than a single benchmark.

What is the realisation rate?

Realisation is the amount billed divided by the standard value of the hours worked. If 400 hours at £150 are worth £60,000 and the firm bills £54,000, realisation is 90%. Low realisation points to discounting, write-offs or fixed-price overruns.

Which methodology suits professional services?

Phased delivery suits fixed-price implementations, Agile suits software work sold as team capacity, hybrid suits system implementations, and Kanban suits retainers and managed services.

Is fixed price or time and materials better?

Neither is always better. Fixed price suits well-understood scope and rewards accurate estimating; time and materials suits uncertain scope and puts more cost risk on the client. A short fixed-price discovery followed by a priced main phase is a common compromise.

How do you stop scope creep with clients?

Log every request, check it against the SOW, size and price anything out of scope, and get written approval before starting. Report open and approved changes in every status update. See our scope creep guide.
