Research Project Management: Steps, Roles and Example

Research project management is the planning and control of research work, such as a funded grant, a multi-partner consortium or a corporate R&D programme, so that it meets funder rules, budgets and deadlines while leaving room for results nobody can predict. This guide covers how research projects differ, who does what, the life cycle, a 12-step process and a worked grant example with milestones, budget and a mid-project review.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 27 August 2026
  • Last updated 4 November 2026
  • 19 min read

Key takeaways

  • Research project management controls time, money, compliance and reporting around work whose results are uncertain; it does not try to plan the findings.
  • The principal investigator leads the science and is accountable to the funder; a research project manager runs the plan, budget, partners, risks and reports.
  • Structure grants as work packages with deliverables and milestones, and write milestones as decisions or completed activities, never as expected results.
  • Ethics approval, recruitment, seasonal or academic-year windows and equipment lead times drive most research schedules, so plan them first.
  • Track spend against the funder's profile every month. Underspend is as risky as overspend because unspent grant money is often lost.
  • Use work packages for grants, stage-gates and TRLs for corporate R&D, and light Agile or Kanban for day-to-day lab and computational work.

What is research project management?

Research project management is the discipline of planning, coordinating and controlling a research project, including its funding, budget, people, partners, ethics and data obligations, milestones and reporting, so that the research can be carried out on the funder's terms and on time, even though its outcome is uncertain. It manages the conditions for good research, not the research question itself.

In a university the work is shared. The principal investigator (PI) leads the science, the research office helps with funding and costing, post-award finance looks after the grant account and, on larger grants, a research project manager runs the plan day to day. In corporate R&D, a project or programme manager does the same job with commercial gates in place of funder reports.

The tools are familiar, such as a Gantt chart, risk register and budget, but each needs adapting: milestones that survive negative results, budgets that follow funder rules and schedules built around approvals and seasons.

Types of research projects

TypeTypical exampleWhat dominates the management work
Single-investigator grantA three-year study funded by a research council or foundationRecruiting staff, spend profile, annual reports, publications
Consortium grantSeveral universities and companies in a multi-country programmePartner coordination, governance, IP, joint reporting
Clinical and health researchA trial or cohort study involving patientsEthics and regulatory approval, recruitment, data protection

How research projects differ from other projects

Most project management assumes you know what you are building. Research starts with a question, so some usual assumptions do not hold.

AspectTypical business projectTypical research project
OutcomeDefined deliverable, such as a system or buildingKnowledge whose content is unknown in advance; negative results are valid
FundingInternal budget approved by a sponsorExternal grant with a fixed period, approved budget and rules on eligible costs
Schedule driversBusiness deadlines and dependenciesEthics approval, recruitment, seasons, academic year, equipment lead times, reporting dates
ApprovalsInternal sign-offsEthics committees or institutional review boards (IRBs), regulators, data protection
TeamStaff allocated from departmentsAcademics with several commitments, fixed-term staff, students, external partners
Success measureBenefits delivered on time and budgetMilestones met, outputs published and shared, compliance maintained, impact achieved

The answer is not to drop project management but to apply it with a light touch: plan the predictable parts (approvals, recruitment, procurement, reporting) tightly, and plan the uncertain science in short stages with decision points.

Principal investigator vs research project manager

The most common source of confusion on research projects is who decides what. The PI is accountable to the funder for the research and its budget; the research project manager runs the machinery of the project on the PI's behalf. On small grants the PI does both jobs.

RoleMain responsibilities
Principal investigator (PI)Scientific lead; accountable to the funder for the research, budget and conduct of the project
Co-investigators and partner leadsLead work packages or a partner's contribution; report progress and spend
Research project managerMaintains the plan; tracks milestones, deliverables, spend and risks; coordinates partners; prepares reports; runs change control
Research office (pre-award)Finds calls, checks eligibility, costs and submits the proposal
Post-award financeRuns the grant account, checks eligible costs, produces financial statements, handles audits
Ethics committee or IRBApproves research involving people, personal data or other sensitive areas before it starts
Data stewardWrites and implements the data management plan

Record who decides what in a RACI matrix at kick-off, especially for decisions that cross partners, such as moving budget between work packages. Our RACI matrix template is a quick start.

The research project life cycle

Research administrators describe the grant life cycle in three stages: pre-award (finding funding and preparing the proposal), award (contracting and set-up) and post-award (running, reporting and closing the funded project). Expanded into phases:

PhaseWhat happensKey output
1. Idea and funding searchResearch question defined; suitable calls identified; eligibility checkedConcept note
2. Proposal development (pre-award)Work packages, milestones, budget, partners, impact and data management plan written and costedSubmitted proposal
3. Award and set-upGrant and collaboration agreements signed, accounts opened, staff recruited, ethics approval obtainedKick-off and baselined plan
4. Research execution (post-award)Experiments, fieldwork and analysis; partner meetings; progress, spend and risk trackingDeliverables and milestone reports
5. Reporting and disseminationInterim and final reports, publications, conferences, data depositReports, papers, data sets
6. Close-outFinal financial statement, data archiving, staff contracts ended or extendedClosure report
7. Impact and follow-onExploitation, technology transfer, policy engagement, follow-on fundingImpact case, next proposal

Phases 4 and 5 overlap, because most funders expect interim reports and outputs throughout. See our guide to project closure for the last phase.

How to manage a research project step by step

The steps follow a funded project from call to close-out. On a small grant one person may do them all; on a consortium each is a task in the management work package.

  1. Read the call and funder rules first. Note the maximum budget, eligible costs, required documents, reporting dates and rules on changes and extensions.
  2. Define the research question and objectives. Write two to four objectives that can be checked at the end, whatever the findings.
  3. Break the work into work packages. Four to eight work packages, each with a lead, months, tasks and deliverables, including management and dissemination.
  4. Find the fixed dates and long lead times. Ethics review, recruitment, equipment delivery, fieldwork seasons and academic terms go on the timeline first. Then find the critical path.
  5. Set milestones as decisions. Add go/no-go points where the next stage depends on a result, and write each milestone so it can be met whatever the data show.
  6. Cost the project with the research office. Build the budget by work package and funder cost category.
  7. Write the risk register, data management plan and ethics plan. Cover technical, recruitment, staffing, partner, data and regulatory risks, each with an owner.
  8. Agree governance and IP before the start. Sign the consortium agreement and set decision, dispute and publication rules.
  9. Kick off and baseline the plan. Confirm the plan and spend profile with every partner in the first month.
  10. Monitor monthly. Review milestones, spend against profile, recruitment and risks in a short status note.
  11. Report on a calendar. Put every report, financial statement, deliverable and conference deadline in one calendar with owners.
  12. Close out and archive. Submit final reports, deposit data, close the account and record lessons for the next proposal.

Worked example: a three-partner research grant

This example is illustrative: the project, partners and figures are invented. It is a 36-month grant to develop low-cost air quality sensors and measure pollution exposure in 20 primary schools. University A leads and runs engineering and fieldwork, University C does the statistics and modelling, and Company B, a small sensor maker, contributes staff time in kind.

Work packages and deliverables

WP1 ManagementUniversity A1–36D1.1 Project handbook (M2); annual and final reports
WP2 Sensor developmentUniversity A, with Company B1–9D2.1 Sensor design and calibration report (M8)
WP3 School deployment and data collectionUniversity A4–30D3.1 Deployment report (M12); D3.2 Data collection report (M30)
WP4 Analysis and modellingUniversity C10–34D4.1 Interim analysis (M18); D4.2 Exposure model report (M33)
WP5 Data and disseminationUniversity C1–36D5.1 Data management plan (M6); D5.2 Open data set (M34); D5.3 Guidance for schools (M35)

Milestones and go/no-go points

MilestoneMonthVerified by
MS1 Consortium agreement signed; ethics application submitted3Signed agreement; submission receipt
MS2 Lab calibration complete; go/no-go on in-house sensor or commercial fallback6Calibration report and steering group decision
MS3 Sensors installed and transmitting in 20 schools10Deployment log
MS4 First winter data quality-checked; decision on year 2 sensor placement16Data quality report
MS5 Exposure model validated, or alternative approach agreed28Validation report
MS6 Final data set deposited in a public repository34Repository record

MS2 and MS5 can be met whatever the results. If the in-house sensor fails calibration, the decision is still made and the project continues with the fallback.

Budget

Some funders pay a share of the full economic cost (fEC) of research. UK Research and Innovation (UKRI), for example, normally pays 80% of fEC and the host institution covers the rest (EPSRC guidance updated 12 March 2026). The illustrative budget uses that model:

Cost categoryWhat it coversFull economic cost
StaffPI time, two postdocs for 30 months, technician at 50%£620,000
Equipment60 sensor units and a calibration chamber£90,000
Consumables and fieldworkParts, mobile data, school visits£60,000
Travel and meetingsPartner meetings, advisory board, two conferences£45,000
Other direct costsOpen access fees, data storage, translation£35,000
Indirect and estates costsOverheads under the funder's method£400,000
Total fEC£1,250,000
Funder contribution at 80%£1,250,000 × 0.8£1,000,000
Institutions' contribution£1,250,000 − £1,000,000£250,000

Direct costs (everything except indirect and estates) total £850,000. That is the figure the project manager tracks monthly by partner and work package, using the same lines as the funder's budget.

Month 9 review: ethics delay and underspend

MeasureCalculationResult
Time elapsed9 ÷ 36 months25%
Planned direct spend to dateFrom the baselined spend profile£215,000
Actual direct spend to dateFrom the grant account£148,000
Spend against profile£148,000 ÷ £215,00069%

The first postdoc started in month 4 instead of month 1, and ethics review took 14 weeks instead of the 8 assumed, so school recruitment started late. The bigger problem is the schedule: MS3 is now forecast for month 13, but the study needs a full winter heating season, which in this example runs from month 10 to month 15. Installing in month 13 would lose half of the first winter.

OptionEffectTrade-off
A. Install in the 10 schools already recruited by month 11; the rest before the second winterOne full winter for half the sample, two winters for the restSmaller first-year data set; interim analysis uses 10 schools
B. Request a 6-month no-cost extension and study the second and third wintersFull sample in both wintersNeeds funder approval; extension staff costs must come from underspend

The decision and what changed

The steering group chooses option A and keeps B as a fallback. The project manager then:

  • Splits MS3 into MS3a (10 schools, month 11) and MS3b (20 schools, month 22) and updates the Gantt chart.
  • Re-forecasts the spend profile so the staff underspend is visible and planned for.
  • Raises a change request and informs the funder's programme officer, because a reported milestone has moved. Check whether your funder needs notification or prior approval.
  • Adds a risk for second-winter school recruitment, owned by the WP3 lead, with earlier outreach as the response.
  • Records the lesson: assume at least 14 weeks for ethics review here, and recruit staff before the start date where the funder allows.
Tip

Keep your tracker's budget lines, partner split and work packages identical to the funder's. Every report then comes from filtering one sheet, and variances show up in the month they happen.

Writing milestones when results are uncertain

A milestone that depends on a particular result is missed whenever the science goes a different way, so the project looks late when it is not. Write milestones as completed activities or decisions.

Weak milestone (depends on the result)Better milestone (verifiable whatever happens)
Sensor shown to be accurateLab calibration complete; go/no-go on sensor design
New catalyst discovered200 candidate compounds screened; top 10 selected or criteria revised
Intervention reduces symptomsMain analysis complete and reported to the steering group
Paper published in a leading journalManuscript submitted (publication timing is outside your control)
Participants recruited150 participants consented, or recruitment plan revised at the month 12 review
  • Give every milestone a month, an owner and a means of verification.
  • Put a go/no-go decision before any expensive stage, such as a main trial after a pilot.
  • Agree the fallback in advance so a 'no' does not stall the project.
  • Keep milestones few: five to ten across a three-year grant is usually enough.

Stage-gates and TRLs in corporate R&D

In company R&D the question at each stage is commercial: is the next stage worth paying for? In a stage-gate process, a review board decides at each gate to continue, change course or stop.

Technology readiness levels (TRLs) describe maturity on a scale from 1 to 9. The scale was developed at NASA and is now used by many funders, including the European Commission, which gives gate reviews a shared language.

TRL bandMeaningTypical gate question
TRL 1–3Principles observed, concept formulated, proof of concept shownIs the idea feasible enough to fund lab validation?
TRL 4–5Validated in the lab, then in a relevant environmentDoes performance justify a prototype?
TRL 6–7Prototype demonstrated in a relevant, then operational, environmentIs there a credible product and customer case?
TRL 8–9System complete and qualified, then proven in operationHand over to product development or manufacturing?

Stopping a weak idea at TRL 4 frees money for stronger ones, so record why each project stopped. See our guides to engineering project management and pharmaceutical project management for stage-gates in regulated product development.

Grant budgets and funder rules

Grant money comes with conditions, and breaking them can mean repaying costs. Finance staff are the experts, but the project manager needs to spot problems early.

  • Eligible costs. Funders list what they will pay for, often with special rules for equipment, overheads and partners. For example, UKRI funds equipment at 80% of fEC and raised its capital equipment threshold from £10,000 to £25,000 from 1 April 2025.
  • Spend profile. Compare actual spend with the expected profile monthly, by category and partner.
  • Budget changes. Moving money between categories or partners often needs approval above a threshold. Ask before you spend.
  • Underspend. Money not spent by the end date is usually lost. Late recruitment is the most common cause.
  • No-cost extensions. Many funders allow extra time without extra money. Under US National Institutes of Health (NIH) rules, grantees can usually make one extension of up to 12 months, subject to conditions; further extensions need prior approval.

Ethics approval and research data management

Ethics and regulatory approval

Research involving people, personal data, human tissue, animals or hazardous materials needs approval from an ethics committee, IRB or regulator before that part of the work starts. Review times vary, so treat approval as a critical-path task with its own buffer, and remember that later changes usually need an approved amendment.

Data management plans

A data management plan (DMP) explains how research data will be collected, documented, stored, shared and preserved. Many funders require one:

  • Horizon Europe requires a DMP for projects that generate or reuse research data, with the full plan due by month 6 and updates during longer projects.
  • NIH has required a Data Management and Sharing Plan for applicable research since 25 January 2023.
  • Other funders and universities have their own rules, so check the call and your institution's policy.

A DMP is not a project plan: it covers the data, while the project plan covers who does what and when. Turn DMP actions, such as anonymisation and repository deposit, into scheduled tasks with owners.

Managing research teams and consortia

Research teams rarely share a line manager. Academics balance teaching and other grants, postdocs are often on fixed-term contracts and partners answer to their own organisations, so management works through agreement and transparency.

  • Governance. A steering group with one representative per partner, a meeting rhythm and clear decision rules.
  • Consortium agreement. Ownership and use of results, background IP, publication review periods, confidentiality and disputes, agreed before work starts.
  • Rhythm. A monthly work package leads call, quarterly steering group and annual all-hands meeting is a common pattern.
  • Turnover. Document methods, code and data as you go so work continues when someone leaves.

PIs often run several grants at once, each with its own reporting calendar. A portfolio view of milestones helps; see how to manage multiple projects.

Methodologies that suit research

Most teams combine a structured outer plan, which the funder sees, with a lighter way of organising daily work.

ApproachBest forNotes
Work packages and milestonesFunded grants and consortiaThe standard structure for many funders, including EU framework programmes
Stage-gateCorporate R&DGo/kill decisions control investment in uncertain work
Agile or KanbanLab, software and computational teamsShort cycles and visual boards suit exploratory work; see what Kanban is
WaterfallClinical trials, facility builds, procurementRegulated or sequential work needs a fixed plan

A practical hybrid: keep the work package plan for the funder, and run each work package with a simple board and a fortnightly check-in.

Essential documents and templates

  • Proposal or charter: research question, objectives, scope and partners.
  • Work packages and work breakdown: tasks and deliverables for each package.
  • Timeline: the grant Gantt chart with milestones and reporting dates; start from our Gantt chart template.
  • Budget and spend profile: by partner, work package and funder category.
  • Risk register template: technical, recruitment, ethics, partner, staffing and data risks.
  • Data management plan and ethics approvals: with renewal and amendment dates tracked.
  • Consortium agreement: IP, publication and governance rules.
  • Reporting calendar and lessons learned: every deadline in one place, and what to do differently next time.

Skills, qualifications and career path

Research project managers are either researchers who move into management or project professionals who move into research. Both need planning, budgeting, clear writing and enough grasp of the science to ask good questions.

  • Research administration credentials. In the US, the Research Administrators Certification Council (RACC) offers the Certified Research Administrator (CRA), Certified Pre-Award Research Administrator (CPRA) and Certified Financial Research Administrator (CFRA).
  • PMP certification. Requires three, four or five years of project experience in the last ten years depending on education (two with a GAC-accredited degree), plus 35 contact hours or the CAPM. From 1 December 2026, live training must come from a PMI Authorized Training Partner.
  • PMAI. Our free PMAI certification covers project management foundations, including planning, risk and stakeholders.

Health research has its own rules; see healthcare project management. For foundation and charity grants, see nonprofit project management.

Frequently asked questions

What is research project management?

It is the planning, coordination and control of research work, including funding, budgets, ethics approvals, partners, milestones, deliverables and reporting, while allowing for results that cannot be predicted.

What does a research project manager do?

They support the principal investigator by maintaining the plan, tracking milestones, spend and risks, coordinating partners, running change control and preparing funder reports.

What is the difference between a principal investigator and a research project manager?

The PI leads the science and is accountable to the funder. The research project manager runs the plan, budget tracking, partners and reporting. On small grants the PI often does both.

What are the phases of a research project?

Idea and funding search, proposal development, award and set-up, research execution, reporting and dissemination, close-out, and impact and follow-on, grouped by research offices as pre-award, award and post-award.

What is the difference between pre-award and post-award?

Pre-award covers finding funding and costing, writing and submitting the proposal. Post-award covers running the funded project: spending, compliance, changes, reporting and close-out.

How do you write milestones when research results are uncertain?

Write them as completed activities or decisions, such as 'pilot analysed; go/no-go on main study', not expected findings. Give each a month, owner and means of verification.

What is a work package in a research grant?

A group of related tasks with a lead, start and end months and deliverables. Grants usually have four to eight, including management and dissemination.

Can you use Agile for research projects?

Yes, for day-to-day work. Most teams keep a work package plan with milestones for the funder and run each work package with short cycles and a visual board.

Is a data management plan the same as a project plan?

No. A DMP covers how data are collected, stored, shared and preserved; a project plan covers tasks, owners, dates, budget and risks. Put DMP actions into the project plan.

What is a no-cost extension?

Extra time on a grant without extra money. Rules vary: the NIH generally allows one extension of up to 12 months, with further extensions needing prior approval.

What qualifications does a research project manager need?

There is no single requirement. RACC credentials (CRA, CPRA, CFRA) are recognised in the US, and the PMP and CAPM are valued everywhere. Research experience helps.

How should a PhD student manage their research project?

Work back from the submission date, split the thesis into studies or chapters with milestones, allow time for ethics and data collection, and keep a simple board or Gantt chart up to date.
