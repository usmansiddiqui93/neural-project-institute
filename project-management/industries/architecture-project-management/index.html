Architecture Project Management: Stages, Fees and Tools

Architecture project management is how an architectural practice plans, resources and controls a design commission, from the client brief through the design stages, approvals and construction-phase services, so that the design is delivered well and the practice earns a fair return on its fee. It means running two projects at once: the client's building and the practice's own time and money. This guide covers the stages, a step-by-step process, worked fee and resourcing examples, roles, KPIs and tools.

Key takeaways

  • Architecture project management serves two goals: delivering the client's design and protecting the practice's fee.
  • Structure the programme, fee and sign-offs around the RIBA Plan of Work or AIA phases.
  • Turn the fee into hour budgets by stage and check hours against progress every week, not at invoice time.
  • Treat every client change after sign-off as a potential additional service, and agree it in writing before doing the work.
  • Approvals such as planning and, for higher-risk buildings in England, Gateway 2 belong on the critical path.
  • Use BIM tools for design and a work management platform such as Wrike for programmes, resources and approvals.

What is architecture project management?

Architecture project management is the planning, coordination and control of an architectural commission. It covers the design programme, the client's decisions and approvals, coordination with consultants and the contractor, and the practice's fee, hours and resources, so that the building is designed to quality and the commission is profitable.

It differs from construction project management, which controls the building works, and from the client-side project management of the whole development. The architect's project manager looks after the design team's part of the project and the practice's commercial position within it.

The client's projectThe practice's project
ObjectiveA well-designed building that meets the brief, budget and programmeDeliver the appointment to quality within the agreed fee
BudgetConstruction cost and the client's wider budgetThe fee, converted into hours and costs by stage
Key decisionsBrief, design options, stage sign-offs, approvalsStaffing, hours, additional services, risk and invoicing
Typical measuresDesign quality, construction cost, approvals obtained, programmeFee earned vs fee spent, utilisation, profit by stage

In small studios the project architect usually does all of this. Larger practices add dedicated project managers, practice managers or resourcing leads. If your work is mainly fit-out, see our guide to interior design project management.

What makes architecture projects different

  • The fee is the budget. Practices are paid a fixed fee, a percentage of construction cost or hourly rates, and every hour spent beyond the fee comes out of profit.
  • Design is iterative. Clients need to see options and respond, so work loops back before it moves forward.
  • Approvals shape the programme. Planning or zoning, building regulations or code review, heritage consents and client sign-offs gate each stage, and their timing is often outside your control.
  • Coordination with consultants. Structural, building services and other engineers, cost consultants and specialists must work to a shared information programme.
  • Professional liability. Drawings and specifications carry legal responsibility, so quality checking and document control are essential.
  • Long, uneven timelines. Projects can pause for funding, planning or client decisions, which makes resourcing across several commissions difficult.
Tip

Write your fee proposal stage by stage, and close each stage with a written client sign-off. It is the single most effective way to make extra work visible and chargeable when the client changes direction.

Architecture project stages: RIBA and AIA

Practices in the UK usually work to the RIBA Plan of Work 2020, which organises a building project into eight stages from 0 to 7. In the US, the AIA's standard owner–architect agreement (B101–2017) defines five phases of basic services. The table maps them roughly; they are not exact equivalents.

RIBA stageRoughly equivalent AIA phaseMain architect activitiesTypical sign-off
0 Strategic DefinitionPre-design (often an additional service)Help define the client's need and whether a building is the answerClient confirms the business case and requirements
1 Preparation and BriefingPre-design / programmingDevelop the brief, site appraisal, feasibility, appointment and feeProject brief agreed
2 Concept DesignSchematic designDesign options, massing, concept proposals, early cost adviceConcept design approved
3 Spatial CoordinationDesign developmentCoordinated design with engineers; planning application in the UKCoordinated design approved; planning submitted
4 Technical DesignConstruction documentsDetailed drawings and specifications for building and tenderTechnical design and tender package issued
5 Manufacturing and ConstructionProcurement and constructionAnswer queries, review submittals, inspect, certify where appointedPractical completion
6 HandoverConstruction phase closeoutSnagging, as-built information, defects periodEnd of defects period
7 UsePost-occupancy (often an additional service)Post-occupancy evaluation and feedbackLessons captured

Stage boundaries and approvals

According to RIBA guidance, Stages 0 to 4 generally run in sequence, while Stages 4 and 5 overlap on most projects because the contractor or specialists complete parts of the technical design. RIBA also says planning applications are generally submitted at the end of Stage 3, and recommends earlier submission only when enough information exists and the client is experienced enough to manage the risk. Building planning periods into the programme at the right point, with time for pre-application discussions and conditions, is one of the project manager's main jobs.

Higher-risk buildings in England: Gateway 2

Under the Building Safety Act 2022, higher-risk buildings in England (broadly, residential and some other buildings at least 18 metres or seven storeys tall) must pass building control approval from the Building Safety Regulator, known as Gateway 2, before construction starts. The statutory period is 12 weeks for new buildings and 8 weeks for work to existing ones, but reported waits in 2025 were far longer. The regulator became a standalone body sponsored by the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government in January 2026 and has set a target of deciding non-complex Gateway 2 applications within 18 weeks by March 2027. Because applications need construction-ready design, usually RIBA Stage 4, plan Gateway 2 as a critical-path activity and check current guidance when you programme the work.

AIA phases in the US

AIA B101–2017 lists five phases of basic services: schematic design, design development, construction documents, procurement and construction. Pre-design programming and post-occupancy work are usually added as additional services. The AIA does not prescribe how the fee should be split across phases, so each practice sets its own split from its project history.

How to manage an architecture project step by step

The steps below run from first enquiry to post-occupancy. They apply whether you are a five-person studio or a large practice; only the formality changes.

  1. Qualify the enquiry. Check the client, the budget, the site and your capacity before you spend time on a proposal.
  2. Agree the brief. Capture the client's requirements, budget, programme and constraints in a project brief.
  3. Define your scope of services. List exactly which stages and services are included and which are not (surveys, interiors, Stage 7, extra planning submissions).
  4. Build the fee from the bottom up. Estimate hours by stage and role, apply cost rates, add risk allowance and profit, then compare with what the market or the client expects.
  5. Sign the appointment. Make sure the scope, fee, payment stages and process for additional services are written into the appointment before work begins.
  6. Set up the project. Create stage budgets in hours, the design programme, a RACI matrix for the design team and a risk register.
  7. Agree an information programme with consultants. Set dates for each consultant's information so coordination reviews happen on time.
  8. Run each stage to a sign-off. Present options, record client decisions and close the stage with written approval of the stage report.
  9. Track hours and progress weekly. Compare hours spent with the stage budget and with the deliverables actually complete (see the examples below).
  10. Control change. Log every client change, assess its effect on fee and programme, and agree any additional service before doing the work using a change request.
  11. Manage approvals. Plan pre-application meetings, submissions and determination periods, and track conditions.
  12. Support construction. Respond to queries and submittals within agreed turnaround times and record site visits and instructions.
  13. Close out and learn. Agree final fees, archive the project and hold a lessons learned review that feeds actual hours back into future fee proposals.

Fees, hours and resourcing: worked examples

The fee is the practice's budget, so fee control is the heart of architecture project management. The examples below use illustrative figures for a mid-sized commission.

Common fee bases

Fee basisHow it worksMain risk for the practice
Percentage of construction costFee is a percentage of the construction budget or final costFee may not reflect effort if cost falls or design work increases
Lump sum (fixed fee)Fixed fee for a defined scope, often split by stageOverruns come straight out of profit unless changes are charged
Time charge (hourly)Hours charged at agreed rates, sometimes cappedClients may resist open-ended costs; caps turn it into a fixed fee
HybridFixed fee for early stages, time charge for construction-phase servicesNeeds clear boundaries between the parts

Example 1: turn the fee into stage hour budgets

A practice agrees a lump-sum fee of £180,000 for RIBA Stages 1 to 6. It splits the fee by stage using its own project history, targets a 15% profit margin (so 85% of the fee is available for cost) and uses an average cost rate of £60 per hour (salary plus overheads).

StageShare of feeStage feeCost budget (85%)Hour budget at £60/hour
1 Preparation and Briefing5%£9,000£7,650128
2 Concept Design15%£27,000£22,950383
3 Spatial Coordination20%£36,000£30,600510
4 Technical Design30%£54,000£45,900765
5 Manufacturing and Construction25%£45,000£38,250638
6 Handover5%£9,000£7,650128
Total100%£180,000£153,0002,552

The hour budgets are what the team actually manages. Split each one further by role (director, project architect, architect, technologist) so the resourcing plan and timesheets use the same structure.

Example 2: check a stage half-way through

Six weeks into Stage 3, the team has booked 360 hours against the 510-hour budget. Looking at the deliverables (coordinated plans, sections, elevations, the planning package), the project architect judges the stage to be about 50% complete. So the work done is worth 50% × 510 = 255 budgeted hours, but it has taken 360.

MeasureValue
Hour budget for the stage510 hours
Hours spent360 hours
Earned hours (50% × 510)255 hours
Efficiency (earned ÷ spent)0.71
Forecast hours at completion (510 ÷ 0.71)720 hours
Forecast overrun210 hours ≈ £12,600 at £60/hour

At the current rate the stage will cost about £43,200 against a stage fee of £36,000, so it will lose money rather than make £5,400. The causes turn out to be two rounds of layout changes requested by the client and late structural information. The project manager logs the client changes as a claim for additional services, agrees new dates with the engineer and resets the remaining hour budget. This is earned value applied to hours; see our guide to earned value management.

Example 3: check studio resourcing and utilisation

A six-person studio has 6 × 37.5 = 225 hours a week available. The practice sets itself a target of 75% chargeable time, leaving room for practice work, CPD and bids, so it aims for about 169 chargeable hours a week. Planned demand for the next four weeks is:

CommitmentHours per week
Project A (Stage 4)80
Project B (Stage 3, planning deadline)60
Project C (Stage 5, site queries)25
Competition entry (non-chargeable)30
Total demand195

Demand exceeds the target by about 26 hours a week. The options are to move the competition to after the planning submission, agree a revised date for part of Project A's information, bring in a freelance technologist for four weeks, or accept overtime for a short period. Seeing this four weeks ahead, rather than in the week itself, is the point of a resource plan. Our guides to resource allocation and managing multiple projects cover the techniques.

Example 4: price a client change as an additional service

After Stage 3 sign-off, the client asks to add a roof terrace. Before redesigning, the project manager assesses the impact:

ItemImpact
Architect's timeAbout 70 hours to redesign, coordinate and re-issue drawings; at a charge rate of £95/hour, about £6,650
ConsultantsStructural and services engineers each need to review loads, drainage and access; they will quote separately
ApprovalsThe approved planning drawings change, so a planning amendment or new application may be needed
ProgrammeAbout four weeks added to Stage 4 plus any planning determination period
Construction costCost consultant to update the cost plan before the client decides

The practice sends the client a short additional services letter with the fee, the programme effect and the decision needed, and starts work only once it is approved.

Project architect vs project manager

The project architect leads the design and the technical information: what is drawn, how it is detailed and whether it is coordinated. A project manager in a practice leads the programme, the hours, the fee and the client's commercial relationship. In smaller practices one person holds both roles; in larger ones they work as a pair, and the project manager may also cover several projects at once.

Methodologies that fit

ApproachUse in architecture
Stage-gated planFollows the RIBA or AIA stages with sign-off at each, as in waterfall project management
Iterative design cyclesShort cycles of options, review and refinement in concept and developed design; see iterative and incremental development
Kanban for productionA Kanban board to manage drawing production, queries and submittal reviews
Lean and BIM collaborationIntegrated design workflows with consultants and contractors to cut rework

Many practices find a hybrid works best: a fixed stage plan for the client and the fee, with short iterative loops inside each design stage and a simple board for production and construction-phase queries.

Common mistakes in architecture project management

  • Starting work before the appointment is signed. Unsigned scope is the root of most fee disputes.
  • Tracking fees only at invoice time. By then the hours are spent; check weekly.
  • Skipping stage sign-offs. Without them, every later change looks like part of the original service.
  • Not feeding actual hours back into proposals. Fee estimates stay optimistic unless they use real project data.
  • Letting one person hold all project knowledge. Keep decisions, drawings and actions in shared systems so others can step in.

Skills and qualifications

  • Design and technical knowledge, so you can judge how long design and coordination will really take.
  • Commercial skills: fee estimating, appointments, additional services and invoicing.
  • Planning and resourcing across several projects at once.
  • Communication and negotiation with clients, consultants, planners and contractors.
  • Regulatory awareness, including planning, building regulations or codes, and building safety duties.

Professional practice is part of architectural registration in most countries. In the UK, registration with the Architects Registration Board follows a professional practice qualification, often known as Part 3. In the US, the Architect Registration Examination run by NCARB includes Practice Management and Project Management divisions. Many architects add a general project management qualification such as the PMP.

Frequently asked questions

What is architecture project management?

It is how an architectural practice plans, resources and controls a design commission from brief to construction-phase services, delivering the design to quality while protecting its fee.

What does an architectural project manager do?

They run the programme, resourcing, fee tracking, risk, change and client reporting for a commission, working alongside the project architect who leads the design and technical information.

What is the difference between a project architect and a project manager?

The project architect leads the design and its technical coordination. The project manager leads the programme, hours, fee and commercial relationship. In small practices one person often does both.

What are the RIBA Plan of Work stages?

Stage 0 Strategic Definition, 1 Preparation and Briefing, 2 Concept Design, 3 Spatial Coordination, 4 Technical Design, 5 Manufacturing and Construction, 6 Handover and 7 Use.

What are the AIA phases of design?

AIA B101–2017 defines five phases of basic services: schematic design, design development, construction documents, procurement and construction. Pre-design and post-occupancy work are usually additional services.

How should an architect split the fee across stages?

There is no fixed rule; the AIA does not prescribe a split. Use your own project history, giving the largest shares to technical design or construction documents and to construction-phase services, and review the split after each project.

How do architects track fees on a project?

Convert the fee into hour budgets by stage and role, record time weekly, estimate how complete each stage is from its deliverables, and compare earned hours with hours spent to forecast the final position.

What is a good utilisation rate for an architecture practice?

It depends on the practice's size, roles and business model, so set your own target. Leave room for practice management, CPD and bids, and track actual utilisation against that target each week.

How do architects handle scope creep?

By defining the scope of services in the appointment, closing each stage with a sign-off, logging every client change and agreeing additional services in writing before doing the work.

When is a planning application submitted in the RIBA Plan of Work?

RIBA guidance says planning applications are generally submitted at the end of Stage 3 (Spatial Coordination), with earlier submission only when enough information exists and the client can manage the risk.

What is Gateway 2 under the Building Safety Act?

It is the building control approval that higher-risk buildings in England need from the Building Safety Regulator before construction starts. Applications need construction-ready design, so it should be planned as a critical-path activity.

What methodology suits architecture projects?

Most practices use a hybrid: a stage-gated plan based on RIBA or AIA stages for the client and fee, with iterative design cycles inside each stage and a Kanban board for drawing production and queries.

What software do architects use for project management?

BIM and CAD tools for design, a common data environment for sharing information, practice management software for timesheets and invoicing, and a work management platform such as Wrike for programmes, resourcing and approvals.
