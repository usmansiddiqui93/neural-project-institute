Event Project Management: Phases, Timeline, Budget and Tools

Event project management is the planning and delivery of conferences, trade shows, product launches, festivals and corporate events as projects, with a fixed date, a budget, a target audience and measurable goals. Its defining feature is the immovable deadline: the event happens on the day whether or not everything is ready, so planning backwards, controlling suppliers and managing risk matter far more than late recovery. This guide walks through the life cycle, a step-by-step process, a worked timeline and budget, the run of show, roles, risk, KPIs and tools.

Key takeaways

  • Event project management treats an event as a project with a fixed date, budget, audience and success measures.
  • Plan backwards from the event date and identify the critical path early: venue, programme lock, print and shipping deadlines.
  • Read venue and catering contracts closely: attrition, minimum spend and cancellation clauses are among the biggest financial risks.
  • Track registrations weekly against a target curve so you can act while there is still time.
  • Run the day from a minute-by-minute run of show with a clear chain of command, then close with a budget reconciliation and lessons learned.

What is event project management?

Event project management is the application of project management methods to plan, coordinate and deliver an event. It covers objectives, scope, schedule, budget, suppliers, marketing, risk and on-the-day operations, from the first idea to the post-event review. The event project manager owns the plan and makes sure every workstream is ready by a date that cannot move.

Every event is a project: it is temporary, produces a unique outcome and has a defined start and end. "Event management" is the wider profession, including creative design, hospitality and production. "Event project management" is the planning and control discipline inside it: the schedule, budget, dependencies, risks and decisions that turn an idea into a well-run day.

What makes event projects different

  • A date that cannot move: venues and speakers are booked months ahead, so every slip is absorbed by scope, cost or quality rather than time.
  • Heavy supplier dependence: venue, catering, AV, staging, security, transport and accommodation are mostly outsourced.
  • Live delivery: the event itself is a short, intense operation where decisions are made in minutes.
  • Safety and licensing: crowd management, fire safety, food hygiene, accessibility and local licences all apply.
  • Revenue as well as cost: ticket sales and sponsorship often fund the event, so registrations are a live risk.
  • One chance to get it right: unlike most projects, there is no second release. Rehearsals and contingency plans replace testing in production.

Most events move through seven phases. The timing depends on size, but the order rarely changes.

Tip

Read venue and supplier contracts for attrition and cancellation clauses before you sign. Room-block minimums and catering guarantees are among the biggest financial risks in an event budget.

How to plan an event step by step

  1. Set objectives. Write two to four measurable project objectives, such as "400 attendees, 80% satisfaction and 150 qualified sales leads".
  2. Define the audience and format. In person, virtual or hybrid; one day or several; keynote-led or workshop-led.
  3. Agree a sponsor and decision rights. Name who approves the budget, programme and major changes.
  4. Build an outline budget. Include income (tickets, sponsorship) as well as costs, and hold a contingency line.
  5. Choose the date and venue. Check capacity, accessibility, transport, clashes with other events and contract terms.
  6. Break the work down. Create a work breakdown structure by workstream: venue, programme, marketing, registration, production, catering, sponsorship, staffing and safety.
  7. Plan backwards from the event date. Set milestones and supplier deadlines, and identify the critical path.
  8. Contract suppliers. Get comparable quotes, confirm everything in writing and record delivery and arrival times.
  9. Launch marketing and registration. Track sign-ups weekly against a target curve.
  10. Assess risks and plan for safety. Complete the risk assessment and emergency plan, and agree the chain of command.
  11. Finalise logistics. Lock the programme, produce the run of show, brief staff and speakers, and rehearse.
  12. Deliver, then close. Run the event, reconcile the budget, report to sponsors and hold a lessons learned review.

Building a backwards event timeline

Event schedules are planned in reverse. Start with the event date, then work back through each deadline that must be met for it to happen. Supplier lead times, print and shipping dates and the point at which you must give final numbers to the venue are the fixed points.

Worked example: timeline for a 400-person conference

This illustrative plan is for a one-day industry conference with a keynote, breakout sessions, a small exhibition and lunch. "E" is the event date.

WhenMilestoneOwner
E minus 12 to 9 monthsObjectives, outline budget, venue shortlist, site visits, venue contract signedEvent project manager, sponsor
E minus 8 monthsDate announced, save-the-date sent, sponsorship prospectus issuedMarketing lead, sponsorship manager
E minus 6 monthsKeynotes confirmed, AV and production supplier contracted, early-bird registration opensProgramme manager, event project manager
E minus 4 monthsFull agenda published, caterer and menu chosen, hotel room block agreedProgramme manager, logistics coordinator
E minus 3 monthsSponsor deliverables agreed, main marketing campaign runningSponsorship manager, marketing lead
E minus 6 weeksProgramme locked for print and event app; signage and badge artwork approvedProgramme manager
E minus 4 weeksFloor plan, staffing rota, risk assessment and emergency plan completeLogistics coordinator, safety lead
E minus 2 weeksSpeaker briefings, slide deadline, supplier reconfirmationProgramme manager, event project manager
E minus 7 daysFinal numbers to venue and caterer, run of show final, staff briefing pack issuedEvent project manager
E minus 1 dayBuild, AV installation and technical rehearsalProduction supplier
EEvent deliveryWhole team
E plus 1 to 4 weeksAttendee survey, budget reconciliation, sponsor reports, lessons learnedEvent project manager

In this plan the critical path usually runs through the venue contract, keynote confirmation, programme lock, print and badge production, and the final-numbers deadline. Anything on that chain slipping puts the date at risk, so track it weekly. Lay the plan out visually with the Gantt chart template.

Tip

Build at least one week of float before every print and shipping deadline. Late speaker changes almost always arrive after the programme lock.

Event budgeting and contract risk

An event budget has two sides: income and costs. Build both, hold a contingency line and know your break-even point before you commit to large contracts. Our project budget guide covers the general method; the example below shows how it applies to an event.

Worked example: conference budget

The same 400-person conference: 350 paid tickets at £295, 50 complimentary places for speakers, press and staff, and four sponsors at £7,500 each. The figures are illustrative.

LineCalculationAmount
Ticket income350 × £295£103,250
Sponsorship income4 × £7,500£30,000
Total income£133,250
Venue hireDay rate including rooms£18,000
Catering400 × £65 per head£26,000
AV and productionQuote£22,000
Speakers (travel and fees)Estimate£9,000
MarketingCampaign budget£10,000
Registration platform and badgesQuote£4,500
Staffing and securityRota × rates£6,500
Print, signage and brandingQuote£4,000
InsuranceQuote£1,500
Subtotal£101,500
Contingency10% of subtotal£10,150
Total costs£111,650
Forecast surplus£133,250 − £111,650£21,600

Break-even check. Catering is the main cost that varies with paid attendance (£65 a head). The other costs, plus catering for the 50 complimentary places (£3,250), are about £88,900. Each paid ticket contributes £295 − £65 = £230. After sponsorship, you need (£88,900 − £30,000) ÷ £230 ≈ 256.1, so 257 paid tickets to break even. That number becomes your minimum registration target and helps you decide whether to go ahead at each review point.

How much contingency to hold is a judgement call. Many planners use around 10% for a familiar event and more for a first-time event, a new venue or an outdoor site. Approve every draw on contingency through one person and record it; see contingency planning.

Attrition and minimum spend clauses

Venues and hotels often require you to guarantee minimum numbers. An attrition clause lets your numbers fall by an agreed amount without penalty, but charges you for shortfalls beyond it.

  • Catering guarantee: you contract a minimum of 380 covers at £65. If only 300 people attend, you still pay 380 × £65 = £24,700 rather than £19,500, so the shortfall costs £5,200.
  • Hotel room block: you reserve 120 room-nights at £160 with an 80% pick-up requirement, so you must fill 96. If attendees book only 70, you may be charged for 26 room-nights, up to £4,160, depending on the contract terms.
  • Cancellation scale: charges usually rise as the event approaches. Know each date at which the cancellation charge steps up and put those dates in your plan as decision points.

Negotiate realistic minimums, review terms with whoever handles contracts in your organisation, and set your final-numbers date in the schedule. Supplier contracting is covered in more depth in our guide to project procurement management.

The run of show

The run of show (also called a running order or show flow) is the minute-by-minute script for the event. It lists every session, transition, cue and responsible person, and it is the single document everyone works from on the day: stage manager, AV crew, speakers, hosts and venue staff.

A good run of show includes:

  • Start time and duration of every item, including changeovers and walk-ons.
  • Location (main stage, breakout room, foyer).
  • Who is on stage and who is responsible backstage.
  • AV and technical cues: slides, video, microphones, lighting and music.
  • Notes such as speaker arrival times, accessibility needs or sponsor mentions.

Here is a short illustrative extract:

08:0060 minRegistration and coffeeFoyerRegistration leadBackground music; holding slide on screens
09:005 minWelcome from hostMain stageHostWalk-on music; host mic 1; title slide
09:0540 minOpening keynoteMain stageProgramme managerLapel mic 2; speaker slides; clicker check at 08:45
09:4515 minPanel: industry outlookMain stageModeratorFour handheld mics; panel stools set during keynote Q&A

Issue the run of show with version numbers. After the final version is issued, route every change through one person, usually the producer or stage manager, and announce it on the radio channel or team chat.

What does an event project manager do?

  • Turns the event brief into a plan with milestones, budget and owners.
  • Negotiates and manages supplier contracts.
  • Tracks registrations, spend and risks, and reports to the sponsor.
  • Coordinates marketing, programme, logistics and sponsorship workstreams.
  • Leads the operation on the day and makes fast decisions when things change.
  • Closes the project: reconciliation, reports and lessons learned.

The most useful skills are planning, budgeting, negotiation, stakeholder management, risk awareness and calm decision-making under pressure.

Managing suppliers

Most of an event is delivered by other organisations, so supplier management is where many events succeed or fail.

  1. Write a clear specification for each supplier: what, when, where, quantities and standards.
  2. Get at least two comparable quotes for significant spend.
  3. Check credentials: insurance, relevant certifications, references and, for structures, safety documentation.
  4. Contract in writing with delivery times, payment milestones, cancellation terms and named contacts.
  5. Keep a supplier tracker with contact details, arrival and departure times, access needs and status.
  6. Reconfirm everything in the final week and share the run of show with suppliers who need it.
  7. Hold a short supplier briefing on site before doors open.

Agencies that run events for clients will recognise the same pattern; see agency project management.

Event risk, safety and compliance

Record risks in a risk register template from the start and review them at every planning meeting. Typical event risks and responses:

RiskLikelihood and impactResponse
Registrations below break-evenMedium likelihood, high impactWeekly tracking against a curve; extra promotion triggers; go/no-go dates linked to cancellation terms
Keynote speaker cancelsLow to medium, high impactBack-up speaker or format; contract terms on cancellation
AV or power failureLow, high impactTechnical rehearsal, spare equipment, backup laptop with all slides
Supplier no-showLow, high impactWritten confirmation, backup contacts, reconfirmation in the final week
Bad weather (outdoor)Varies, high impactWritten wet-weather plan with a decision deadline
Medical emergency or crowd incidentLow, very high impactFirst aid provision, emergency plan, staff briefing, clear chain of command
OverspendMedium, medium impactContingency line with single approver and weekly budget review

Safety and legal duties depend on where the event takes place, its size and the venue. Agree with the venue who is responsible for what, and take professional advice where needed. Some UK examples of what to check:

  • Health and safety: the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) states that the CDM 2015 regulations apply to construction work in the entertainment industry, including building and dismantling temporary structures for live events.
  • Outdoor events: the Purple Guide is widely used industry guidance for planning outdoor events safely.
  • Security: the Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, known as Martyn's Law, received Royal Assent on 3 April 2025 with an implementation period of at least two years. Once in force, it introduces duties for qualifying premises and for qualifying public events with 800 or more people. Check current government guidance for dates and requirements.
  • Licensing, food hygiene, accessibility and data protection for attendee data also apply; check local authority and venue requirements early.

For the general method, see our guide to project risk management.

Methodologies for events

  • Waterfall and milestones: the backbone of most events, planned backwards from the event date with clear supplier deadlines.
  • Critical path: venue contract, programme confirmation, print and shipping deadlines usually form the path to the event.
  • Kanban: useful for the stream of tasks in the final weeks, from badge printing to signage.
  • Agile sprints: some teams plan marketing and content for large events in short cycles, adjusting to registration data.
  • Incident command on the day: a clear chain of command and radio channels for decisions during the live event.

Essential documents and templates

  • Event plan: start with our event planning template.
  • Budget: income and cost lines with contingency in the budget template.
  • Timeline: a Gantt chart planned back from the event date.
  • Agenda: publish sessions and timings with the meeting agenda template, which also works for workshops and smaller events.
  • Run of show: minute-by-minute timings, cues, speakers and responsibilities for each session.
  • Supplier tracker and contracts: who to call, when they arrive and what they deliver.
  • Risk assessment and emergency plan: plus the project risk register.
  • Responsibilities: a RACI matrix for on-the-day decisions.
  • Debrief: the lessons learned template.

Event project management software

Event teams usually combine two kinds of tool: a project management platform for tasks, timelines, approvals and supplier coordination, and specialist event software for registration, ticketing, badging, attendee apps and floor plans. Look for reusable event templates, timeline or Gantt views, workload views across several events, approvals for creative assets and integrations with your registration platform.

Wrike, our top-ranked tool, is a strong all-in-one choice for corporate event and marketing teams that run several events a year. It offers reusable templates, interactive Gantt charts, request forms, proofing and approvals for collateral, and workload views across events. monday.com is easy to set up and very visual, and Asana works well for cross-team task coordination.

Skills and certifications

Event project managers need supplier negotiation, budgeting, risk and safety awareness, and calm decision-making under pressure.

The Certified Meeting Professional (CMP), awarded by the Events Industry Council, is a widely recognised event industry credential. Eligibility combines industry experience (typically 36 months of full-time work in meetings, events or hospitality, reduced for graduates of relevant degrees) with at least 25 hours of continuing education in meeting management within the past five years, followed by an exam. Check the Events Industry Council's current requirements before you apply.

Frequently asked questions

What is event project management?

It is managing an event as a project with a fixed date, budget, audience and objectives, from concept and venue booking through suppliers, marketing and delivery to evaluation.

What does an event project manager do?

They own the plan and budget, contract and coordinate suppliers, track registrations and risks, lead the team on the day and close the project with a budget reconciliation and lessons learned.

What are the phases of event project management?

Concept and objectives, planning and venue, suppliers and programme, marketing and registration, final logistics, event delivery, and evaluation and close.

What are the steps to plan an event?

Set objectives, define the audience and format, agree a sponsor, build a budget, book the venue, break down the work, plan backwards, contract suppliers, launch registration, assess risks, finalise logistics, then deliver and review.

How far ahead should you start planning an event?

It depends on size and type. Small meetings may need a few weeks, while large conferences often start a year or more ahead because popular venues and speakers book early.

What is a run of show?

A minute-by-minute running order listing every session, transition, technical cue, location and responsible person. It is the single script the whole team works from on the day.

How much contingency should an event budget have?

Many planners hold around 10% for a familiar event and more for a first-time event, new venue or outdoor site. Control it through one approver.

What is an attrition clause in an event contract?

A clause that lets your guaranteed numbers, such as catering covers or hotel rooms, fall by an agreed amount without penalty, and charges you for shortfalls beyond that amount.

How do you manage risk at an event?

Keep a risk register from the start, carry out a risk assessment and emergency plan, check contract cancellation terms, hold contingency budget, plan for weather and brief staff on the chain of command.

Which KPIs measure event success?

Registrations against target, attendance rate, budget variance, cost per attendee, sponsor revenue and renewal, attendee satisfaction or NPS, and leads or meetings generated.

Which templates are used in event project management?

An event plan, budget, Gantt timeline, agenda, run of show, supplier tracker, risk register, RACI matrix and lessons learned template.

What software is used for event project management?

A project management platform such as Wrike for planning, approvals and coordination, plus specialist tools for registration, ticketing, badging and floor plans.

Is there a certification for event project management?

The Certified Meeting Professional (CMP) from the Events Industry Council is a recognised event credential; general project management certifications help with planning skills.
