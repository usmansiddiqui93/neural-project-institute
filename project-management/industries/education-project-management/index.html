Education Project Management: Phases, Roles and Example

Education project management is the planning and delivery of change in schools, colleges, universities and training organisations: new courses, curriculum redesigns, learning platform rollouts, building works and accreditation reviews. What sets it apart is the academic calendar. Most changes must be ready on the first day of a term, teaching cannot stop while they are made, and approvals follow committee cycles that do not move. This guide covers the life cycle, roles and methods, the compliance rules to plan for in 2026–27, and a worked example that schedules a platform replacement backwards from a September start.

Key takeaways

  • Plan backwards from the term start date and from the dates of the committees or governing bodies that must approve the work; both are fixed constraints.
  • Missing one approval meeting can cost far more than the weeks between meetings, because it can push the next steps out of the term in which staff are available.
  • Use ADDIE or an iterative design model for course content, phased plans for buildings and core systems, and Kanban or Scrum for continuing digital work.
  • Build accessibility, data protection and safeguarding checks into requirements and procurement, not into the final week before launch.
  • Most education projects are led by people with another job, so keep the method light: a one-page plan, a visible board and protected project time.
  • Measure readiness for term start, then adoption and learner outcomes; on-time delivery alone says little about whether teaching improved.

What is education project management?

Education project management is the application of project management to temporary initiatives in education organisations, such as a new degree programme, a learning management system rollout or a school building extension. It combines standard scope, schedule, cost, risk and stakeholder control with sector constraints: fixed term dates, committee-based approval, academic ownership of teaching, and duties towards learners.

It is different from running the institution. Timetabling, admissions rounds and marking are recurring operations; a project creates something new and then hands it over to those operations. The distinction matters because the same people usually do both, and project work competes directly with teaching for their time.

This page is part of our series on project management by industry. If your projects are funded research rather than teaching or operational change, read research project management instead.

How education projects differ from other projects

A 2002 paper in PMI's library by William Scally listed ten issues in managing education projects, among them working out what students actually need, reconciling classroom expectations with management expectations, and measuring success by more than one criterion. Those issues remain recognisable, and several structural differences sit behind them:

  • The academic calendar is a hard constraint. A system that is not ready for the first week of term may wait a term or a full year, because switching mid-term disrupts learners. Holidays are the main windows for building and infrastructure work, but most teaching staff are unavailable then.
  • Shared governance. Universities decide through senates, academic boards and committees; schools and colleges through governing bodies and trust boards. They meet on cycles fixed a year ahead, with paper deadlines before each meeting, so a missed deadline can mean waiting a full term for the next decision.
  • Academic autonomy. Teaching staff expect to shape changes to curriculum and assessment, and in universities the senate or academic board usually holds formal responsibility for academic matters. Change imposed without consultation tends to be adopted on paper only.
  • Many stakeholder groups. Learners, parents and carers, teaching and support staff, governors, inspectors, regulators, awarding bodies, funders and the local community can all be affected by one project.
  • Duties towards learners. Safeguarding, special educational needs, accessibility and protection of learner data shape requirements and procurement, especially where children are involved.
  • Funding cycles that do not match the academic year. Capital grants, annual budgets and the institution's financial year may each start on different dates. Academy trusts in England, for example, account for a year ending 31 August, so a project spanning summer can straddle two budget years.
  • Limited dedicated capacity. Many schools have no project manager; a deputy head, business manager or teacher leads the project alongside a full timetable.

The education project life cycle

Most education projects follow seven broad phases. They are planned backwards from the launch date, and course projects often run the ADDIE instructional design model (analysis, design, development, implementation, evaluation) inside phases 2 to 7. These are life-cycle phases, not the PMBOK process groups; see project management phases for the general model.

PhaseWhat happensTiming tip
1. Proposal and approvalNeed identified; business case or course proposal approved by the relevant committee or governing bodyFind the meeting and paper deadline dates first; they fix the earliest start
2. AnalysisLearner needs, regulatory or awarding body requirements, current state, data protection screeningConsult staff and learners during term, before the summer break
3. DesignLearning outcomes, curriculum structure, system or building requirements, accessibility requirementsAlign with timetabling, enrolment and validation deadlines
4. Development and buildContent creation, platform configuration and integration, procurement, constructionBuilding and infrastructure work in holidays
5. Pilot and trainingPilot with a small group, train staff, prepare guidance for learners and familiesFinish training before staff leave for the summer
6. LaunchGo-live at term start with additional supportPlan a support rota for the first two to three weeks of term
7. Evaluation and closureLearner feedback, results, adoption data, lessons learned, handover to operationsEvaluate after a full term or year, not at launch

Planning backwards from the academic calendar

Because the launch date is fixed, education schedules are usually built with a backward pass: start from the date teaching begins, subtract each activity's duration and find the latest date each activity can start without missing it. The forward and backward pass guide explains the method in detail. The difference between the latest start and the earliest realistic start is the activity's total float; see float and slack.

Two features make the education version harder than a textbook network. Activities that need teaching staff can only run in term time, and activities that need governance approval can only start after a meeting. Both create gaps in the calendar that a simple duration count ignores.

Calendar windows and what they suit

The windows below follow a typical English school and college year; adapt them to your own calendar, including university semesters, trimester systems or southern-hemisphere years.

WindowGood forAvoid
Autumn term (September to December)Analysis, consultation, approval papers, procurementLaunching new systems after the first weeks of term; staff are inducting new learners
Spring term (January to Easter)Design, configuration, building contracts, early pilotsMajor changes during mock exams or coursework deadlines
Summer term (Easter to July)Pilots, staff training, content migration, readiness checksAsking exam-year teachers for time in May and June
Summer holidayBuilding works, infrastructure, data migration, cutoverAnything needing teachers or learners to test or decide
Short holidays and half-termsSmall infrastructure changes, network upgradesAssuming staff will attend training
Tip

Put every governance meeting and paper deadline on the project schedule as a milestone, with its dependency on the work it approves. A missed paper deadline should show up as a schedule problem straight away, not at the meeting.

Worked example: a VLE replacement for a September start

This hypothetical example uses an illustrative further education college in England with 380 teaching staff. It is replacing its virtual learning environment (VLE) and wants the new platform live for the first day of teaching, Monday 3 September 2029. The college's governing body must approve the contract because its value exceeds the principal's delegated limit; the board meets on Wednesday 13 December 2028 and next on Wednesday 14 March 2029, with papers due 10 working days before each meeting. Durations are in calendar weeks, and the estimates absorb bank holidays.

Step 1: the backward pass

Staff leave for the summer after Friday 13 July 2029, so all work that needs teachers must finish by then. The IT team can do technical cutover in August. Working backwards:

ActivityDurationMust finish byLatest startReason for the finish date
Content migration and teacher checks6 weeksFri 13 Jul 2029Mon 4 Jun 2029Teachers must check their own courses before the summer
Staff training (within the migration period)4 weeksFri 13 Jul 2029Mon 18 Jun 2029Training is most useful close to first use, but before the summer
Pilot with six courses6 weeksFri 25 May 2029Mon 16 Apr 2029Must end before the half-term week starting 28 May and leave time to act on findings
Configuration and student record system integration8 weeksFri 13 Apr 2029Mon 19 Feb 2029The pilot needs a working, integrated platform
Contract signature and supplier onboarding3 weeksFri 16 Feb 2029Mon 29 Jan 2029Configuration cannot start until the supplier is engaged

So the contract work must start no later than Monday 29 January 2029, which in turn requires governing body approval before that date. Only the December meeting qualifies.

Step 2: the float under each approval date

  • Approval on 13 December 2028. The paper is due on Wednesday 29 November 2028. The college is closed from Saturday 16 December and reopens on Tuesday 2 January 2029, after the New Year bank holiday. Because the estimates absorb bank holidays, the three weeks of contract work count from the week beginning Monday 1 January, so configuration can start on Monday 22 January 2029. Its latest start is Monday 19 February, so the project has 4 weeks of total float.
  • Approval slips to 14 March 2029. Contract work starts on Monday 19 March, so configuration starts on Monday 9 April and ends on Friday 1 June 2029. That is 7 weeks after its latest finish: the float is −7 weeks. A six-week pilot would end on Friday 13 July, the last day staff are available, leaving no time for training or content migration with teachers.

A paper that misses the 29 November deadline, even by a few days, turns 4 weeks of float into −7 weeks, a swing of 11 weeks. The cause is the 13-week gap between board meetings combined with the fixed summer break, not the few days by which the paper was late. This is why the critical path in education projects often runs through a governance date rather than through technical work.

What the project manager should do with this result:

  1. Treat the 29 November paper deadline as the most important date in the plan and agree who writes and checks the paper now.
  2. Ask the clerk to the governors whether the college's scheme of delegation allows urgent approval between meetings, for example by the chair, and record the answer in the risk register as a fallback.
  3. Prepare a contingency plan if approval slips: launch with new courses only in September and migrate existing courses for the January term, or keep the old VLE for one more year. Each option has a cost and should be priced before December, not after.

Step 3: can the training window cope?

Each of the 380 teaching staff needs one 3-hour hands-on session. The training room holds 16 people, and the timetable allows one trainer to run 2 sessions a day on 3 days a week during the 4-week training window.

  • Sessions needed at full attendance = 380 ÷ 16 = 23.75, rounded up to 24 sessions.
  • Sessions available with one trainer = 4 weeks × 3 days × 2 sessions = 24 sessions.
  • If booked sessions run at 85% attendance on average (an assumption for this example), sessions needed = 380 ÷ (16 × 0.85) = 27.94, rounded up to 28 sessions.

Capacity exactly matches demand only if every booked seat is filled, which is unrealistic. The plan needs at least 4 more sessions: a second trainer for two weeks, a recorded module for experienced staff, or training for some departments during the pilot. The capacity planning guide covers the general method, and the training plan template records each group, the activity, method, dates, cost, owner and status.

Step 4: readiness criteria for go/no-go

Agree go/no-go criteria with the sponsor in advance and review them at a readiness meeting before the summer break. The thresholds below are illustrative; set your own with the sponsor.

CriterionIllustrative thresholdIf not met
Courses migrated and checked by their ownersAt least 95% of courses running in SeptemberLaunch with checked courses; keep read-only access to the old VLE for the rest
Teaching staff trainedAt least 90%Run drop-in sessions in the September staff development days
Enrolment data flowing from the student record systemTested end to end with real enrolment dataNo-go: learners would not see their courses
Accessibility check of templates and key pagesMeets the target standard, with known issues loggedFix critical issues before launch; publish the rest in the accessibility statement
Data protection impact assessmentApproved by the data protection officerNo-go until approved

Choosing a delivery approach

No single method suits every education project. Choose by the type of work and how stable the requirements are, and combine approaches where a project has both fixed and uncertain parts.

ApproachBest forWhen it is a poor choice
Predictive (waterfall) with stage approvalsBuilding works, student record system replacements, accreditation reviewsCourse content whose design will change after learners try it
ADDIECourse and curriculum development with a clear specificationRun as a strict sequence when the audience or content is uncertain; repeat the cycle instead
Iterative design, such as the Successive Approximation Model (SAM)Online courses and resources that benefit from rapid prototypes tested with learnersProjects where an awarding body fixes the content and assessment
ScrumLearning apps, web services, online course production with a dedicated teamPart-time teams that cannot meet regularly or keep a stable Sprint length
KanbanContinuing course updates, IT requests, admissions improvementsLarge one-off projects that need a fixed scope and budget approval
PRINCE2Larger publicly funded programmes that need formal stage boundariesSmall school projects, where its governance overhead outweighs the benefit
HybridDigital learning programmes: phased approvals and a fixed launch, with iterative content developmentTeams that use it to avoid deciding who approves what

ADDIE is an instructional design model, not a project management method; it describes how to create learning, while the project plan controls time, cost and approvals around it. For the wider choice between approaches, read Agile vs waterfall, and see what Kanban is for continuing improvement work.

Legal and compliance requirements to plan for

The rules below affect requirements, procurement and schedules for many education projects. They are summarised as at 25 November 2026 for planning purposes and are not legal advice; check the current text and take advice from your data protection officer or legal team.

Digital accessibility

  • UK. GOV.UK guidance on the Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018 says public sector websites and apps must meet WCAG 2.2 AA and publish an accessibility statement. The same guidance says primary and secondary schools and nurseries are partially exempt, except for content people need to use their services, and must still publish a statement. Jisc advises that colleges and universities are within scope and that VLEs and intranets are covered.
  • United States. The Department of Justice's 2024 ADA Title II rule requires state and local government web content and mobile apps, including those of public schools, community colleges and public universities, to meet WCAG 2.1 AA. An interim final rule effective 20 April 2026 moved the compliance dates to 26 April 2027 for public entities with a total population of 50,000 or more and 26 April 2028 for smaller entities and special district governments. Because the rule invited comments, check for later changes.

For a platform project, this means accessibility belongs in the procurement requirements and acceptance tests, and course templates should be checked before teachers copy them hundreds of times.

Learner data

  • UK. Under UK GDPR, a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) is required for processing likely to result in a high risk to individuals. In England, the Department for Education's guidance on procuring educational technology (updated 13 November 2026) tells schools to start the DPIA early, check where data is stored, retention, sub-processors and breach notification, agree an exit plan for data at the start, and check whether pupil data is used to train AI features.
  • United States. FERPA protects education records at agencies and institutions that receive funds from programmes administered by the US Department of Education, which includes public schools and most colleges and universities. Contracts with platform suppliers need to reflect it.

Safeguarding

In England, Keeping Children Safe in Education 2026 came into force on 1 September 2026, replacing the 2025 edition. Projects that change online services used by pupils should involve the designated safeguarding lead, because the guidance expects appropriate filtering and monitoring; building projects need arrangements for contractors working on a site with children present.

Engaging staff, learners and families

Adoption, not delivery, is where most education projects succeed or fail. A new VLE that teachers do not use, or a curriculum that exists only in documents, has not achieved its objective. Practical techniques:

  • Map stakeholders by group and by term. A head of department's interest and availability change through the year; plan consultation for when people can respond. Our stakeholder management guide covers the power-interest grid.
  • Recruit early adopters from each department to pilot, give feedback and support colleagues after launch.
  • Limit concurrent changes. As a rule of thumb, avoid launching two changes that affect the same staff in the same term; sequence them across a year instead.
  • Tailor messages. Teachers need to know what changes in their lessons; learners need to know where to find their work; parents and carers need to know how to support them. A communication plan sets out the audience, message, channel, timing and owner.
  • Release time for project work. Timetabled remission or cover is more effective than asking staff to fit project tasks around teaching.

Essential documents and templates

  • Project proposal or business case for committee or governor approval; see the business case template.
  • Project charter setting out purpose, scope, sponsor and success measures; see how to write a project charter.
  • Schedule mapped to term dates, governance meetings and holidays; the project schedule template has tasks, owners, start dates, durations, predecessors, per cent complete and milestones.
  • RACI matrix for who designs, approves and delivers each part.
  • Risk register including safeguarding, data protection, accessibility and term-start risks.
  • Training plan for staff, linked to the launch criteria.
  • Communication plan for staff, learners, families and governors.
  • Lessons learned reviewed before the next intake or rollout; see lessons learned.

Software for education project management

Education organisations run their operations on learning management systems, student information systems and timetabling software, then use a separate project tool to plan and track change. Keep learner records in the student system; the project tool should hold tasks, plans and decisions, not personal data about learners.

  • Simple boards for part-time teams and timelines for term-based plans.
  • Education or nonprofit pricing, where the vendor offers it.
  • Guest access for suppliers, contractors and external partners.
  • Single sign-on with the institution's identity system.
  • Data protection terms and data location that your data protection officer can approve.

For universities, colleges and multi-academy trusts running many projects, Wrike, ranked first in our project management tool reviews, suits portfolio dashboards, Gantt plans and request forms; our review notes a steeper learning curve than simpler tools. Smaller schools may prefer a lighter board tool such as Trello, or Microsoft Planner where the school already uses Microsoft 365. A small project management office helps larger institutions prioritise competing projects.

Frequently asked questions

What is education project management?

It is the planning and delivery of projects in schools, colleges, universities and training organisations, such as new courses, learning platforms and building works, around the academic calendar and governance cycle.

What does a project manager do in education?

They plan and coordinate change projects, prepare approval papers, manage budgets, risks and suppliers, and make sure staff and learners are ready for launch. In schools the role is often part of a teacher's or business manager's job.

Why is the academic calendar so important?

Most changes must go live at the start of a term, teaching staff are unavailable in holidays, and building work must fit into those holidays. Missing a window can delay a project by a term or a year.

How do you schedule an education project?

Start from the term start date, work backwards through each activity's duration to find its latest start, and add governance meetings and term-time-only activities as constraints. Then compare the latest starts with realistic start dates to find the float.

What is ADDIE in education project management?

ADDIE is an instructional design model with five phases: analysis, design, development, implementation and evaluation. It structures how learning is created; a project plan still controls time, cost and approvals around it.

Which methodology is best for education projects?

It depends on the work. Predictive plans suit buildings and core systems, ADDIE or iterative design suits courses, and Kanban or Scrum suit continuing digital work. Many projects combine them.

Do schools need a data protection impact assessment for new edtech?

In the UK, a DPIA is required where processing is likely to be high risk, and Department for Education guidance tells schools to start one early when procuring edtech. Ask your data protection officer whether your project needs one.

What KPIs are used for education projects?

Readiness for term start, staff training completion, adoption, learner satisfaction, attainment or retention changes, schedule and cost performance, and support requests after launch.

How do you get teachers to adopt a new system?

Involve them in design and piloting, train them before they need the system, release time for project work, avoid stacking several changes in one term and provide visible support in the first weeks.

What software do schools and universities use for projects?

They combine their learning and student systems with a separate project tool. Larger institutions often use a portfolio-capable tool such as Wrike; smaller schools often use a simple board tool or Microsoft Planner.
