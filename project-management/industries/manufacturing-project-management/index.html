Manufacturing Project Management: NPI, Phases and KPIs

Manufacturing project management is the planning and control of projects that create or change how products are made: new product introductions (NPI), new production lines, equipment installations, plant expansions and improvement programmes. The project is only finished when the factory can make the product reliably at the target rate, quality and cost, not when the machine is switched on. This guide covers the NPI and capital project life cycles, roles, methods, three worked examples, KPIs, templates and software.

Key takeaways

  • Manufacturing project management ends at a stable, capable process running at rate, so plan ramp-up and handover as part of the project.
  • New products usually follow stage-gate or APQP phases ending in production part approval; capital projects follow specification, FAT, installation, SAT, commissioning and ramp-up.
  • Long-lead tooling and equipment set the critical path, so freeze the design before tooling release and track suppliers weekly.
  • Keep FMEAs for product and process risks and a project risk register for schedule, supply and cost risks, and cross-reference them.
  • Agree measurable exit criteria (OEE, first pass yield, rate) with the plant manager at the start, not at the end.
  • Keep product and production data in PLM, ERP and MES, and coordinate the cross-functional project work in a platform such as Wrike.

What is manufacturing project management?

Manufacturing project management is the application of project management methods to temporary initiatives that introduce or change products, processes, equipment or facilities in a manufacturing business. A manufacturing project manager coordinates design, manufacturing engineering, quality, procurement, suppliers and production so that the change reaches stable, approved, at-rate production on time and within budget.

It is different from running the factory day to day. Production management repeats the same process every shift; a project has a defined start, end and unique outcome. The two meet at handover, where the project is judged.

Project typeExampleWhat usually sets the critical path
New product introduction (NPI)A new model, variant or component launched into an existing plantDesign freeze, tooling lead times and customer approval
Capital equipment and new linesA new assembly line, CNC cell, moulding press or packaging lineEquipment build, shutdown windows and acceptance tests
Plant expansion or new facilityA new building, hall extension or greenfield sitePermits, construction, utilities and equipment install
Process improvementReducing scrap, cutting changeover time, improving flowData collection and trial runs on a live line
Automation and digitalRobots, vision inspection, MES rollout, connected sensorsIntegration, software testing and operator training
Product or line transferMoving production between plants or to a contract manufacturerBuild-ahead stock, requalification and customer approval

What makes manufacturing projects different

The standard toolkit still applies: a charter, a work breakdown structure, a schedule and a risk register. What changes is the set of constraints you plan around.

The factory keeps running

Many projects happen inside a live plant. Installation work has to fit planned shutdowns, weekends or holiday periods, and every hour of unplanned downtime costs output. Customers still expect deliveries, so build-ahead stock and temporary routings become part of the plan.

Physical long-lead items

Moulds, dies, fixtures, special machines and some components can take many weeks or months to make. Once tooling is cut, design changes become expensive. That makes design freeze and tooling release the most important decisions in many NPI schedules.

Quality systems and customer approval

Quality management standards such as ISO 9001, sector standards such as IATF 16949 (automotive), AS9100 (aerospace) and ISO 13485 (medical devices), and good manufacturing practice in food and pharmaceuticals require documented, controlled processes. Automotive customers usually require formal production part approval before shipments start. These approvals are milestones on the critical path, not paperwork at the end.

Success is measured after go-live

A line that is installed on time but takes six months to reach its target rate has not delivered its business case. Ramp-up, capability and handover criteria belong in the project scope.

Many functions must sign off

Design, manufacturing engineering, quality, purchasing, logistics, maintenance, health and safety, finance and production all hold part of the outcome. The project manager's main job is to keep them working to one integrated plan.

The NPI life cycle: stage-gate and APQP

New product introduction in many companies follows a stage-gate process: work is divided into phases and a gate review decides whether the project has earned the next tranche of investment. In automotive and related supply chains, the AIAG Advanced Product Quality Planning (APQP) framework provides the structure and ends with Production Part Approval Process (PPAP) submission. AIAG published the third edition of the APQP manual in 2024 with a standalone Control Plan manual; the third edition adds a gated management section with suggested checklists for each gate.

APQP phaseWhat happensKey outputsTypical gate question
1. Plan and define the programmeCapture customer requirements, targets, volumes and constraintsDesign goals, quality targets, preliminary bill of materials and process flow, special characteristicsIs the scope clear enough to fund design?
2. Product design and developmentDetailed design, design FMEA, prototypes and design verificationDrawings, DFMEA, prototype build, verification results, design freezeIs the design frozen enough to release tooling?
3. Process design and developmentDesign the process, layout, tooling, fixtures, gauges and controlsProcess flow diagram, process FMEA, pre-launch control plan, layout, work instructionsAre tools, equipment and suppliers on track for trials?
4. Product and process validationTrial runs at rate, measurement system analysis, capability studiesProduction run results, MSA, capability data, production control plan, PPAPIs the process capable and approved for shipment?
5. Feedback, assessment and corrective actionRun at rate, reduce variation, close actionsImprovement actions, delivery and quality performance, lessons learnedCan the project close and hand over?

What goes into a PPAP submission

PPAP is the customer's evidence that your process can consistently make parts that meet every requirement. The AIAG PPAP manual lists 18 elements, including design records, engineering change documents, the DFMEA and PFMEA, process flow diagram, control plan, measurement system analysis, dimensional results, material and performance test results, initial process studies, appearance approval, sample parts and the Part Submission Warrant. The customer chooses one of five submission levels, which sets how much evidence you send and how much you retain.

Tip

Outside automotive the names change but the logic is the same: aerospace suppliers complete a first article inspection, medical device makers complete design transfer and process validation, and food and pharmaceutical plants qualify equipment and validate processes before release.

The capital project life cycle: from specification to ramp-up

Installing a new line, cell or major machine follows a parallel path. It looks more like a Waterfall project with a hard sequence of acceptance tests.

StageWhat happensKey output
1. Business case and conceptDefine capacity, product mix, cycle time and budget; compare optionsApproved capital request and business case
2. User requirements specification (URS)What the equipment must do: rate, tolerances, changeovers, safety, utilities, dataSigned URS used for quotations and acceptance tests
3. Supplier selectionRequest for quotation, technical comparison, negotiationPurchase order, functional specification and payment milestones
4. Design reviewReview layouts, guarding, controls and maintenance access with the supplierApproved design and FAT protocol
5. Factory acceptance test (FAT)Run the equipment at the supplier with your parts and criteria before shippingSigned FAT report and punch list
6. Delivery and installationSite preparation, utilities, rigging, mechanical and electrical installationInstalled equipment, safety checks completed
7. Site acceptance test (SAT) and commissioningProve performance in your plant with your utilities, materials and operatorsSigned SAT report, open points list
8. Ramp-up and handoverStaged increase to target rate; training; spares; maintenance planExit criteria met and formal handover

In regulated sectors such as pharmaceuticals and food, installation, operational and performance qualification (IQ, OQ, PQ) are added before the equipment can make saleable product.

How to run a manufacturing project step by step

This sequence works for both NPI and capital projects. Scale the documentation to the size of the project.

  1. Define the outcome and exit criteria. Write the project charter with product, volume, rate, quality target, budget and start-of-production (SOP) date. Agree measurable handover criteria with the plant manager now.
  2. Form the cross-functional team. Name owners from design, manufacturing engineering, quality, purchasing, logistics, maintenance, safety and production, and set out decision rights in a RACI matrix.
  3. Break down the work. Build a work breakdown structure covering product, process, tooling, equipment, facility, people and documentation.
  4. Identify long-lead items first. List tooling, equipment and components with quoted lead times and order as early as design maturity allows.
  5. Schedule backwards from SOP. Work back through approval, trials, tooling and design freeze to find the latest start for each item and the critical path.
  6. Assess risk twice. Run DFMEA and PFMEA for product and process risks, and keep a project risk register for schedule, supplier, cost and resource risks.
  7. Set the gate reviews. Define what must be complete at each gate, who decides and what happens if criteria are not met.
  8. Control changes. Route every engineering and process change through change control with tooling, validation, cost and schedule impact assessed.
  9. Prove the process. Run FAT and SAT, trial runs, measurement system analysis and capability studies, and fix problems with structured root cause analysis.
  10. Prepare people. Write work instructions and train operators and maintenance staff before SOP; set up spares and preventive maintenance.
  11. Ramp up in stages. Increase rate against a plan, track daily against exit criteria and keep the project team in place until they are met.
  12. Hand over and close. Sign the handover, transfer open actions to production owners, close the budget and run a lessons learned review that feeds the next launch.

Worked example 1: backward scheduling an NPI launch

A supplier must launch a new injection-moulded housing for a customer whose SOP is week 40. The figures are illustrative planning assumptions, not industry standards; use your own supplier quotes.

MilestonePlanned weekDuration logic
Kick-off and customer requirements review1Project start
Design freeze8Prototypes and design verification complete
Tool kick-off (purchase order to toolmaker)9Needs frozen design data
First tool trial (T1) samples25Quoted 16-week tool build
Tool corrections after T125 to 29One round of corrections allowed
Second tool trial (T2) and dimensional layout30Confirms corrections
Production run, MSA and capability studies32 to 35Run at rate with production operators
PPAP submission35Evidence pack complete
PPAP approval37Customer review assumed at two weeks
SOP40Three weeks of buffer

The critical path runs through design freeze, tool build, trials, the production run and approval. Packaging design, operator training and label approval all have float.

What happens when the design changes late

In week 20 the customer requests a change to a clip feature. The toolmaker quotes four extra weeks of tool work. The buffer was three weeks, so SOP is now at risk by one week. The options:

  • Crash the tool work: pay for overtime or a second shift at the toolmaker to recover one to two weeks. See fast-tracking vs crashing.
  • Fast-track validation: start measurement system analysis on the gauges while tool corrections are underway, accepting some rework risk.
  • Defer the change: agree with the customer to launch on the frozen design and introduce the clip change after SOP.
  • Re-baseline: if none of these work, agree a new SOP date formally rather than letting the date slip silently.

Whatever the decision, record it as a change request with its cost and schedule impact and update the baseline. Our project schedule template and critical path template are set up for this kind of plan.

Worked example 2: installing a line in a shutdown window

A plant will replace an ageing assembly line during a two-week summer shutdown (10 working days). The customer keeps calling off 2,000 units a day, five days a week, throughout. The new line should reach full rate three weeks after the shutdown.

Step 1: calculate the build-ahead stock

Source of lost outputCalculationUnits
Shutdown (line not running)10 days x 2,00020,000
Ramp-up week 1 at 40% of rate5 days x 2,000 x 60% shortfall6,000
Ramp-up week 2 at 70% of rate5 days x 2,000 x 30% shortfall3,000
Ramp-up week 3 at 90% of rate5 days x 2,000 x 10% shortfall1,000
Safety stock for start-up problems3 days x 2,0006,000
Total build-ahead required36,000

Spread over the 40 working days before the shutdown, the old line must make 900 extra units a day. If that is beyond its capacity, the plan needs to change: a longer build-ahead period, a temporary extra shift or a staged changeover. Check warehouse space and shelf life too.

Step 2: plan the shutdown day by day

DaysWorkAcceptance point
Before shutdownFAT passed, site prepared, utilities run, permits and method statements approvedReadiness review: go or no-go to remove the old line
1 to 2Isolate and remove the old line, floor preparationArea handed over clean and safe
3 to 5Position, level and fix the new line; mechanical and electrical connectionInstallation checks complete
6 to 7Power-up, safety system and guarding checks, dry cyclingSafety validation signed off
8 to 9SAT with production parts and operatorsSAT criteria met or punch list agreed
10Trial production, first-off approvalGo for production or activate the contingency plan

Rehearse the critical tasks, name an owner for every shift of the plan and agree in advance what triggers the fallback, for example keeping the old line in storage until the new one passes SAT.

Step 3: define ramp-up exit criteria

Week after shutdownTarget rateCriteria to move to the next stage
140%No open safety actions; first pass yield at least 90%
270%OEE trend improving; top downtime causes not repeating
390%First pass yield at target for five consecutive days
4 onwards100%Rate, OEE and yield sustained for two weeks; handover signed

These targets are examples. Set yours from the business case and the supplier's guaranteed performance.

Worked example 3: calculating OEE at handover

Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) is the most common handover measure for new lines. It multiplies availability, performance and quality.

FactorData for one shiftCalculationResult
AvailabilityPlanned time 450 minutes; 45 minutes of stops(450 - 45) / 45090.0%
PerformanceIdeal cycle 1.0 minute; 380 parts in 405 minutes of run time(380 x 1.0) / 40593.8%
Quality365 good parts out of 380365 / 38096.1%
OEE90.0% x 93.8% x 96.1%81.1%

If the agreed handover target is 80%, this shift passes. Judge the trend over several weeks rather than one shift, and use the loss breakdown to direct improvement: here the 45 minutes of stops are the biggest loss. Agree how planned stops, changeovers and trials are counted before you start, or the project team and the plant will report different numbers.

Skills and certifications

Useful credentials combine project control with manufacturing knowledge: the PMP or PRINCE2 for project management, Lean and Six Sigma belts for improvement work, and training in the automotive core tools (APQP, PPAP, FMEA, MSA, SPC) or the quality standard your sector uses.

Managing risk: FMEA and the project risk register

Manufacturing projects need two complementary risk tools.

FMEA (design and process)Project risk register
FocusHow the product or process could fail and the effect on the customerWhat could stop the project meeting time, cost or scope
OwnerDesign and quality engineersProject manager with workstream leads
ExamplesWeld undersize; wrong part fitted; label misprintToolmaker capacity; late customer data; budget overrun
MethodSeverity, occurrence and detection ratings; the AIAG and VDA FMEA handbook ranks actions by action priority (high, medium, low)Probability and impact scoring, owners, responses and triggers
OutputDesign and process changes, controls in the control planMitigation actions, contingency and reserve

Cross-reference the two: a high-priority failure mode with a late corrective action is also a schedule risk. Example register entry: Risk: the toolmaker has three other tools due at the same time. Effect: T1 slips two to four weeks, consuming all schedule buffer. Response: agree a capacity commitment in the purchase order, hold fortnightly progress visits and line up a second toolmaker for corrections. Owner: supplier quality engineer.

Quality standards and regulations to plan for

Standards and laws differ by sector and country, so confirm what applies with your quality and safety teams. These are the ones that most often appear as milestones.

  • ISO 9001. ISO 9001:2026 was published on 16 September 2026, replacing the 2015 edition, with a transition period of about three years for certified organisations. Its changes include stronger expectations on quality culture, risk and opportunity, and planned change, so certified plants may run a transition project alongside their launches.
  • Sector standards. IATF 16949 for automotive, AS9100 for aerospace and defence, ISO 13485 for medical devices, and good manufacturing practice for pharmaceuticals and food. Each adds specific approval and validation steps.
  • Machinery safety. In the EU, the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies to machinery placed on the market from 20 January 2027, replacing the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC. Equipment delivered around that date may fall under the new rules, so state the conformity requirements in the purchase order. Great Britain and the US have their own regimes.

Common challenges and mistakes

  • Tooling and equipment delays. Track long-lead items weekly, visit suppliers and make FAT a real test with your parts rather than a demonstration.
  • Late design changes. Freeze the design before tooling release and assess every change for tooling, validation, cost and schedule impact before approving it.
  • Planning only to installation. A plan that ends at SAT hides the ramp-up. Include ramp-up stages and exit criteria in the baseline.
  • Untrained operators at SOP. Train operators and maintenance staff on the real equipment before start of production.
  • Shared engineers overloaded. Plants running several launches depend on the same few engineers. Prioritise at portfolio level; see managing multiple projects.
  • Unclear handover. Agree acceptance criteria with the plant manager at the start and keep the project open until they are met.

Software for manufacturing project management

Manufacturers rely on specialist systems for operational data: ERP for materials, purchasing and costs; PLM for product data, bills of materials and engineering changes; MES for shop floor execution; and a quality management system for non-conformances and corrective actions. These systems are not designed to coordinate a cross-functional launch, which is where a project management platform helps.

Look for Gantt charts with dependencies and baselines, stage-gate templates, approval workflows, workload views across projects, portfolio dashboards and integrations with your ERP and file storage. Keep detailed product and production data in PLM, ERP and MES, and link to it from the project rather than copying it.

Wrike is the strongest all-in-one choice in our tool rankings for this coordination role, with Gantt, workload and dashboard features that suit multi-project NPI and capital portfolios. Spreadsheet-minded engineering teams also get on well with Smartsheet.

Frequently asked questions

What is manufacturing project management?

Manufacturing project management is planning and controlling projects that create or change how products are made, such as new product introductions, new lines, equipment installations, plant expansions and improvement programmes. It ends when the factory can make the product reliably at the target rate, quality and cost.

What are the phases of manufacturing project management?

For new products, many firms use stage-gate or APQP phases: plan and define, product design and development, process design and development, product and process validation, and feedback and corrective action. Capital projects run from business case and specification through FAT, installation, SAT, commissioning and ramp-up.

What is APQP in manufacturing project management?

Advanced Product Quality Planning is a framework published by AIAG and widely used in automotive supply chains. It plans product and process development so parts meet customer requirements, and ends with PPAP approval. The third edition, published in 2024, adds gated management checklists.

What is NPI in manufacturing project management?

New product introduction is the process of taking a new product from design into reliable volume production, including tooling, process development, validation, customer approval and ramp-up.

What is the difference between FAT and SAT in manufacturing project management?

A factory acceptance test checks equipment at the supplier before it ships. A site acceptance test checks it after installation in your plant, with your utilities, materials and operators.

How is manufacturing project management different from production management?

Production management runs the factory every day to make current products to plan. Manufacturing project management delivers a one-off change, such as a new product or line, and hands it over to production when agreed exit criteria are met.

What KPIs are used in manufacturing project management?

Project KPIs include milestones on time, capital spend against budget, gate readiness and open engineering changes. Result KPIs include time to market, ramp-up time, OEE, first pass yield and process capability.

How do you calculate OEE for a manufacturing project handover?

Multiply availability by performance by quality. For example, 90% availability, 93.8% performance and 96.1% quality give an OEE of about 81%. Agree how planned stops and trials are counted before you measure.

What documents are used in manufacturing project management?

A charter, business case, WBS, schedule, cost estimate, RACI, risk register and change requests, plus engineering documents such as FMEAs, process flow diagrams, control plans, work instructions, specifications and acceptance test protocols.

How do you manage risk in manufacturing project management?

Use design and process FMEAs for failure risks, a project risk register for schedule, supply and cost risks, and gate reviews to confirm readiness before major commitments such as tooling release.

Which methodology is best for manufacturing project management?

Stage-gate or APQP suits new products, Waterfall with critical path suits installations and expansions, Lean and Six Sigma suit improvement projects, and Agile works for automation software inside a gated plan. Most plants use a hybrid.

What software is used for manufacturing project management?

ERP, PLM, MES and quality systems hold operational data, and a project platform such as Wrike coordinates schedules, gates, approvals, resources and reporting across launches and capital projects.
