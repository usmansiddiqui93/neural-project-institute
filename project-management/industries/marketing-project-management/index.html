Marketing Project Management: Process, Roles and Tools

Marketing project management is the planning, coordination and control of marketing work, from a single asset to a multi-channel campaign, so that it launches on time, on brand, within budget and against a measurable goal. It differs from most project work because launch dates are usually fixed before scope is known, many people review the same assets and the work never really stops: one campaign launches while the next is being briefed. This guide covers the campaign life cycle, backwards planning, a worked example, intake, approvals, roles, KPIs and tools.

Key takeaways

  • Marketing project management runs many overlapping campaigns with fixed dates, shared specialists and heavy review cycles.
  • A signed brief, one intake queue and capped, consolidated review rounds solve most delivery problems.
  • Plan backwards from the launch date and cut deliverables early rather than squeezing review time.
  • Score incoming requests against goals, deadline and effort so priorities are agreed, not shouted.
  • Track delivery KPIs (on-time launch rate, cycle time, review rounds) separately from campaign results.
  • Most teams use a hybrid: a milestone plan for launches with Kanban or sprints for production.

What is marketing project management?

Marketing project management is the use of project management methods to plan, schedule, resource and track marketing work, such as campaigns, product launches, content programmes, events and website projects, so it is delivered on time, within budget and against a clear business objective. It applies standard project management practice to the realities of marketing: fixed dates, many reviewers and shared creative capacity.

Marketing strategy decides what to do and why: the audience, positioning and channels. Marketing project management decides how the work gets done: who does what, by when, with which approvals. Both matter, and a good marketing project manager connects them by making sure every plan traces back to a goal in the brief.

Why marketing teams need project management

Marketing teams rarely fail for lack of ideas. They struggle with volume, interruptions and approvals. A light project management approach helps in practical ways:

  • Visibility. Everyone can see what is in flight, who owns it and when it launches, which cuts status meetings and chasing.
  • Fewer missed launches. Plans built backwards from fixed dates expose problems while there is still time to cut scope.
  • Faster approvals. Named approvers and scheduled review windows replace open-ended email threads.
  • Balanced workloads. Capacity views show when designers, writers or developers are overloaded before quality drops.
  • Clear priorities. One intake queue and a scoring model replace whoever shouts loudest.
  • Better results over time. Retrospectives and results reports feed the next brief, so the team learns from each campaign.

What makes marketing projects different

Marketing projects use the same building blocks as other projects, but six features change how you plan them.

Launch dates are fixed

Product launches, trade shows, seasonal sales and press moments set the date before the scope is known. That makes scope and the number of deliverables the main things that can flex.

Many reviewers

Brand, legal, product, sales, regional teams and senior leaders may all want to approve the same assets. Review rounds are where most marketing schedules slip.

Shared specialists

Designers, copywriters, video editors, web developers and marketing operations staff are split across many campaigns at once, so capacity, not budget, is often the binding constraint.

Compliance and claims

Regulated sectors such as financial services, healthcare, alcohol and gambling need claims checked and approvals recorded before anything goes live. Privacy and consent rules also affect email, tracking and data capture.

The project continues after launch

A campaign is optimised while live: budgets shift between channels, creative is refreshed and results are reported. Plan this phase, its owners and its reporting dates as part of the project.

Continuous volume

Alongside big campaigns there is a steady stream of small requests: social posts, sales collateral, event materials and web updates. Without an intake process, small requests quietly consume the capacity that planned campaigns need.

The marketing campaign life cycle: 7 phases

Most marketing projects follow the same seven phases. Small requests compress them into a day or two; a major launch may take months.

PhaseWhat happensMain output
1. Request and intakeA stakeholder asks for work; the team checks fit, priority and capacityAccepted, prioritised request
2. BriefObjective, audience, message, channels, budget, mandatory items, approvers and success measures agreedSigned campaign or creative brief
3. PlanningDeliverables listed, tasks and owners assigned, review steps and dates set back from the launch dateCampaign plan, schedule and budget
4. Creative developmentConcepts, copy, design, video and landing pages producedDraft assets
5. Review and approvalFeedback rounds from stakeholders, brand, legal and complianceApproved assets
6. LaunchAssets trafficked to channels, tracking tested, campaign goes liveLive campaign
7. Optimise, report and closePerformance monitored, budget shifted between channels, results and lessons reportedResults report and lessons learned

If you know the five standard project management phases, phases 1 and 2 are initiation, phase 3 is planning, phases 4 to 6 are execution with monitoring, and phase 7 is monitoring and closure.

How to plan a marketing campaign step by step

Use these ten steps for any campaign, scaling the documentation to its size.

  1. Confirm the business goal. Write the objective in measurable terms, such as 400 qualified leads or 2,000 event registrations, and agree how it will be measured.
  2. Write and sign the brief. Audience, key message, channels, deliverables, budget, mandatory items, launch date, approvers and the number of review rounds. Start from our creative brief template.
  3. List every deliverable. Each asset, size, language and channel. This is your scope; anything added later is a change.
  4. Break the work down. Turn deliverables into tasks with owners, from copy and design to tracking links and QA. A simple work breakdown structure helps on large campaigns.
  5. Map dependencies. Copy before design, landing page before paid media, tracking before launch. See task dependencies.
  6. Schedule backwards from the launch date. Place final approval, review rounds, production and briefing dates working back from go-live.
  7. Check capacity. Compare the plan with your specialists' availability across all campaigns; book freelancers or cut scope early if it does not fit.
  8. Agree the budget. Separate media spend from production costs and set a contingency.
  9. Run production and reviews to plan. Hold short weekly check-ins, keep status visible and handle changes through a change request.
  10. Launch, optimise and report. Test tracking before go-live, monitor results, report against the goal and run a short retrospective.

Planning backwards from the launch date

Because the launch date is fixed, marketing schedules work best when built backwards. Start at launch and place each milestone before it, including review time and buffers. If the first task lands in the past, you know immediately that scope must shrink or more capacity is needed.

  1. Fix the launch date and the final approval date (allow a few working days between them for trafficking and QA).
  2. Work back through each review round, adding reviewer turnaround time.
  3. Work back through production for each deliverable, using the longest chain of dependencies.
  4. Add concept and brief dates.
  5. Compare the first date with today. If it has passed, cut or phase deliverables now.

Here is a backwards plan for an illustrative email and paid social campaign launching on a Monday, counted in working days before launch:

Tip

If the dates do not fit, cut deliverables early rather than squeezing review time. A smaller campaign that launches well beats a full one with rushed, unchecked assets.

The same logic underpins the critical path method: find the longest chain of dependent tasks, because that chain sets the earliest possible launch.

Worked example: a product launch campaign

A B2B software company is launching a new reporting feature. The launch date is set for six weeks away to align with a customer conference. The marketing team has one marketing project manager, a product marketer, a content writer, a designer shared with two other teams, a marketing operations specialist and a paid media agency. This is an illustrative example, not a real company.

Brief summary

  • Objective: 300 demo requests from existing customers and prospects within six weeks of launch.
  • Audience: operations and finance leads in mid-sized companies; existing customers on older plans.
  • Key message: reports that used to take a day now take minutes.
  • Approvers: head of product marketing (messaging), brand lead (visuals), legal (customer claims). Two review rounds included.

Deliverables and owners

DeliverableOwnerDepends on
Messaging and positioning documentProduct marketerProduct briefing
Launch blog postContent writerMessaging
Landing page with demo formDesigner and marketing operationsMessaging, blog draft
Customer email (two sends)Content writer, marketing operationsLanding page
Paid social ads (six variants)Designer, paid media agencyKey visual, landing page
Sales one-pager and slidesProduct marketer, designerMessaging
Conference stand screen loopDesignerKey visual
Tracking and reporting dashboardMarketing operationsLanding page, UTM plan

Budget

LinePlannedNotes
Paid social media spend$12,000Split across two platforms
Agency fees$3,000Ad build and optimisation
Freelance video editing$1,500Short product clip
Contingency (about 10%)$1,650Held by the project manager
Total$18,150

What happened

Two weeks before launch, the product team renamed one feature. Because the brief named the product marketer as messaging owner, the change went through a quick change request: the copy was updated in round 2 rather than after approval, and the stand loop was finalised last because it was the least time-critical item. The shared designer was overbooked in week four, so the project manager agreed with the head of marketing to delay a lower-priority brochure for another team by a week rather than cut launch assets.

After launch, the team moved a share of paid budget from the weaker platform to the stronger one in week two. The retrospective recorded three lessons: get product naming frozen at brief sign-off, book shared design time at the planning stage, and test tracking a day earlier. Results were reported against the 300-demo objective, separately from delivery measures such as on-time launch and review rounds.

Request intake and prioritisation

Uncontrolled requests are the biggest threat to planned campaigns. Put every request through one intake route, usually a form, and review the queue at a fixed time each week.

What the intake form should capture

  • Requester, team and business goal the request supports.
  • What is needed: deliverables, formats and channels.
  • Audience and key message.
  • Deadline and the reason for it (a real event or a preference).
  • Budget, if any, and approvers.
  • Links to existing assets, brand guidelines and data.

Our work request template includes an intake form and request log you can adapt.

A simple prioritisation score

Score each request from 1 to 5 on three criteria, weight them and compare totals. Agree the weights with the marketing leader once, then apply them consistently.

RequestGoal impact (x3)Deadline urgency (x2)Effort, inverted (x1)Score
Launch landing page5 (15)5 (10)3 (3)28
Partner webinar promotion4 (12)3 (6)4 (4)22
Refresh of old brochure2 (6)1 (2)2 (2)10

Requests with the highest scores go first; low scorers are scheduled later, simplified or declined with a reason. For the wider discipline of juggling competing work, see managing multiple projects.

Approval workflows that do not stall

Approvals cause more marketing delays than production. These practices keep them moving:

  1. Name approvers in the brief. Separate who approves (one person per area: messaging, brand, legal) from who is consulted or informed. A RACI matrix makes this explicit.
  2. Cap and schedule rounds. Agree the number of rounds and put each review window in reviewers' calendars when the plan is approved.
  3. Consolidate feedback. One person per stakeholder group collects comments and resolves contradictions before they reach the creative team.
  4. Review on the asset. Use proofing so comments are pinned to the image, page or video, with versions tracked.
  5. Run compliance in parallel. Where possible, legal and brand review the same round as stakeholders rather than adding a round at the end.
  6. Record approval. Note who approved which version and when, especially in regulated sectors.
  7. Treat late changes as changes. Anything that alters the brief after sign-off goes through change control with an impact on time and cost.

Unmanaged additions after the brief is signed are a classic form of scope creep, so log every one, even when it looks small.

What does a marketing project manager do?

A marketing project manager turns briefs into plans and keeps campaigns moving. Day to day that means running intake and prioritisation, building schedules and budgets, booking specialist time, running review rounds, tracking status, managing risks and changes, coordinating launch, and reporting results with lessons learned. In smaller teams, marketing managers or marketing operations staff often do this alongside their main role.

Marketing project manager vs marketing manager

A marketing manager owns strategy and results: which audiences, messages and channels to use, and whether the campaign hit its goal. A marketing project manager owns delivery: the plan, schedule, resources, approvals and launch. The roles work as partners, and in small teams one person often does both, which is why separating strategic and delivery decisions in the brief helps.

Methodologies that fit marketing

Choose a method by how work arrives and how fixed the dates are.

ApproachWhen to use itHow it looks
KanbanOngoing, request-driven work such as content, social and design supportBoard with Intake, Brief, In progress, Review, Approved, Live; WIP limits per stage
Scrum or Agile sprintsGrowth, digital and content teams that can plan in two-week cycles and test and learnBacklog, sprint planning, review and retrospective; see Agile marketing
WaterfallLarge, fixed-date launches with long lead-time items such as print, events or TVPhased plan with dependencies and a Gantt chart
HybridMost marketing teamsA milestone plan for the launch with Kanban or sprints for the creative work inside it

Whatever the method, make work visible on one board or plan, set clear intake rules and agree review rounds up front. WIP limits stop the team starting more than it can finish.

Managing many campaigns at once

Most marketing teams run a portfolio rather than a single project. Four habits keep it under control:

  • One marketing calendar. Show every campaign launch, major content piece and event on a single shared calendar so clashes are visible early.
  • A capacity view across campaigns. Plan specialist time across all work, not campaign by campaign. See capacity planning for the method.
  • A weekly prioritisation meeting. Short and regular, with the marketing leader deciding trade-offs using the agreed scoring model.
  • Templates for repeat work. Webinar promotions, product updates and newsletters follow the same steps each time; save them as templates with tasks, owners and relative dates.
Tip

Keep a small reserve of capacity, for example part of each specialist's week, for genuinely urgent requests. It is easier to protect planned campaigns when there is an agreed place for the unexpected.

KPIs for marketing project management

Separate delivery KPIs (how well the team runs projects) from campaign KPIs (how well the campaign performed). A campaign can launch perfectly and still miss its goal, or succeed despite a chaotic process; you need both views to improve.

Using AI in marketing project management

Many project management and marketing platforms now include AI features. Used sensibly, they save administrative time. Practical uses include:

  • Drafting a first version of a brief or task list from meeting notes, for the team to edit.
  • Summarising long comment threads and status updates for stakeholders.
  • Suggesting subtasks and dates from a campaign template.
  • Flagging tasks at risk of missing deadlines based on progress data.
  • Producing first drafts of copy variants for testing, with human review.

Keep people in charge of decisions, claims and brand judgement. Check AI-generated copy for accuracy and compliance, and follow your organisation's rules on what data can be shared with AI tools.

Marketing project management software

Look for a tool that offers:

  • Request forms with required fields and automatic routing.
  • Calendar and timeline views to see every launch in one place.
  • Proofing and approvals with comments on the asset and recorded sign-off.
  • Workload views across campaigns.
  • Templates for repeat campaigns with relative dates.
  • Dashboards for status and delivery KPIs.
  • Integrations with design tools, file storage or a DAM, marketing automation and chat.

Wrike is a strong all-in-one choice for marketing teams because it combines request forms, built-in proofing and approvals, workload management, dashboards and reusable campaign templates in one platform, and it ranks first in our project management software rankings. monday.com is popular with marketing teams for its visual boards and easy automations, and Asana suits teams that want straightforward task, timeline and goal tracking. Most teams also run specialist tools alongside: a marketing automation platform, analytics and often a digital asset management (DAM) system.

Small teams can start with a shared calendar, a campaign plan template and a request form, then move to a dedicated platform when the number of campaigns and reviewers grows.

Skills and career path

Marketing project managers combine core project management skills, such as planning, scheduling, risk and stakeholder management, with marketing knowledge: channels, creative production, tracking and how campaigns are measured. Calm negotiation matters, because you will often balance senior requests against limited capacity.

Many people move into the role from marketing coordination, account management or marketing operations. Certification is not required; practical experience counts most. A general project management certification or Agile training can help as campaigns get larger.

Frequently asked questions

What is marketing project management?

Marketing project management is the planning, scheduling, resourcing and tracking of marketing work, from single assets to multi-channel campaigns, so it launches on time, within budget and against a measurable business goal.

What does a marketing project manager do?

They run intake and prioritisation, turn briefs into plans and budgets, book specialist time, run review and approval rounds, track status, manage changes and risks, coordinate the launch and report results with lessons learned.

What is the difference between a marketing manager and a marketing project manager?

A marketing manager owns strategy and results: audiences, messages, channels and whether the goal was met. A marketing project manager owns delivery: the plan, schedule, resources, approvals and launch. In small teams one person often does both.

What are the phases of a marketing project?

Request and intake, brief, planning, creative development, review and approval, launch, and optimise, report and close.

What is an example of a marketing project?

A product launch campaign is a typical example: a brief with a measurable goal, a landing page, emails, paid social ads, sales materials and tracking, all planned backwards from a fixed launch date and reported against the goal afterwards.

How far ahead should you plan a marketing campaign?

It depends on scope and lead times. Build the plan backwards from the launch date, including review rounds and buffers. If the first task already falls in the past, reduce or phase the deliverables. Print, events and video usually need the longest lead times.

Is Agile good for marketing project management?

Yes, especially for request-driven and test-and-learn work. Many teams use Kanban or sprints for production and keep a milestone plan for big launches. See Agile marketing.

How do you prioritise marketing requests?

Send every request through one intake form, score it against agreed criteria such as goal impact, deadline urgency and effort, and review the queue weekly with the marketing leader. Low scorers are scheduled later, simplified or declined with a reason.

How do you reduce approval delays?

Name approvers in the brief, schedule review windows in their calendars, consolidate feedback through one person per group, cap the number of rounds, run legal and brand checks in parallel and use proofing so comments sit on the asset.

Which KPIs matter in marketing project management?

Track delivery KPIs such as on-time launch rate, review rounds per asset, cycle time, throughput and budget variance, and separately track campaign KPIs such as leads, cost per lead, conversion rate and ROI.

How do you manage several marketing campaigns at once?

Use one shared marketing calendar, plan specialist capacity across all campaigns, hold a short weekly prioritisation meeting and save repeat campaigns as templates. See managing multiple projects.

What is the best software for marketing project management?

Choose a tool that combines request forms, proofing, calendars, workload views and templates. Wrike is a strong all-in-one option, with monday.com and Asana also popular. Compare them in our tool rankings.

Do you need a certification for marketing project management?

No. Practical experience matters most. A general project management certification or Agile training can help as you take on larger campaigns; see project management certification.
