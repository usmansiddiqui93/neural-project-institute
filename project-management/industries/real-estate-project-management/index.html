Real Estate Project Management: Phases, Roles and Examples

Real estate project management is the coordination of a property development or major property improvement from site acquisition and appraisal through planning, finance, construction and leasing or sales to a stabilised or sold asset. Where construction project management focuses on building the asset, real estate project management keeps the investment case at the centre of every decision. This guide covers the life cycle, roles, a worked appraisal, KPIs, risks, templates and software.

Key takeaways

  • Real estate project management judges success on profit, yield and value, not only on time, cost and quality.
  • The life cycle runs from acquisition and appraisal through planning, finance and construction to leasing, sales and exit.
  • Time is money: finance costs, extended site costs and delayed income make every month of delay expensive.
  • Use stage gates tied to the appraisal so each major commitment (purchase, planning, contract, finance) is a conscious decision.
  • Keep the numbers in your appraisal model and use a platform such as Wrike to coordinate advisers, approvals and reporting across schemes.

What is real estate project management?

Real estate project management is the planning and control of a property project on behalf of its owner or investor, from finding and appraising a site to letting, selling or holding the finished asset. It combines standard project management disciplines with development finance, planning, legal work and market risk, so every decision is tested against the expected return.

In practice the work is usually led by a development manager on the developer's side, supported by a client-side project manager who runs design and construction. The development manager asks "does this still make money?"; the construction team asks "can we build it on time, on budget and to the right quality?". Both questions matter, and good real estate project management keeps them connected.

Main goalBuild the asset to time, cost and qualityDeliver the investment return the asset was bought for
StartsAt design or contract awardAt site search and acquisition
EndsPractical completion and handoverStabilised income, sale or transfer to asset management

What makes real estate projects different

  • The return is the goal. A scheme that finishes on time and on budget can still fail if rents, sale prices or yields have moved against it.
  • Finance shapes the timeline. Debt and equity are drawn against milestones and interest accrues every month, so delay has a direct cost even if nothing else changes.
  • Market risk. Values and demand can shift between purchase and completion, which may be several years.
  • Planning and legal risk. Planning permission or zoning approval, title, easements, rights of light, party walls and leases all need to be resolved, often by different advisers.
  • Many advisers on separate appointments. Agents, lawyers, valuers, cost consultants, designers and contractors each hold part of the picture, and nobody sees all of it unless you create that view.
  • Revenue workstreams. Leasing or sales marketing runs alongside construction and must be timed so buyers or tenants can move in as soon as the building is ready.
Tip

Link every scope or programme decision back to the appraisal. A two-month delay can matter more than a small cost saving once finance costs and lost rent or sales are counted.

The real estate development life cycle

Most property projects move through eight phases. Some overlap (financing often runs alongside design, and leasing starts during construction), but each ends with a clear decision.

PhaseWhat happensKey outputsDecision at the end
1. Sourcing and acquisitionFind a site, negotiate terms, legal and technical due diligenceHeads of terms, due diligence reports, purchase completionBuy, buy conditionally or walk away
2. Feasibility and appraisalTest schemes and model costs, values and returnsDevelopment appraisal, residual land value, outline business planProceed to planning or not
3. Planning and entitlementsDesign for planning, pre-application talks, consultation, application, agreements with the authorityPlanning permission or zoning approval, conditions and obligationsAccept the consent and its costs
4. Design and procurementDetailed design, cost plan, contractor tenderBuilding contract, cost plan, construction programmeAward the contract
5. FinancingSecure debt and equity against the approved schemeFacility agreement, drawdown scheduleFinancial close
6. ConstructionBuild the scheme; monitor cost, programme and quality for the lender and investorsMonitoring reports, drawdowns, practical completionAccept practical completion
7. Leasing, sales and stabilisationMarketing, lettings or sales, tenant fit-outsSigned leases or sale completions, stabilised incomeDeclare stabilisation
8. Hold or exitHand over to asset and property management, or sellAsset management plan or saleHold, refinance or sell

Front end: acquisition, appraisal and planning

The first three phases carry the most uncertainty and the least spend, which is exactly when good project discipline pays off. Keep a due diligence tracker, a single action log for all advisers and a version-controlled appraisal. Where you can, buy subject to planning (a conditional contract or option) so the biggest risk is resolved before the biggest payment. A short feasibility study at this stage tests whether the scheme works before you commit fees.

Middle: design, procurement, finance and construction

Once planning is secured, the project looks more like a conventional construction project: design stages, tenders, contracts and a site programme. The difference is that the lender and investors now watch closely. Expect a monitoring surveyor to review every drawdown request, and agree reporting formats before work starts. Our guide to project procurement management covers tendering and contract selection.

Back end: leasing, sales and exit

Revenue work should start well before completion. Pre-lets and off-plan sales reduce risk and often improve finance terms. Plan show flats, marketing suites, tenant fit-out windows and handover packs as tasks with owners, not as afterthoughts. The project ends when income is stable or the asset is sold, followed by a lessons learned review.

If you know the classic project management phases, phases 1 to 2 are initiation, 3 to 5 planning, 6 to 7 execution with continuous monitoring, and 8 closure.

How to manage a real estate development project step by step

Use these steps for any scheme, scaling the paperwork to the size of the deal.

  1. Set investment criteria. Agree target returns, location, asset type, maximum land price and risk appetite with the investors before you look at sites.
  2. Screen and secure the site. Run a quick appraisal, agree heads of terms and secure exclusivity or a conditional contract.
  3. Run due diligence as a tracked workstream. Title, searches, surveys, environmental and ground reports, planning history and rights of light. Track each item with an owner and a date in a project tracker.
  4. Build the appraisal and the business case. Model value, costs, finance and profit, then test sensitivities. Record the approved figures as your baseline in a business case.
  5. Set up governance. Define who approves what, the stage gates and tolerances (for example, any change above 2% of the budget goes to the investment committee). See project governance.
  6. Appoint the team. Planning consultant, architect, cost consultant, lawyers and agents, each with a clear scope. A RACI matrix prevents gaps between appointments.
  7. Create one integrated programme. Acquisition, planning, design, procurement, finance, construction, marketing and handover on a single timeline, using a Gantt chart template.
  8. Secure planning and discharge conditions. Treat pre-commencement conditions and legal agreements as critical-path tasks.
  9. Procure and contract. Tender with a clear specification and a request for quote for smaller packages; choose the contract route that fits your risk appetite.
  10. Reach financial close. Agree the facility, drawdown process, reporting and conditions precedent.
  11. Control construction against the appraisal. Report cost to complete, programme and risk monthly, and route every change through change control.
  12. Drive revenue and handover. Run leasing or sales to a plan, coordinate fit-outs and completions, and hand over manuals and warranties.
  13. Close and learn. Compare actual returns with the appraisal, capture lessons and update your cost and programme benchmarks.

Worked example: a development appraisal and the cost of delay

The numbers below are illustrative, rounded for clarity and not market benchmarks. They show how a development manager uses a simple residual appraisal to set a land price and then to judge the cost of a delay.

Step 1: calculate the residual land value

A developer is appraising a 40-home scheme. Each home is expected to sell for £350,000, so the gross development value (GDV) is £14,000,000. The developer's target profit is 17.5% of GDV.

LineBasisAmount
Gross development value (GDV)40 homes × £350,000£14,000,000
Construction costFrom cost consultant's estimate£6,400,000
Professional fees10% of construction£640,000
Contingency5% of construction£320,000
Planning obligations and leviesEstimate from planning consultant£400,000
Sales and marketing2.5% of GDV£350,000
Finance costsFrom finance model£600,000
Total costs excluding landSum of the above£8,710,000
Developer's profit17.5% of GDV£2,450,000
Residual land valueGDV − costs − profit£2,840,000

The residual of £2,840,000 is the most the developer can pay for the land, before purchase costs such as taxes and legal fees, and still hit the target profit. If the seller wants more, the scheme, the price or the target has to change.

Step 2: check profit on cost

Investors often ask for profit on cost as well as profit on GDV. Total cost including land is £8,710,000 + £2,840,000 = £11,550,000. Profit on cost is £2,450,000 ÷ £11,550,000 = 21.2%. The two measures describe the same profit, so always state which one you are reporting.

Step 3: price a three-month delay

Suppose a planning condition takes three months longer to discharge, pushing back construction and sales. Two costs appear straight away:

Cost of delayCalculationAmount
Extra interest£7,000,000 average drawn debt × 8% a year × 3/12£140,000
Extended site and team costs£45,000 a month × 3 months£135,000
Total£275,000

Profit falls from £2,450,000 to £2,175,000, so profit on GDV drops from 17.5% to about 15.5%. A value engineering saving of £150,000 that added three months would therefore lose money overall. This is why development managers price time explicitly and why a sensitivity analysis on programme belongs in every appraisal.

Step 4: yield on cost for an income-producing scheme

For a scheme you plan to hold, such as an office let to tenants, the headline measure is yield on cost: stabilised annual net rent divided by total development cost. A building that costs £20,000,000 and produces £1,200,000 a year in net rent has a yield on cost of 6.0%. Investors compare that with the yield at which similar buildings sell; the gap is the value created by developing rather than buying.

Development manager vs project manager vs developer

The developer is the business that takes the risk and the profit. The development manager acts for the developer across the whole life cycle, from deal to exit, and is judged on returns. The project manager usually focuses on design and construction and is judged on time, cost and quality. On small schemes one person may do two or all three jobs; on large ones each is a separate team.

Skills a real estate project manager needs

  • Financial literacy: reading an appraisal, cash flow and cost report, and explaining the effect of a change on returns.
  • Planning and legal awareness: enough to know when to call the specialist and how long their work takes.
  • Programme management: linking acquisition, planning, construction and revenue into one schedule.
  • Commercial negotiation: with sellers, contractors, tenants and buyers.
  • Stakeholder management: investors, lenders, local authorities, neighbours and tenants; see stakeholder management.
  • Risk judgement: spotting market, planning and cost risks early and setting trigger points.

Governance: stage gates and investment approvals

Real estate projects commit large sums in discrete steps. A simple stage-gate process makes each step a deliberate decision rather than momentum. The gates below are typical; adjust them to your organisation.

GateBefore you…What the approval paper should show
Gate 1: acquisitionExchange or complete on the landAppraisal, due diligence findings, planning view, key risks, funding route
Gate 2: planning submissionSpend on a full planning applicationScheme design, updated appraisal, planning strategy and obligations estimate
Gate 3: contract awardSign the building contractTender results, cost plan, programme, contract terms, contingency
Gate 4: financial closeDraw the first debtFacility terms, drawdown schedule, investor approvals
Gate 5: practical completionAccept the buildingDefects, certificates, handover information, revenue status
Gate 6: exit or holdSell, refinance or transferActual vs appraised returns, market advice, hold strategy

Keep each paper short: what has changed since the last gate, the updated appraisal, the top five risks and the decision required. An executive summary template works well for investor reporting between gates.

Tip

Set trigger points in advance, for example "if build cost tenders exceed the cost plan by more than 5%, or sales values fall more than 5%, we re-appraise before contract award". Agreed triggers make difficult decisions faster and less personal.

Planning, permits and regulatory gateways

Planning and permitting are often the longest and least predictable part of a real estate programme. The detail depends on where you build, so take local advice, but the project management principles are the same.

  • Start with pre-application discussions. Early feedback from the planning authority reduces redesign and helps you price obligations.
  • Programme the whole consent, not just the decision. Include consultation, committee dates, legal agreements, conditions discharge and any appeal or challenge periods.
  • Price obligations early. In the UK, planning obligations and the Community Infrastructure Levy can be significant; in the US, entitlement conditions, impact fees and zoning variances play a similar role.
  • Track conditions as tasks. Pre-commencement conditions can stop a site from starting even after permission is granted.

Building Safety Act gateways in England

In England, the Building Safety Act 2022 introduced three gateways for higher-risk buildings (broadly, residential buildings of at least 18 metres or seven storeys with two or more homes). Gateway 1 adds a fire statement at planning; at gateway 2 the Building Safety Regulator must approve the design before construction starts; at gateway 3 the regulator must issue a completion certificate before the building can be occupied. Industry and legal commentary through 2025 and 2026 has reported long decision times at gateways 2 and 3, so allow realistic time for both in the programme and in the finance model, and check the regulator's current guidance for your scheme.

Common mistakes in real estate project management

  • Treating the appraisal as a one-off. It should be updated at every gate and whenever a major cost, value or date changes.
  • Ignoring time in value engineering. Savings that add months can cost more in interest and lost income than they save.
  • Separate programmes for each adviser. Without one integrated programme, planning, legal and construction dates drift apart.
  • Starting marketing too late. Revenue is the reason for the project; plan it alongside construction.
  • Unclear authority. If nobody knows who can approve a change of specification, decisions stall or happen without control.
  • Thin contingency. Price risk realistically rather than hoping for the best, and hold a costed contingency plan for the largest risks.

Frequently asked questions

What is real estate project management?

Real estate project management coordinates a property development or improvement from acquisition and appraisal through planning, finance, construction and leasing or sales to a stabilised or sold asset, with the investment return as the main measure of success.

What does a real estate project manager do?

They plan and control the project for the owner: coordinating advisers and contractors, managing the programme and budget, reporting to investors and lenders, managing risks and changes, and making sure the building is delivered in time for tenants or buyers.

What is the difference between a real estate developer and a project manager?

The developer owns the opportunity, takes the financial risk and keeps the profit. The project manager (or development manager) runs the project on the developer's behalf and is accountable for delivery against the approved plan.

What is the difference between a development manager and a construction project manager?

A development manager runs the whole life cycle from acquisition to exit and is judged on returns. A construction project manager focuses on design and construction and is judged on time, cost and quality.

What are the phases of real estate development?

Sourcing and acquisition, feasibility and appraisal, planning and entitlements, design and procurement, financing, construction, leasing or sales and stabilisation, and hold or exit.

How do you manage a real estate development project?

Set investment criteria, secure the site, run due diligence, build the appraisal, set up stage gates, appoint the team, create one integrated programme, secure planning, procure, reach financial close, control construction against the appraisal, drive leasing or sales and close with a review.

How do you calculate residual land value?

Take the gross development value and subtract all development costs (construction, fees, contingency, obligations, sales costs and finance) and the developer's target profit. What remains is the most you can pay for the land before purchase costs.

What is the difference between profit on cost and profit on GDV?

Profit on cost divides profit by total development cost including land; profit on GDV divides the same profit by the scheme's gross development value. Profit on cost is always the higher percentage, so state which one you mean.

What KPIs are used in real estate project management?

Profit on GDV, profit on cost, IRR, yield on cost, cost to complete against budget, pre-let or pre-sales levels, lease-up or sales velocity and programme variance.

What skills do you need for real estate project management?

Financial literacy, planning and legal awareness, programme management, negotiation, stakeholder management and sound risk judgement.

How do I get into real estate project management?

Common routes include construction project management, quantity surveying, planning, architecture and real estate degrees. A recognised project management qualification and experience on a live scheme both help.

What software do real estate developers use?

Appraisal and financial modelling tools, construction management platforms, property and asset management systems, and a work management platform such as Wrike to coordinate tasks, approvals and reporting across schemes.
