Video Production Project Management: Complete Guide

Video production project management is the planning and control of a video from the first brief through pre-production, the shoot and post-production to final delivery, so that it meets the brief on time and on budget. Because shoot days are expensive and hard to repeat, most of the work happens before the camera rolls, and most overruns happen in post-production when review rounds are not controlled. This guide gives you a five-phase workflow, roles, a step-by-step process, a worked budget and schedule, a pre-production checklist, delivery specs, KPIs and tools.

Key takeaways

  • Put most of your planning effort into pre-production: problems solved on paper are cheap, problems solved on set are not.
  • Gate the project at treatment, script, rough cut and picture lock, with one named approver at each gate.
  • Build the budget line by line, split above-the-line and below-the-line costs and hold a contingency.
  • Agree the number of review rounds per cut in the contract and treat extra rounds and late changes after picture lock as change requests.
  • Releases, licences, insurance, risk assessments and data backups are project management tasks, not afterthoughts.
  • Write the deliverables list (formats, aspect ratios, captions, loudness) at the start, because it drives how you shoot and edit.

What is video production project management?

Video production project management is the process of planning, budgeting, scheduling, resourcing and controlling a video project, from concept and pre-production through filming and post-production to delivery and archiving. It coordinates creative people, crew, talent, equipment, locations and client approvals so that the finished video meets the brief, on time and within budget.

It applies to brand films, adverts, corporate and training videos, documentaries, music videos, event coverage and the steady stream of short-form social video that in-house teams produce. Whether one person or a team of 40 is involved, the same disciplines apply, scaled to the size and risk of the job.

What makes video projects different

Video shares a lot with other creative project management, but five features change how you plan it.

  • Fixed, expensive shoot days. Crew, kit, talent and locations are booked for specific days. A lost day is a large, often unrecoverable cost, so everything before the shoot is about removing risk from it.
  • Physical logistics. Locations, permits, travel, catering, insurance, parking, power and weather all need planning, which makes a shoot closer to an event project than to office work.
  • Rights and releases. Contributors, locations, music, stock footage and archive material need signed releases or licences before the video can be used, and the usage terms (media, territory, duration) must match how the client will publish it.
  • Iterative post-production. Edits go through several cuts with feedback at each. The number of rounds, and how late changes arrive, drives most post-production overruns.
  • Technical delivery. Each channel needs its own formats, aspect ratios, captions and audio levels, so the deliverables list affects how you shoot, not just how you export.

The five phases of video production

Most video projects move through five phases. Small social videos compress them into days; broadcast and brand work adds formal gates and more paperwork.

1. DevelopmentBrief, objectives, audience, concept, treatment, initial budget and timelineSigned brief, treatment, budget estimateTreatment and budget approved by the client or commissioner
2. Pre-productionScript, storyboard, shot list, casting, recces, permits, crew and kit booking, insurance, risk assessment, scheduleApproved script, shooting schedule, call sheets, releases readyScript and storyboard approved; green light to shoot
3. ProductionShoot days, directing, sound, data management, daily progress reportsRushes backed up, production reports, signed releasesProducer confirms all required shots and paperwork captured
4. Post-productionLogging, assembly, rough cut, fine cut, picture lock, grade, sound mix, graphics, captionsApproved masterRough cut, fine cut and picture lock approved; master signed off

If you know the standard project management phases, development is initiation, pre-production is planning, production and post-production are execution with tight monitoring, and delivery is closure.

Tip

Never book crew or locations before the treatment, script and budget are approved. Every change after booking costs money in cancellation fees, rebooking or wasted preparation.

Producer vs line producer

The producer decides what the project is and makes the big trade-offs: creative scope, budget level, key hires and client expectations. The line producer (often called production manager on smaller jobs) turns that into a workable plan and keeps it on budget: every day of crew, every kit hire and every location fee. In-house teams often have a single video producer or project manager doing both, with a coordinator on larger shoots.

How to manage a video production project step by step

Use this process for any video, scaling the paperwork to the size of the job.

  1. Capture the brief. Agree objective, audience, key message, tone, length, channels, deliverables, budget, deadline and the named approver. A creative brief template keeps every request consistent.
  2. Develop and approve the treatment. Describe the creative approach, style, references and structure. Approve it with a first budget estimate before writing the script.
  3. Build the budget line by line. Separate above-the-line and below-the-line costs, add post-production and licences, and hold a contingency. A project cost estimate template helps.
  4. Write and approve the script and storyboard. Lock what will be filmed and said. Changes after this point affect bookings and cost.
  5. Break down the script into a shot list. List every shot or setup with location, cast, kit and special requirements.
  6. Schedule backwards from delivery. Place final delivery, then the master approval, picture lock, review rounds, edit time and shoot days. Find the critical path to the first shoot day, which usually runs through permits, casting and key crew availability.
  7. Book people, kit and locations. Confirm in writing with dates, rates, cancellation terms and overtime rules.
  8. Complete the legal and safety work. Risk assessment, insurance, permits, release forms and music licences, with owners and due dates.
  9. Issue call sheets and run the shoot. Track progress against the shot list every few hours, back up footage at each card change and collect releases on the day.
  10. Run post-production in controlled rounds. Rough cut, fine cut and picture lock, each with one consolidated set of notes from one contact by an agreed date.
  11. Finish, version and quality-check. Grade, mix, graphics and captions, then produce every version on the deliverables list and check each against the spec.
  12. Deliver, archive and review. Deliver files, log rights and usage terms, archive project files and rushes, and hold a short wash-up to capture lessons learned.

What an extra review round costs

In this example each additional edit round takes about one editor day (£450) plus half a producer day (£225), so £675 before any grade, mix or graphics rework. A change after picture lock can cost several times that, because the grade, mix, graphics and captions may all need to be redone. Showing this calculation to the client before the edit starts is one of the most effective ways to keep feedback consolidated and on time.

A video budget is built bottom-up from the script and schedule, not top-down from a guess.

  • Hold a contingency of around 10% as a starting point, more for outdoor shoots, complex logistics or untested formats.
  • Update a cost report after every shoot day and at each post-production gate, comparing actuals with the approved budget line by line.
  • Price changes before agreeing them. Every client request after script approval should come back with a time and cost impact.

Scheduling the shoot

The shooting schedule groups scenes by location, cast and setup rather than by script order, to reduce moves and talent days.

  1. List every setup. A setup is a camera and lighting position; several shots may come from one setup.
  2. Estimate time per setup. Simple interviews or pick-ups may take 15 to 20 minutes; lit dialogue scenes or moving shots can take an hour or more. Use your DoP's estimates.
  3. Group by location and lighting. Shoot everything in one place and one direction before moving.
  4. Schedule talent efficiently. Group each actor's scenes to reduce paid days.
  5. Protect the hardest shots. Put the most important or complex setups early in the day, when the crew is fresh and light is predictable.
  6. Add moves, meals and margin. Include company moves between locations, meal breaks and a buffer for the unexpected.
  7. Plan weather cover. For exterior work, keep an interior cover set or a contingency day available.

For example, a ten-hour day with a one-hour lunch and two 30-minute company moves leaves eight hours of shooting. At an average of 40 minutes per setup that is 12 setups. If the shot list has 30 setups across two days, you are six short: you either simplify setups, combine shots or add a half-day before anyone arrives on set.

Use a project schedule template or a Gantt chart template for the overall plan and a separate shooting schedule for the shoot days.

Managing shoot days

The call sheet

A call sheet is sent the day before each shoot day. It typically includes the date, call times for each person, location addresses and maps, parking, contacts, the day's schedule and scenes, cast, key kit, weather, sunrise and sunset, nearest hospital and safety notes.

Track progress during the day

  • Check progress against the shot list at agreed points, for example mid-morning, lunch and mid-afternoon.
  • If you are behind, decide early with the director what to simplify, combine or drop, rather than running into overtime.
  • Record what was shot, any issues and overtime in a short daily production report.

Protect the footage

Footage is the only output of an expensive day, so data management is a project control, not just a technical task. A common rule of thumb is to keep at least two verified copies on separate drives before a camera card is formatted, with a third copy off site as soon as practical. Use a consistent folder and file naming convention agreed with the editor.

Post-production: controlling cuts and review rounds

Post-production is where most video budgets are lost, usually through extra review rounds, conflicting feedback and changes after picture lock.

CutPurposeWhat feedback is appropriate
AssemblyAll usable takes in script order (often internal only)Structure and missing material
Rough cutStory, structure and pacingStory, order, length, messaging
Fine cutRefined edit with temporary graphics and musicDetail of shots, timing, graphics content
Picture lockEdit fixed; no more changes to timingApproval only
Online and finishingGrade, sound mix, final graphics, captionsTechnical and brand checks
Master and versionsFinal files and cut-downsQuality control against the spec
  • Agree the number of review rounds per cut in the contract or statement of work, for example two rounds at rough cut and one at fine cut.
  • Name one contact who consolidates all notes into a single document with timecodes.
  • Set turnaround dates for feedback and show what happens to the delivery date if they slip.
  • Use a review tool that lets reviewers comment on specific frames, so notes are precise.
  • Treat changes after picture lock as change requests with a cost and time impact, using a change request template.
Tip

Write picture lock into the schedule as a milestone with a named approver and date. Changes after lock ripple into the grade, mix, graphics and captions, and are where post budgets usually overrun.

Common video production mistakes

  • Booking before approval. Crew and locations booked before the script is signed off lead to cancellation fees or rushed shoots.
  • An over-full shot list. Too many setups per day leads to overtime, rushed performances and missed shots.
  • No weather or illness cover. Exterior shoots need an interior cover set or a contingency day.
  • Missing releases. Collect signatures on the day; chasing them afterwards is slow and sometimes impossible.
  • Single copy of footage. Back up before cards are reused.
  • Too many reviewers. Uncoordinated notes from several people create conflicting changes and extra rounds.
  • Deliverables agreed at the end. Late requests for vertical or square versions may need reframing that the footage cannot support.
  • Uncontrolled scope creep. Extra versions and late changes added without a cost conversation erode the budget.

Software for video production project management

Video teams usually combine a project management platform with specialist production tools. In the project management platform, look for:

  • Timeline and calendar views for production schedules and shoot days.
  • Request forms so every video starts with a complete brief.
  • Resource and workload views for crew, editors and kit.
  • Proofing with frame-accurate comments on video, plus version history and recorded approvals.
  • Budget or cost tracking, or an easy link to the budget spreadsheet.
  • Templates for repeat formats and checklists for pre-production and delivery.

Frequently asked questions

What is video production project management?

It is the planning, budgeting, scheduling and control of a video project from concept through pre-production, the shoot and post-production to final delivery, so that the video meets the brief on time and on budget.

What are the stages of video production?

The usual stages are development, pre-production, production (the shoot), post-production, and delivery and archive. Each ends with an approval gate, such as treatment, script, picture lock and master sign-off.

What does a video producer do?

A producer owns the project: budget, schedule, hiring, client relationship and delivery. On larger shoots a line producer or production manager runs the budget and logistics day to day, supported by a production coordinator.

What is the difference between a producer and a line producer?

The producer decides what the project is and makes the major creative and budget trade-offs. The line producer turns that into a workable plan and keeps every day of crew, kit and location on budget.

How do you budget for a video production?

Build it line by line from the script and schedule: above-the-line costs (producer, director, talent, usage), below-the-line costs (crew, kit, locations, insurance), post-production and licences, plus a contingency of around 10% as a starting point.

How long does a video production take?

It depends on length, complexity and approvals. A simple social video can take days; a two-minute brand film with a two-day shoot often takes six to ten weeks from brief to delivery, with approvals usually the biggest variable.

What is a call sheet?

A document sent before each shoot day with call times, locations, contacts, schedule, scenes, cast, weather, sunrise and sunset, and safety information such as the nearest hospital.

What is picture lock?

Picture lock is the point at which the edit is approved and no further timing changes are made. Grading, sound mixing, final graphics and captions are completed after lock, so later changes are expensive.

How many revision rounds should a video include?

There is no standard number. A common approach is two rounds at rough cut and one at fine cut, agreed in the contract. Extra rounds and changes after picture lock should be priced as change requests.

What should a video deliverables list include?

Every version and cut-down, aspect ratios, codec, resolution and frame rate, captions and subtitles, loudness targets, graphics and legal text, and which files will be archived.

What is the difference between above-the-line and below-the-line costs?

Above-the-line costs cover the key creative people and talent, such as producer, director, writer and lead cast. Below-the-line costs cover crew, equipment, locations, logistics and post-production.

Is Agile useful in video production?

Partly. Individual productions are phased because shoot days are fixed, but post-production works in short review cycles, and in-house content teams often run Kanban boards from request to published.

What software is used for video production project management?

Teams combine a project management platform for schedules, requests and approvals, such as Wrike, with specialist tools for script breakdown, scheduling, budgeting, editing and video review.
