Consulting Project Management: Phases, Roles and Methods

Consulting project management is the planning and control of advisory engagements, where a consulting team helps a client diagnose a problem, recommend what to do and often support the change, within an agreed fee and timeline. It differs from most project work because the question is not fully known at the start and the main deliverable is insight the client must accept and act on. This guide covers the engagement life cycle, hypothesis-driven workplans, fee build-up, governance, scope control and tools, with worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • Consulting project management turns a client question into a dated, owned workplan and delivers an answer the client accepts, within the fee.
  • Frame the key question in writing at kick-off; most consulting overruns start with a vague or moving question.
  • A hypothesis-driven workplan (issue tree, hypotheses, analyses) keeps short engagements focused on work that could change the answer.
  • Track days used against earned progress every week, not just days against budget, so a fee overrun shows up at week 3 rather than week 7.
  • Share emerging findings with the sponsor early and often: a final presentation with no surprises is a sign of a well-run engagement.
  • Use a work management platform such as Wrike for workplans, resourcing and client collaboration, and add PSA or finance tools for billing.

What is consulting project management?

Consulting project management is the discipline of running an advisory engagement so the team answers the client's question, produces recommendations the client accepts and stays within the agreed fee and timeline. It combines standard project management controls with structured problem solving, close client collaboration and commercial control of a fee.

It applies to strategy and management consultancies, operations and technology advisers, transformation teams, internal consulting units and independent consultants. The person accountable is usually called the engagement manager or project lead; on larger engagements a separate project manager may run the plan, risks and reporting for them.

Main commercial riskBudget overrunUnpaid extra work on a fixed fee; lost follow-on work

Consulting project management vs project management consulting

The two phrases are often confused. Consulting project management (this guide) is how a consulting firm runs its own engagements. Project management consulting is a service in which a firm is hired to improve a client's project capability, for example to set up a project management office, recover a failing programme or introduce a methodology. Project management consultants run their own engagements using exactly the methods below.

What makes consulting engagements different

The core toolkit is the same as any project. Six features of consulting work change where an engagement manager spends attention.

The question is not fully known at the start

A client may ask why margins are falling or how to enter a new market. The real question often sharpens once the first data arrives. Scope is therefore written as questions to answer and deliverables to produce, not as a full list of tasks, and the plan is refined as findings emerge.

Short, intense engagements

Many diagnostic and strategy phases run for 4 to 12 weeks with a team of three to six people. There is little room to recover from a slow first week, so mobilisation and data access matter more than on a long project.

Client-facing every day

Consultants often work on the client site with client staff, interview executives and present to boards. Stakeholder management is a daily activity, not a monthly report.

Credibility is the deliverable

Recommendations only create value if the client trusts the analysis and acts on it. Quality of evidence, clear storylines and involving the people who will implement the change are part of delivery.

The fee is fixed before the answer is known

Most engagements are sold at a fixed fee or a capped estimate. Extra analysis that feels useful but will not change the answer comes straight out of the firm's margin.

You depend on client resources you do not control

Data, interviews and decisions come from client staff who also have day jobs. A PMI congress paper on project management for consultants lists scarce client resources and resistance to change among the profession's recurring challenges, and both need to be planned for, not hoped away.

The consulting engagement life cycle

Firms use different names, but most engagements pass through the same seven phases. Diagnostic and strategy work may stop after phase 5; implementation engagements continue into phase 6.

PhaseWhat happensEngagement manager focusKey output
1. ProposalUnderstand the client issue; propose approach, team, timeline and feeRealistic scope, assumptions and a fee that covers the workProposal and engagement letter or SOW
2. Scoping and mobilisationConfirm the key question, stakeholders, data access and governance; kick offWritten key question, data request list, steering datesEngagement charter and workplan
3. DiagnosisInterviews, data gathering, current-state analysisData arriving on time; early hypotheses testedFact base and refined hypotheses
4. Analysis and solution designTest hypotheses, model options, develop recommendationsStopping analysis that will not change the answerOptions, business case and recommendations
5. Recommendation and decisionPresent findings, align stakeholders, agree decisionsPre-wiring key stakeholders before the final meetingFinal report or board presentation; decision log
6. Implementation supportPlan and support the change, often through a PMOClear hand-over of ownership to client staffImplementation roadmap, PMO reporting
7. CloseHand over materials, gather feedback, capture knowledgeSign-off, final invoice, lessons and follow-on needsClosure note and lessons learned
Tip

Hold a short sponsor check-in every week and share emerging findings as soon as they are reasonably solid. The final presentation should confirm conclusions the sponsor has already seen, not reveal them.

How to run a consulting engagement step by step

The steps below are our recommended sequence for a typical 8 to 12 week diagnostic or strategy engagement. Adapt the timing to your firm's methods.

  1. Qualify and scope the opportunity. Agree the business problem with the prospective sponsor, check that you have the expertise and capacity, and estimate effort from similar past engagements. See effort estimation.
  2. Write the proposal and engagement letter. State the key question, deliverables, approach, team, timeline, fee, client responsibilities (data, access, decision makers) and assumptions. Use a project proposal template and confirm the legal terms in a statement of work.
  3. Frame the key question in one sentence. Make it specific, measurable and time-bound, for example: how can the client restore operating margin from 3% to 5% within 24 months? Record it in a problem statement and get the sponsor to approve it.
  4. Build the issue tree and hypotheses. Break the key question into mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive (MECE) sub-questions and write a likely answer for each. The next section shows a worked example.
  5. Turn hypotheses into a workplan. For each hypothesis list the analysis, data source, owner and week. This becomes your schedule and the basis for tracking progress.
  6. Mobilise and kick off. Send the data request list before day one, book interviews for the first two weeks, agree steering committee dates and set up shared workspaces with the right client access.
  7. Run a weekly rhythm. Team planning on Monday, a problem-solving session midweek, a sponsor check-in and a short status note on Friday. Update the workplan and the budget every week.
  8. Control scope and fee. Log every new request, assess its effect on time and fee and agree it with the sponsor before starting. See the scope change process below.
  9. Build the storyline and pre-wire. Draft the final storyline from week 3, test it with the sponsor and walk key stakeholders through findings individually before the final meeting.
  10. Close properly. Present, agree decisions and owners, hand over models and data, get sign-off, issue the final invoice and run a lessons learned review with the team.

Building a hypothesis-driven workplan

A hypothesis-driven approach starts with a likely answer and plans only the analyses needed to prove or disprove it. It is widely used in management consulting because it keeps short engagements focused. It works in three layers:

  • Issue tree: the key question broken into sub-questions that do not overlap (mutually exclusive) and together cover the whole question (collectively exhaustive), usually shortened to MECE.
  • Hypotheses: a testable statement for each branch, written before the analysis, such as: store labour cost per sale has risen faster than sales.
  • Analyses: the specific piece of work, data and owner that will confirm or reject each hypothesis.

Worked example: from issue tree to workplan

A regional grocery chain has seen operating margin fall from 5% to 3% over two years. The key question agreed with the sponsor is: how can the client restore operating margin to 5% within 24 months? The team splits margin into revenue, cost of goods and operating costs, writes hypotheses and turns them into a six-week workplan. The figures below are illustrative.

BranchHypothesisAnalysisData sourceOwnerWeeks
RevenueLike-for-like sales are flat because of lost basket size, not footfallBasket and footfall trend by store, 24 monthsTill dataConsultant A1–2
RevenuePromotions are eroding price without lifting volumePromotion uplift and margin analysis, top 200 linesPromotions calendar, sales dataAnalyst2–3
Cost of goodsSupplier terms lag the market for key categoriesBenchmark terms for top 20 suppliersContracts, buyer interviewsConsultant B2–4
Cost of goodsWaste in fresh categories has risenWaste by category and storeStock and waste recordsAnalyst1–3
Operating costsStore labour hours are not matched to demandHours against hourly sales profile, 10 sample storesRota and till dataConsultant A3–4
Operating costsEnergy cost per square metre is above peersEnergy spend trend and benchmarkUtility bills, facilities teamConsultant B3–4
SynthesisTwo or three levers can close most of the 2-point gapSize each lever; build the margin bridgeOutputs of the analyses aboveEngagement manager5–6

Each row is a work package with an owner, data and dates, so the issue tree doubles as the work breakdown structure for the engagement. Progress is measured by hypotheses tested, not hours spent.

Keeping the workplan honest

  • Prioritise by impact. Size each branch early with rough numbers and put the most effort into the branches that could close most of the gap.
  • Kill hypotheses quickly. If the first cut of data rejects a hypothesis, record it and move the time to a branch that matters. Do not polish analysis that will not change the answer.
  • Re-check MECE at the end of week 1. New information often reveals a missing branch, such as a shift in product mix, that must be added and resourced.
  • Use root cause tools where the question is operational. A fishbone diagram or root cause analysis template helps explain why a problem occurs, and a gap analysis template structures current versus target state.

Scoping and pricing a consulting engagement

The pricing model sets who carries the risk if the work takes longer. Many engagements combine models, for example a fixed-fee diagnostic followed by time and materials implementation support.

Pricing modelHow it worksWho carries overrun riskBest used when
Fixed feeOne price for defined deliverablesThe consulting firmQuestion and approach are well understood and you have done similar work before
Time and materialsClient pays for days at agreed ratesMostly the clientScope is uncertain or the client wants to direct the work
Capped time and materialsDay rates up to a ceilingShared; the firm absorbs days above the capClient wants flexibility with a budget limit
RetainerMonthly fee for agreed capacity or accessSharedOngoing advisory or PMO support
Value or success feePart of the fee depends on measured resultsLargely the firmResults are measurable and a baseline is agreed before work starts

Worked example: building a fixed fee

An eight-week diagnostic with one partner, one engagement manager, two consultants and one analyst. Day rates are illustrative standard rates, not market benchmarks.

RoleDaysStandard day rateStandard value
Partner8£3,000£24,000
Engagement manager40£1,800£72,000
Consultants (2)80£1,200£96,000
Analyst40£800£32,000
Total168£224,000
  1. Estimate days bottom-up from the workplan: interviews, analyses, workshops, drafting and steering meetings, then compare with similar past engagements.
  2. Price at standard rates to get the standard value of the work: £224,000 in this example.
  3. Add a contingency for fixed-fee risk. Ten percent (£22,400) gives £246,400. The size should reflect how uncertain the question and data access are.
  4. Make the commercial decision. The partner agrees a fee of £245,000 plus expenses at cost, accepting a small discount on the contingency to win the work.
  5. Write the assumptions that protect the fee: number of interviews, data provided by week 1, two steering meetings, one round of revisions on the final report, and sponsor availability.

The fee sets the budget the engagement manager must deliver within: 168 days of effort plus a small buffer. How that turns into firm-level utilisation, realisation and margin is covered with worked numbers in our guide to professional services project management.

Roles and team structure

RoleResponsibility on the engagement
Engagement partner or directorOwns the client relationship, quality of the answer and the commercial result; leads steering meetings
Engagement managerRuns the engagement day to day: workplan, team, budget, client meetings, storyline and deliverables
ConsultantsLead workstreams: interviews, analyses, workshops and drafting
AnalystsData gathering, modelling and slide production
Subject matter expertsSpecialist input for part of the work, often a few days each
Client sponsorCommissions the work, removes obstacles, makes decisions and champions the recommendations
Client counterparts and steering groupProvide data and access, challenge findings and own implementation

Consulting teams are usually shaped as a pyramid: a small amount of senior time and a larger amount of consultant and analyst time. This leverage keeps fees affordable but means the engagement manager carries most of the day-to-day quality control. Agree who reviews what before work starts, and write it down in a RACI matrix, particularly for client-side approvals.

Tip

Name one client counterpart for each workstream in the engagement charter. Consultants who have to find their own data owner lose days in the first fortnight.

Governance and client communication

Good project governance in consulting is light but regular. The cadence below suits most engagements of 6 to 12 weeks.

ForumWhoFrequencyPurpose
Team planningEngagement manager and teamWeekly (start of week)Priorities, workplan updates, blockers

Send a written agenda before every steering committee and keep status notes short: our guide to the project status report has a structure that works.

Build the storyline answer first

Senior clients want the answer before the evidence. Many consultancies use the pyramid principle, described by Barbara Minto, which puts the governing message first, then the supporting arguments, then the data. In practice:

  1. Write the one-sentence answer to the key question, even if it is still a hypothesis.
  2. List the three to five supporting points that would make the answer convincing.
  3. Map each supporting point to the analyses in the workplan; gaps show which analysis is still missing.
  4. Draft slide headlines as full sentences that state the finding, and test the headline sequence with the sponsor.
  5. Finish with the executive summary, decisions required and next steps.

Engagement health (RAG) criteria

  • Green: key question stable, data on time, days used in line with earned progress, sponsor engaged.
  • Amber: data late by more than a week, a forecast overrun within contingency, or a key stakeholder not yet aligned.
  • Red: the key question has changed, the forecast overrun exceeds contingency, or the sponsor is unavailable for decisions.

Managing scope change in a consulting engagement

In consulting, scope creep rarely arrives as a formal request. It arrives as a reasonable question in a meeting: could you also look at the online channel? Use a simple, consistent process.

  1. Capture the request in the change log the same day, with who asked and why.
  2. Check it against the engagement letter. Is it covered by the key question and listed deliverables? If yes, it is in scope and you plan it. If not, continue.
  3. Size the impact in days, fee and dates, and say what else would move if you absorb it.
  4. Offer options: add it with a fee change, swap it for lower-value work, or defer it to a follow-on phase.
  5. Agree with the sponsor in writing before starting any work, using a change request template.
  6. Update the workplan, budget and RAID log, and mention the change in the next status note.

Example: in week 3 of the grocery engagement, the commercial director asks the team to add an online channel profitability review. The engagement manager estimates 12 extra days, mainly consultant time, worth about £15,000 at standard rates and one week of delay to the final steering meeting. The sponsor chooses to swap it for the energy benchmarking analysis, which early data shows is a small lever. Fee and date stay the same, and the decision is recorded.

For the general method, see our guides to change control and how to set boundaries in a statement of work.

Mid-engagement budget health check (worked example)

Comparing days used with days budgeted is not enough: you can be on budget and badly behind. Compare days used with earned days, the share of the workplan completed multiplied by the total budgeted days. This is a simple form of earned value management.

MeasureValue at end of week 4 of 8How it is calculated
Budgeted days (total)168From the fee build-up
Planned days to date84Half the engagement elapsed
Actual days used96From timesheets
Workplan complete45%Weighted share of analyses finished
Earned days75.645% × 168
Efficiency ratio0.7975.6 ÷ 96 (a cost performance index measured in days)
Forecast total daysAbout 213168 ÷ 0.79
Forecast overrunAbout 45 days, roughly £60,000213 − 168, at the blended rate of about £1,333 per day

The forecast overrun is well beyond the £22,400 contingency, so the engagement is red. The engagement manager's options, in order:

  • Stop or shrink analyses that early data shows will not change the answer.
  • Ask the client to provide data in the agreed format, as assumed in the engagement letter, instead of the team cleaning it.
  • Re-sequence work so the senior team reviews storyline drafts earlier, reducing late rework.
  • If the client has added scope informally, raise a change request for it now.
  • Agree with the partner whether any remaining overrun is absorbed by the firm, and record why for future estimates.
Tip

Run this check every Friday from week 2. A ratio below 0.9 two weeks running is an early warning that needs action, not a reason to hope the second half will be faster.

Essential documents and templates

  • Project proposal template to win and frame the work.
  • Project charter template as the engagement charter: key question, scope, team, governance and assumptions.
  • Problem statement template to agree the key question with the sponsor.
  • Gap analysis template and root cause analysis template for diagnosis.
  • RAID log template for risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies.
  • Change request template for any change to questions, deliverables, fee or dates.
  • Executive summary template for the final recommendation, and a lessons learned log at close.

Skills and career path

Engagement managers usually rise from consultant roles. The skills that matter most are structured problem solving, clear writing and presenting, stakeholder management, team leadership and disciplined control of plan and fee.

Frequently asked questions

What is consulting project management?

It is planning and running advisory engagements so the consulting team answers the client's question, delivers recommendations the client accepts and stays within the agreed fee and timeline.

What are the phases of a consulting project?

Proposal, scoping and mobilisation, diagnosis, analysis and solution design, recommendation and decision, implementation support where contracted, and close.

What does an engagement manager do?

The engagement manager runs the engagement day to day: the workplan, team, budget, client meetings, quality of analysis, storyline and delivery of findings, reporting to the engagement partner.

What is a hypothesis-driven approach in consulting?

Breaking the client question into an issue tree, writing a likely answer for each branch early and planning only the analyses needed to prove or disprove those answers.

What does MECE mean?

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive: the branches of an issue tree should not overlap and should together cover the whole question, so nothing is analysed twice or missed.

How long does a consulting project last?

It varies widely. Diagnostic and strategy phases commonly run for several weeks to a few months, while implementation support can continue for many months. Agree the duration of each phase in the engagement letter.

How do you manage scope creep in consulting?

Agree the key question and deliverables in writing, log every new request, size its effect on days, fee and dates, offer options and get the sponsor's written agreement before starting.

How do consultants price projects?

Usually by estimating days by role from a workplan, pricing them at standard day rates, adding contingency for fixed-fee risk and then making a commercial decision. Time and materials, retainers and success fees are also used.

What is a steering committee in a consulting engagement?

A group of senior client leaders, the sponsor and the engagement partner who meet at phase gates to agree findings, direction and any scope changes.

Which KPIs are used in consulting project management?

Engagement measures such as earned days against actual days, forecast days at completion, milestones met and client satisfaction, plus firm measures such as utilisation, realisation, margin and follow-on work.

Is Agile used in consulting?

Yes, especially on digital, data and transformation engagements. Strategy and diagnostic work usually follows phased delivery with steering gates, often with Agile practices inside workstreams.

What is the difference between consulting project management and project management consulting?

Consulting project management is how a consultancy runs its own engagements. Project management consulting is a service that improves a client's project capability, such as setting up a PMO or recovering a programme.

What software is used for consulting project management?

A work management platform such as Wrike for workplans, resourcing and client collaboration, plus time tracking, PSA or finance tools for billing and secure document sharing.
