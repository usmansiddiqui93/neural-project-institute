Construction Project Management: Phases, Steps and Tools

Construction project management is the planning, coordination and control of a building or civil works project from the first idea to handover, so that it is delivered safely, to the agreed quality, on time and within budget. It differs from most project work because the output is a one-off physical asset, built on a specific site by a temporary team of separate firms bound together by contracts. This guide covers the phases, a step-by-step process, delivery routes, roles, illustrative worked examples for programme and cost control, safety duties, KPIs and software.

Key takeaways

  • Construction project management runs a one-off physical asset through design, procurement, construction and handover with a temporary team of separate firms.
  • The delivery route and form of contract decide who designs, who carries which risk and how change is valued, so choose them deliberately.
  • The programme, cost plan, risk register and change register are the four controls you use every week.
  • Plan long-lead items, permits and safety approvals backwards from the date they are needed on site; they drive most delays.
  • Most projects are hybrid: stage-gated overall, critical path for the master programme, Lean planning on site and short iterative loops in design.
  • Use specialist field and BIM tools on site, and a work management platform such as Wrike for planning, approvals and reporting across projects.

What is construction project management?

Construction project management is the discipline of planning, coordinating and controlling a construction project's scope, programme, cost, quality, safety and contracts from inception to handover. It aligns the client, designers, contractors and authorities so a safe, compliant asset is delivered to the agreed budget and date.

The work looks different depending on who you work for. A client-side project manager protects the owner's brief, budget and programme and manages the design team and contractor. A contractor-side project manager delivers the works under the building contract and protects the contractor's margin. Both use the same controls; they simply carry different risks. See our guide to the construction project manager role for the job itself: duties, skills, qualifications and career path.

Do not confuse it with construction management, which can mean either the site-based management of the works or a specific procurement route in which a construction manager coordinates separate trade contracts on the client's behalf. Construction project management is the wider discipline that covers the whole project.

What makes construction projects different

Every construction project is a prototype: a new site, a new design and usually a new mix of firms. The project manager aligns those firms through contracts rather than line management, while working with constraints office-based projects rarely face.

  • Safety and law. Construction is a high-hazard industry, so health and safety planning is a legal duty. In Great Britain, the CDM Regulations 2015 assign duties to the client, principal designer and principal contractor.
  • Permits and approvals. Planning permission or zoning, building regulations or code approval and utility connections all sit on the critical path.
  • Contracts drive behaviour. The procurement route and contract form decide who carries which risk and how change is priced.
  • Physical sequence. Ground before frame, frame before envelope, envelope before fit-out. You cannot build the roof before the foundations.
  • Weather, ground and supply chains. Unknown ground conditions, weather and long-lead items such as switchgear, lifts and façade panels are major sources of delay.
  • Many stakeholders. Clients, funders, end users, neighbours, local authorities, insurers and utility companies all have a say.

The phases of a construction project

Countries name the stages differently, but the flow is broadly the same. This six-phase model is a practical way to organise the work.

PhaseWhat happensKey outputs
1. Pre-design (inception and feasibility)Client defines the need, budget and site; options are testedBusiness case, feasibility study, project brief, outline budget
2. DesignConcept, developed and technical design; planning or zoning applicationDrawings and specifications, planning consent, updated cost plan
3. PreconstructionBuildability reviews, programme, logistics, risk and health and safety planningConstruction programme, logistics plan, construction phase plan, risk register
4. ProcurementTendering the main contract and trade packages, ordering long-lead itemsTender documents, signed contracts, procurement schedule
5. ConstructionSite works from enabling works and substructure through frame, envelope, services and finishesProgress reports, RFIs, submittals, variations, valuations, inspection records
6. Commissioning and closeoutTesting services, snagging (punch list), handover, defects periodO&M manuals, as-built drawings, health and safety file, completion certificate, final account

Phases overlap in practice. On design and build projects, procurement happens before technical design is finished, and long-lead items are often ordered during design.

RIBA and AIA stage names

In the UK, many teams use the RIBA Plan of Work 2020: 0 Strategic Definition, 1 Preparation and Briefing, 2 Concept Design, 3 Spatial Coordination, 4 Technical Design, 5 Manufacturing and Construction, 6 Handover and 7 Use. In the US, the AIA's B101–2017 agreement defines five phases: schematic design, design development, construction documents, procurement and construction. Our guide to architecture project management maps the two in detail.

Tip

Agree at the start which stage-naming system everyone will use. A client who says 'Stage 4' and a contractor who says 'construction documents' may mean different things.

How to manage a construction project, step by step

These ten steps apply whether you are delivering a house extension or a hospital. The scale and the level of formality change; the logic does not.

  1. Define the brief and business case. Agree objectives, scope, quality expectations, budget, target completion date and the client's risk appetite. Record them in a project charter or brief.
  2. Appoint the design team and confirm duty holders. Appoint architect, engineers and cost consultant, and make sure legal safety roles (such as principal designer and principal contractor under CDM) are filled in writing.
  3. Choose the delivery route and contract. Decide who designs, who builds and who carries which risk (see the next section) before design goes too far.
  4. Develop the design in stages with sign-offs. Close each stage with a written client approval and an updated cost plan, so later changes are visible and priced.
  5. Secure permissions early. Put planning, building control or code approvals, utility applications and third-party agreements on the programme as critical activities.
  6. Build the programme and cost plan. Break the works into a work breakdown structure, sequence it, find the critical path and set a baseline. Plan long-lead items backwards from their need date.
  7. Tender and award. Package the work, issue tenders, compare bids on quality and price, and confirm the contract programme before signing.
  8. Control the works. Hold weekly progress and coordination meetings, manage RFIs and submittals, value work monthly, log and price every change before it is instructed, and inspect quality and safety.
  9. Commission and hand over. Plan testing, training, O&M manuals and as-built information from preconstruction, not in the last weeks. Close snags before or soon after practical completion.
  10. Close out and learn. Manage the defects period, agree the final account and run a lessons learned review with designers and contractors.

Delivery routes and contracts

The delivery route (procurement route) is one of the most consequential decisions on a construction project. It sets when the contractor is involved, who is responsible for design and how much cost certainty the client has before work starts.

RouteWho designsMain risk withCost certainty at contractSpeed
Traditional (design-bid-build)Client's consultants, before tenderClient for design; contractor for building to itGood if design is completeSlower: design must finish before tender
Design and build (design-build)Contractor, often after taking over the client's conceptContractor for design and constructionGood, but less client control of detailFaster: design and construction overlap
Construction management / management contractingClient's consultantsClient holds trade contracts (CM) or the manager does (MC)Lower: packages priced as design developsFast for complex jobs
Construction manager at risk (CMAR)Client's consultants, with early contractor inputConstruction manager up to a guaranteed maximum priceGood once the price is fixedFaster than traditional
Two-stage tenderingConsultants, with contractor input in stage oneShared, then contractor after stage two priceFirms up at the second stageFaster start on site
Alliancing / integrated project deliveryIntegrated teamShared through pain/gain mechanismsTarget cost rather than fixed priceDepends on team maturity

Standard forms of contract then set the rules. JCT and NEC4 are common in the UK, FIDIC is widely used internationally, and AIA and ConsensusDocs documents are common in the US. NEC4 builds project management into the contract through mechanisms such as early warnings, which oblige parties to raise risks as soon as they become aware of them. Whatever the form, know exactly what authority your appointment gives you before instructing anything.

Planning the construction programme

The construction programme (schedule) is the backbone of the project. Build it from the work breakdown structure, link activities with dependencies, and use the critical path method to find the chain of activities that sets the completion date. Baseline it once the contract is signed and update it at least monthly.

Worked example: planning a long-lead item backwards

This example is illustrative. A contractor is building a two-storey community health centre on a 60-week programme. The main electrical switchgear must be energised by week 44 so commissioning can start. The supplier quotes 22 weeks to manufacture and deliver. Working backwards:

ActivityDurationMust finish byMust start by
Energise supply1 weekWeek 44Week 44
Test and inspect1 weekWeek 43Week 43
Install switchgear2 weeksWeek 42Week 41
Manufacture and deliver22 weeksWeek 40Week 18
Submittal review and approval3 weeksWeek 17Week 15
Freeze specification (with 2 weeks' buffer)–Week 12–

The design team must therefore freeze the switchgear specification by week 12, long before the electrical fit-out appears on site. Repeat this for every long-lead item and you have a procurement schedule that drives design deadlines, rather than discovering in week 30 that equipment cannot arrive in time.

Worked example: weekly planning with Last Planner

The Last Planner System, developed by Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell through the Lean Construction Institute, adds collaborative short-term planning beneath the master programme: phase pull planning, a six-week lookahead that removes constraints, weekly work plans agreed by the trade foremen and daily huddles. Reliability is measured by per cent plan complete (PPC): commitments completed divided by commitments made.

In an illustrative week, five trades commit to 40 tasks and complete 31, so PPC is 31 ÷ 40 = 77.5%. The team then records why the nine misses happened (four waiting for design information, three because preceding work was unfinished, two for late materials) and acts on the biggest cause before the next week. Cost control and change management

Cost control starts with an elemental cost plan in design and becomes a contract budget at award. During construction, work done is valued monthly, a cash flow forecast is kept current and every change is logged and priced. An S-curve of planned against actual spend gives clients a clear picture at a glance.

Worked example: earned value at the half-way point

Continuing the illustrative health centre project, the contract sum (budget at completion) is £4.8 million. At week 30 the team measures progress:

MeasureFormulaValue
Planned value (PV)Budgeted cost of work scheduled to date£2.40m
Earned value (EV)Budgeted cost of work actually completed£2.16m
Actual cost (AC)Cost incurred to date£2.30m
Schedule variance (SV)EV − PV−£0.24m
Cost variance (CV)EV − AC−£0.14m
Schedule performance index (SPI)EV ÷ PV0.90
Cost performance index (CPI)EV ÷ AC0.94
Estimate at completion (EAC)BAC ÷ CPIAbout £5.11m

The project has earned only 90% of the value it planned to by now, and every pound spent is producing about 94p of planned work. If performance continues, the final cost will be roughly £0.31 million over budget. The project manager now looks for the cause (in this case, a frame subcontractor short of labour), agrees a recovery plan and reports the forecast honestly. Worked example: pricing a change before it is instructed

The client asks for upgraded, easier-to-clean flooring in 12 consulting rooms. Before anything is instructed, the contractor prices it at £21,600 including overheads and profit, and the planner confirms the flooring is not on the critical path, so there is no extension of time. The client's cost consultant checks the rates, the client approves in writing and the change enters the register. With this change, approved variations total £192,000, which is 4.0% of the £4.8 million contract sum, a figure reported monthly. Route every change through formal change control and record it on a change request template.

Health, safety and regulatory compliance

Safety is not a separate workstream; it shapes design, sequencing, logistics and the programme. Requirements vary by country, so confirm the rules where you work. Examples:

  • Great Britain: the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 place duties on the client, principal designer, designers, principal contractor and contractors. According to HSE, a project must be notified if construction work will last longer than 30 working days with more than 20 workers on site at the same time, or exceed 500 person-days.
  • England, higher-risk buildings: under the Building Safety Act 2022, higher-risk buildings need Building Safety Regulator approval before construction starts (Gateway 2) and before occupation (Gateway 3). Treat both as critical-path activities.
  • United States: OSHA construction standards apply, alongside state and local building codes, permits and inspections.

Build safety into the plan: design out hazards early, sequence work to avoid trades working above each other, plan temporary works and site logistics, and allow time for inspections. A construction phase plan and the health and safety file at handover are standard outputs on most projects.

Risk management in construction

Identify risks by phase, give each an owner and a response, price them where you can and review them at every progress meeting. Keep them in a risk register and hold a measured contingency for what remains.

RiskEarly warning signsTypical response
Unforeseen ground conditionsLimited site investigation, old maps showing previous usesCommission ground investigation early; hold a specific contingency
Late design informationRising RFI count, open RFIs older than two weeksInformation release schedule tied to the programme; design freeze by package
Long-lead items arriving lateNo order placed by the back-planned dateProcurement schedule; early orders where the contract allows
Client changesUndecided requirements, new stakeholders joiningStage sign-offs; price every change before instruction
Subcontractor capacity or insolvencyThin labour on site, late payments down the chainCheck financial standing; monitor labour against plan
Approvals delayedLate submissions, unanswered queries from authoritiesPre-application meetings; programme approvals as critical activities

Under NEC contracts, the early warning process turns this into a contractual habit: risks are notified, discussed at early warning meetings and recorded in an early warning register.

Most real projects are hybrid: a stage-gated master programme, Lean planning on site and short iterative loops in design. For BIM-based projects, ISO 19650 is the international standard for managing information across the asset's life cycle.

Construction project management software

Construction teams usually combine two kinds of software. Specialist construction platforms handle field work: drawings and document control, RFIs and submittals, site diaries, inspections and snagging on tablets, plus BIM model coordination and specialist scheduling tools for large critical path programmes. A work management platform then sits above them for the client-side portfolio, design management, approvals, reporting and the office work around every job. Look for:

  • Gantt charts with dependencies, the critical path and baselines.
  • Request and approval workflows for changes and design sign-off.
  • Dashboards that roll up many projects for directors and clients.
  • Controlled access for external consultants and subcontractors.
  • Integration with document storage and finance systems.

For that coordination layer, Wrike is our top-rated platform, with Gantt charts that support dependencies, the critical path and baselines, plus workload views, custom request forms and cross-project dashboards. Smartsheet suits teams that like a spreadsheet-style grid with Gantt views and roll-up reporting, and monday.com is quick for teams to adopt for simpler job tracking. Pair whichever you choose with the field and BIM tools your contractors already use rather than trying to make one product do everything. See our guide on how to choose project management software.

Frequently asked questions

What are the phases of construction project management?

A common breakdown is pre-design, design, preconstruction, procurement, construction, and commissioning and closeout. The UK RIBA Plan of Work 2020 and the US AIA phases use different names for similar stages.

What is the difference between construction management and construction project management?

Construction management can mean site management of the works or a procurement route in which a construction manager coordinates separate trade contracts for the client. Construction project management is the broader discipline of running the whole project from brief to handover.

What does a construction project management plan include?

Scope and objectives, the procurement strategy, the programme, cost plan, risk register, health and safety arrangements, quality plan, communication plan and change control procedure.

What are the main construction delivery methods?

Traditional (design-bid-build), design and build, construction management or management contracting, construction manager at risk, two-stage tendering and collaborative routes such as alliancing or integrated project delivery. They differ in who designs, who carries risk and when the price is fixed.

What is a change order in construction?

A change order (or variation) is a formal instruction that changes the scope, cost or time of the contract works. It should be priced and approved before the work is done and recorded in a change register.

Is Agile used in construction project management?

Yes, mostly in design, fit-out and planning cycles, often alongside Lean methods such as the Last Planner System. The overall project usually stays stage-gated because of contracts and physical sequencing.

What is the Last Planner System?

A Lean planning method developed by Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell in which the people who will do the work make reliable weekly commitments, remove constraints in advance and measure per cent plan complete.

Which KPIs matter most in construction project management?

Schedule and cost performance (SPI and CPI), change orders as a share of contract value, RFI turnaround, safety rates, per cent plan complete and snag closure are among the most widely used.

What software is used for construction project management?

Most firms combine specialist construction software for field work, document control and BIM with a work management platform such as Wrike for portfolio planning, approvals and reporting.

What certifications help in construction project management?

Common choices include the PMP, PRINCE2, chartered membership of the CIOB or RICS in the UK, and the Certified Construction Manager (CCM) credential in the US, plus Lean construction training.

What does closeout involve?

Testing and commissioning, snagging, handover of O&M manuals and as-built drawings, the health and safety file where required, practical completion, the defects period and agreement of the final account.

How do you keep a construction project on budget?

Fix the brief early, freeze design by package, hold a realistic contingency, price every change before instructing it, value work monthly and track CPI and the forecast final cost rather than spend alone.
