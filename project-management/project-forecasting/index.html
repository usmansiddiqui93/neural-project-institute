Project Forecasting: Methods, Examples and Steps

Project forecasting is predicting where a project will finish, mainly its final cost and completion date, from its performance so far and the work still to do. A good forecast gives the sponsor an honest, early warning while there is still time to act, and it is usually best stated as a range with a level of confidence rather than a single number.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 30 September 2026
  • Last updated 25 October 2026
  • 19 min read

Key takeaways

  • Forecast both the final cost (EAC) and the completion date; one without the other gives a misleading picture.
  • Earned value formulas such as EAC = BAC / CPI are a quick, objective starting point, not the final answer.
  • Earned schedule (forecast duration = planned duration / SPI(t)) gives a time forecast that stays reliable late in the project.
  • Monte Carlo simulation and Agile velocity ranges turn forecasts into probabilities and date ranges.
  • Validate formula results with the team's re-estimate of remaining work, open risks and pending changes.
  • Update forecasts every reporting period, track their accuracy and report them honestly.

What is project forecasting?

Project forecasting is the process of predicting a project's future outcomes, chiefly its final cost, completion date and resource needs, using actual performance to date, the remaining work and known risks. Unlike the original estimate, a forecast is updated throughout delivery, so decision makers always see the most likely end point compared with the approved baseline.

Forecasting is part of monitoring and controlling. In PMI's PMBOK Guide, eighth edition (November 2025), it sits within processes such as monitoring and controlling finances and the schedule.

Forecast, estimate, budget and target compared

These four words are often used loosely, which causes confusion in steering meetings.

TermWhat it isWhen it is setCan it change?
EstimateA prediction of cost or duration made before the work is doneDuring planning, from the scope and assumptionsRefined during planning
BudgetThe approved amount of money (the budget at completion, BAC)When the cost baseline is approvedOnly through formal change control
ForecastThe current best prediction of the final outcome, using actual performanceEvery reporting period during deliveryYes, every time it is updated
TargetA desired result, often a stretch goal set by managementAny timeAt management's discretion

The budget is a commitment; the forecast is a prediction. When the forecast moves away from the budget, the gap is a signal that needs a decision, such as a change in scope, extra funding or recovery actions. A forecast that is quietly adjusted to match the budget is no longer a forecast.

What to forecast

Most attention goes on cost and dates, but a complete forecast covers everything the sponsor needs to make decisions.

Forecast itemTypical measureQuestion it answers
Final costEstimate at completion (EAC)What will the project cost in total?
Cost to finishEstimate to complete (ETC = EAC - AC)How much more money do we need?
Variance at completionVAC = BAC - EACHow far over or under budget will we finish?

Resource needsPeople, skills or FTEs by monthDo we have the people for the remaining work?

BenefitsExpected benefits and their timingIs the project still worth doing?

On large projects the forecast is usually produced at work package or control account level and then rolled up.

Project forecasting methods

Forecasting methods fall into two broad families. Qualitative methods rely on judgement and are used when there is little data, for example early in a project or for novel work. Quantitative methods use performance data and models and become more reliable as the project progresses.

Qualitative methods

Expert judgement is the most used method of all, because someone has to decide whether the formula results make sense. The most valuable form is a structured re-estimate of the remaining work by the people doing it. The Delphi technique gathers anonymous estimates from a panel over several rounds, sharing the results between rounds until the range narrows.

Expert judgement→

Reference class forecasting starts from the actual outcomes of a group of similar past projects rather than from the details of this one. The idea comes from Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's work on the planning fallacy and has been applied to projects by Bent Flyvbjerg and others. It is a strong check on optimism bias, especially in business cases.

Quantitative methods

Quantitative methods use the project's own data: earned value and earned schedule project indices forward, a schedule update recalculates the critical path, and Monte Carlo simulation and velocity ranges add a measure of uncertainty. The sections below work through each with numbers.

Forecasting cost with earned value

Earned value management (EVM) is the most widely used quantitative method for cost forecasting. It compares three numbers at each status date: planned value (PV), earned value (EV) and actual cost (AC), and derives the cost performance index (CPI = EV / AC) and the schedule performance index (SPI = EV / PV).

FormulaAssumptionUse when
EAC = BAC / CPICurrent cost efficiency continuesThe cause of the overrun affects the remaining work
EAC = AC + (BAC - EV)Remaining work goes to the original budgetThe variance was a one-off and the cause has gone
EAC = AC + (BAC - EV) / (CPI x SPI)Cost and schedule performance both affect the remaining workYou are late and must still meet a fixed end date
EAC = AC + bottom-up ETCThe original estimates no longer applyScope, rates or approach have changed
ETC = EAC - ACCost of the remaining work
VAC = BAC - EACExpected overrun (negative) or underrun
TCPI = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC)Efficiency needed on the rest of the work to finish on budget

Worked example: forecasting final cost

A software implementation has a budget (BAC) of $200,000 and a planned duration of eight months. At the end of month four the figures are: PV = $80,000, EV = $70,000, AC = $85,000. So CPI = 70,000 / 85,000 = 0.824 and SPI = 70,000 / 80,000 = 0.875. The project is over budget and behind schedule.

ForecastCalculationEACVAC
EAC (CPI continues)200,000 / 0.824$242,857-$42,857
EAC (one-off variance)85,000 + (200,000 - 70,000)$215,000-$15,000
EAC (CPI x SPI)85,000 + 130,000 / (0.824 x 0.875)$265,408-$65,408
EAC (bottom-up)85,000 + team's ETC of $150,000$235,000-$35,000

The formulas give a range of roughly $215,000 to $265,000. To pick a figure, the project manager asks why the overrun happened. Here, the team found that integration work was harder than estimated and that this will affect the remaining interfaces, so the one-off formula is too optimistic. The team's bottom-up re-estimate ($235,000) sits close to the CPI-based figure ($242,857), which gives confidence. The forecast reported to the sponsor is $235,000 to $245,000, most likely around $240,000, with ETC of about $155,000.

The to-complete performance index adds a reality check: TCPI = (200,000 - 70,000) / (200,000 - 85,000) = 1.13. To finish on budget, the team would have to deliver $1.13 of value for every dollar spent from now on, while it has managed $0.82 so far. That gap is too large to close by working harder, so the sponsor must choose between extra funding and reduced scope. For more detail on every formula, see our guide to estimate at completion.

Forecasting the completion date

Cost formulas say nothing directly about dates. There are three common ways to forecast completion; use at least two.

1. Update the schedule and critical path

Enter actual start and finish dates and the remaining duration of each in-progress task, then let the scheduling tool recalculate. The new finish date comes from the updated critical path, which shows which tasks are actually driving the end date. This is the most direct method and the one most sponsors understand. Its weakness is that it depends on honest remaining durations, ideally from three-point estimates.

2. Use SPI to scale the planned duration

A quick check is forecast duration = planned duration / SPI. In the example, 8 / 0.875 = 9.1 months. It is simple but has a known flaw: SPI is calculated in money, and it always drifts back to 1.0 as the project nears completion, because eventually all planned value is earned, even if the project is months late. Late in a project it stops being useful.

3. Use earned schedule

Earned schedule (ES), developed by Walt Lipke in 2003 and extended by Kym Henderson, fixes that flaw by measuring schedule performance in time rather than money. ES is the point in time at which the current EV *should* have been earned according to the baseline. The key formulas are:

  • ES = C + (EV - PV at C) / (PV at C+1 - PV at C), where C is the last whole period in which planned value is less than or equal to EV.
  • SPI(t) = ES / AT, where AT is the actual time elapsed.
  • SV(t) = ES - AT, the schedule variance in time units.
  • Forecast duration (IEAC(t)) = planned duration / SPI(t).

Continuing the example, the cumulative planned value by month is shown below.

Month12345678
Cumulative PV ($000)10285080112145175200
  1. EV at month four is $70,000. Planned value passed $50,000 at the end of month three and reached $80,000 at the end of month four, so C = 3.
  2. ES = 3 + (70,000 - 50,000) / (80,000 - 50,000) = 3 + 0.67 = 3.67 months.
  3. SPI(t) = 3.67 / 4 = 0.917, and SV(t) = 3.67 - 4 = -0.33 months (about a week and a half behind).
  4. Forecast duration = 8 / 0.917 = 8.7 months.

So the date forecasts range from 8.7 months (earned schedule) to 9.1 months (simple SPI). The project manager then checks the critical path: if the delayed integration tasks are on it, the later figure is more realistic; if they have float, the earlier one may be. The reported forecast is a finish between mid-month nine and early month ten, alongside the cost range.

Tip

Earned schedule is not yet a built-in feature in every scheduling tool, but it is easy to calculate in a spreadsheet from the time-phased baseline, the same data used to draw an S-curve.

Probabilistic forecasting with Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation makes uncertainty visible. Instead of one duration or cost for each remaining task, you enter a range (optimistic, most likely and pessimistic), plus the probability and impact of key risks. The software recalculates the schedule and cost thousands of times, picking random values from each range, and plots the spread of results.

The output is a set of confidence levels, often called P-values. P50 means there is a 50% chance of finishing on or before that date or cost; P80 means an 80% chance. The table shows an illustrative result for the remaining work in the example project.

Confidence levelForecast finish (illustrative)Forecast final cost (illustrative)
P108.6 months$226,000
P509.1 months$239,000
P809.6 months$251,000
P909.9 months$258,000

Reading it: the most likely outcome is close to the deterministic forecast, but if the sponsor wants 80% confidence they should plan for 9.6 months and about $251,000. The difference between P50 and P80 is a reasoned basis for schedule and cost contingency. It also shows which tasks and risks drive the spread most.

Monte Carlo is only as good as its ranges: if they are too narrow, which is common, the results look precise and are wrong. Use historical data where you can, and link the model to the risk register so known risks are modelled explicitly.

Forecasting in Agile projects

Agile teams usually fix time and cost per iteration and forecast how much scope will be delivered by a date, or when a given scope will be done. The inputs are the remaining backlog and the team's recent delivery rate: velocity in story points or throughput in items per week.

Worked example: a release forecast from velocity

A Scrum team has 240 story points left in the release backlog and works in two-week sprints. Its velocity over the last six sprints was 28, 32, 25, 30, 35 and 30 points (average 30).

ScenarioVelocity usedSprints needed (240 / velocity)Weeks
Optimistic (average of best three sprints)32.37.4, so 8 sprints16
Average308 sprints16
Pessimistic (average of worst three sprints)27.78.7, so 9 sprints18
Worst observed sprint repeated259.6, so 10 sprints20

The team reports a forecast of 8 to 10 sprints (16 to 20 weeks), most likely 8 or 9. The forecast is updated after every sprint, and the release burn-up chart shows both the completed work and any growth in scope, so the sponsor can see whether a slipping date is caused by slower delivery or by new backlog items.

Throughput and Monte Carlo for flow-based teams

Kanban teams often forecast from throughput instead. A simple Monte Carlo approach samples from the team's actual weekly throughput history to simulate thousands of possible futures, producing statements such as "85% chance of finishing these 60 items within 11 weeks". It needs no item estimates, only a reasonable history of completed work.

Resource and benefits forecasting

Resource forecasting predicts the people, skills and equipment the remaining work will need and when. Start from the updated schedule and ETC, convert the remaining effort into hours or FTEs by month for each role, and compare it with what is available. Gaps show where you need to hire, borrow or re-sequence work; see capacity planning.

Benefits forecasting asks whether the project will still deliver the value in its business case: a later finish delays benefits, an overrun reduces the return and scope cuts may remove benefits altogether. Revisit it at each phase gate.

How to produce a project forecast step by step

  1. Set the status date. Use the same cut-off date for progress, costs and risks so the figures are consistent.
  2. Check the data. Make sure actual costs include accruals and committed costs for completed work, and that progress is measured with agreed rules rather than optimistic percentages.
  3. Calculate the indicators. Work out CPI, SPI, SPI(t) and the formula-based EACs and durations. These take minutes in a spreadsheet.
  4. Update the schedule. Enter actuals and remaining durations and recalculate the critical path.
  5. Re-estimate the remaining work. Ask the people doing the work for a bottom-up ETC on work packages that have changed or are performing differently from plan.
  6. Add risks and changes. Include the expected cost of open risks, the remaining contingency, approved changes and change requests likely to be approved.
  7. Compare and choose. Put the formula results, the schedule update and the bottom-up estimate side by side, decide which is most credible and explain why.
  8. Express a range. State a most likely value and a range, with a confidence level where you have run a simulation.
  9. Report and act. Present the forecast, its drivers and the options to improve it, and record the forecast so you can compare it with the actual outcome later.

Steps 1 to 4 can largely be automated, leaving time for the judgement in steps 5 to 9, which is where forecasts become credible.

Choosing the right forecasting method

SituationRecommended methods
Business case or early planning, little project dataReference class forecasting, analogy, expert judgement, three-point estimates
Less than about 15-20% completeBottom-up re-estimate and schedule update; treat earned value indices as early indicators only
Mid-project with stable performanceEarned value EACs and earned schedule, cross-checked with the critical path
After a major change or replanBottom-up ETC from the new plan; earned value from the new baseline
Late in the projectEarned schedule and critical path for dates (not simple SPI); bottom-up ETC for remaining items
Large, high-risk or contractual projectsMonte Carlo simulation on cost and schedule, alongside earned value
Agile or flow-based deliveryVelocity or throughput ranges, burn-up charts, throughput Monte Carlo

Whatever the situation, use at least two independent methods. When they disagree, the difference tells you something important.

How to present a forecast

A forecast only helps if the sponsor understands it and can act on it. A clear forecast section in the project status report usually includes:

  • Headline: the most likely final cost and finish date, compared with the baseline.
  • Range and confidence: for example "$235,000 to $245,000; finish between mid-month nine and early month ten".
  • Change since last report: what moved and why.
  • Drivers: the two or three main causes of variance.
  • Options: actions that would improve the outcome, with their cost and effect, such as reducing scope, adding people or using contingency.
  • Decision needed: what you are asking the sponsor to decide, and by when.

Agree thresholds in advance, for example escalate if EAC exceeds BAC by more than 5% or the forecast finish slips past a key milestone, so a worsening forecast triggers a decision rather than a discussion. How accurate are project forecasts?

Forecasts become more accurate as the project progresses, because more of the cost is actual rather than estimated. There is some research evidence on how quickly earned value forecasts settle. In a 1993 study of 155 US Department of Defense contracts, David Christensen and Scott Heise found that once a contract was more than 50% complete, the cumulative CPI did not change by more than 10%. Later research, such as Henderson and Zwikael's work on performance stability, has questioned how far this applies to other kinds of project, so treat it as a useful guide rather than a rule.

The practical lesson: a project over budget at the halfway point rarely recovers without a specific change in how the remaining work is done.

Track your own forecast accuracy

Record each period's forecast of final cost and finish date, then compare them with the actual outcome at closure. A simple chart of forecast EAC over time shows whether forecasts were stable, drifted steadily upwards (a sign of optimism) or jumped late (a sign that problems were hidden). Capture the findings in your lessons learned so the next project's estimates and forecasts improve.

Common forecasting mistakes

  • Forecasting what the sponsor wants to hear. Political pressure is the biggest single threat to an honest forecast.
  • Assuming the project will recover without a concrete reason, such as a changed approach or added resources.
  • Defaulting to the most optimistic formula. AC + (BAC - EV) gives the smallest number when you are over budget, but it assumes the problem has gone away.
  • Forecasting cost without dates, or dates without cost. A late project nearly always costs more.
  • Ignoring committed costs and accruals, which makes the project look cheaper than it is.
  • Relying on inflated progress. If tasks sit at 90% complete for weeks, EV is overstated and every forecast is too rosy.
  • Using simple SPI late in the project, when it drifts to 1.0 regardless of actual delay. Use earned schedule or the critical path.
  • Reporting a single precise figure such as $242,857, which implies false certainty.
  • Updating too rarely, so the forecast jumps near the end.
  • Leaving out risks and pending changes that everyone on the team knows about.

Frequently asked questions

What is project forecasting?

Predicting a project's final cost, completion date, resource needs and other outcomes from its performance to date and the work remaining, and updating that prediction throughout delivery.

What are the main project forecasting methods?

Expert judgement and bottom-up re-estimates, reference class forecasting, earned value formulas, earned schedule, critical path updates, three-point estimates, Monte Carlo simulation, and velocity or throughput forecasting for Agile teams.

How do you forecast the final cost of a project?

Calculate estimate at completion with earned value formulas such as EAC = BAC / CPI, then cross-check with a bottom-up estimate of the remaining work plus open risks and pending changes.

How do you forecast a project's completion date?

Update the schedule and critical path with actual progress, and cross-check with earned schedule: forecast duration = planned duration / SPI(t).

What is the difference between a forecast and an estimate?

An estimate is made before the work starts, from the scope and assumptions. A forecast updates the prediction during delivery using actual performance.

What is the difference between a forecast and a budget?

The budget is the approved amount and changes only through change control. The forecast is the current best prediction of the final cost and can change every period.

What is ETC in project forecasting?

Estimate to complete: the expected cost of the remaining work. ETC = EAC - AC, or a fresh bottom-up estimate of the remaining work.

What is earned schedule?

A method, developed by Walt Lipke, that measures schedule performance in time rather than money. It gives SPI(t) = ES / AT and a forecast duration of planned duration / SPI(t), and stays reliable late in a project.

What is a Monte Carlo project forecast?

A simulation that recalculates the schedule and cost thousands of times using ranges for each estimate and risk, showing the probability of finishing by a date or within a cost, such as P50 and P80.

How do Agile teams forecast?

By dividing the remaining backlog by a range of recent velocities or throughput, giving a date range, and updating it every iteration with a burn-up chart.

How often should project forecasts be updated?

Every reporting period, often monthly or every sprint, and whenever a significant change, issue or risk occurs.

Why should a forecast be a range rather than a single figure?

Because the future is uncertain. A range with a confidence level is more honest and helps sponsors set sensible contingency.

When are earned value forecasts reliable?

They become more reliable as the project progresses. Christensen and Heise's 1993 study of US defence contracts found the cumulative CPI changed by no more than 10% once contracts were over 50% complete, though later research questions how widely that applies.
