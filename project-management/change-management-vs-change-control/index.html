Change Management vs Change Control: Differences

Change management vs change control comes down to one distinction: change control decides whether a project's scope, schedule, cost or deliverables should change, while change management helps people adopt the new way of working the project delivers. This guide compares the two side by side, sets out each process step by step and shows both running on one worked example.

Key takeaways

  • Change control protects the project's baselines; change management protects the project's benefits.
  • Change control is a formal decision process run by the project manager, with approval from the right authority.
  • Change management is a people-focused discipline that prepares, equips and supports those whose work changes.
  • In IT service management, 'change management' (now 'change enablement' in ITIL 4) means controlling changes to live systems, which is closer to change control.
  • Most projects that alter how people work need both, planned together from the start.
  • Measure them differently: change control by decision quality and baseline stability, change management by adoption and benefits.

What is the difference between change management and change control?

Change control is the formal process for proposing, assessing, approving or rejecting and recording changes to a project's agreed baselines. Change management is the structured approach to helping individuals and the organisation move from current ways of working to new ones, so the project's output is actually used and its benefits are realised.

Put simply, change control asks 'should we change the plan?' and change management asks 'how do we help people change how they work?' The first is about the project; the second is about the organisation the project serves.

The two terms are often used interchangeably, which causes real problems. A sponsor who asks for 'change management' may expect training and communication, while the project manager hears a request for a change log. Agreeing which meaning you intend, early, saves wasted effort.

Tip

A useful memory aid: change control keeps the project on track; change management keeps the benefits on track.

What is change control?

Change control is a project control process. Once the scope, schedule and budget are agreed and baselined, any proposal to alter them goes through a defined route: it is logged, its impact is analysed, the person or board with the right authority decides, and the decision is recorded. Approved changes then update the plan and the project baseline.

It exists because uncontrolled change is one of the most common causes of overruns. Small additions that each look harmless add up to scope creep, and without a record nobody can explain why the project now costs more or finishes later.

What change control covers

  • Scope: features, deliverables and requirements added, removed or altered.
  • Schedule: milestone dates and the critical path.
  • Cost: budget, contingency and funding.
  • Quality and specifications: acceptance criteria, standards and designs.
  • Contracts and documents: statements of work, plans and other controlled items.

Change control in the standards

The PMBOK Guide 6th edition describes this as Perform Integrated Change Control, a process within project integration management that reviews all change requests and manages changes to deliverables, documents and the project management plan. PRINCE2 covers the same ground through its issue and change control procedure: the change theme in the 6th edition, and the issues practice in PRINCE2 7. Change control works closely with configuration management, which makes sure the approved version of each item is the one everyone uses.

What is change management?

Change management, often called organisational change management (OCM), is the discipline of preparing, equipping and supporting people to adopt a change. A project can deliver a new system, process or structure perfectly to specification and still fail to deliver value if people keep working the old way. Change management closes that gap.

What change management covers

  • Impact assessment: who is affected, how much their work changes and when.
  • Sponsorship: visible, active leadership that explains why the change matters.
  • Communication: targeted messages for each affected group, through trusted channels.
  • Training and support: building the knowledge and skills people need, plus help after go-live.
  • Resistance management: understanding concerns and addressing their causes.
  • Reinforcement: measuring adoption, recognising progress and removing ways back to the old process.

PMI's practice guide *Managing Change in Organizations* (2013) combines organisational development and HR perspectives with portfolio, programme and project management. Change management therefore overlaps heavily with stakeholder management, but it focuses specifically on the people whose work or behaviour must change, rather than everyone with an interest in the project.

Neither is a subset of the other. They answer different questions and are owned by different people, but they meet at one important point: change management activities such as extra training, a phased rollout or a pilot cost time and money, so they often enter the project through change control.

The change control process step by step

Most organisations use a variation of the following eight steps. The names differ between methods, but the logic is the same.

  1. Raise the request. Anyone can propose a change, but it should be written down on a standard form describing what, why and who is asking.
  2. Log it. Give the request a unique ID in the change log so it can be tracked and never lost.
  3. Initial review. The project manager checks the request is complete, is not a duplicate and is genuinely a change rather than a defect or clarification.
  4. Analyse the impact. Estimate the effect on scope, schedule (including the critical path), cost, quality, risk and benefits. Include the option of not making the change.
  5. Decide. The person or board with the relevant authority approves, rejects or defers the request, and the reason is recorded.
  6. Update plans and baselines. For approved changes, revise the schedule, budget, scope statement and any affected documents, and update the configuration record.
  7. Implement and verify. The team makes the change and the project manager checks it was done as approved.
  8. Communicate and close. Tell the requester and affected stakeholders the outcome, then close the entry in the log.

Set authority levels so small changes move quickly

A change control board that sees every minor request becomes a bottleneck. Agree delegated limits in the project management plan, for example:

Change size (illustrative)Who decidesTypical turnaround
Under £5,000, no milestone impact, no benefit changeProject managerWithin 2 working days
£5,000 to £50,000, or a milestone moves up to 2 weeksChange control boardNext scheduled board meeting
Over £50,000, a milestone moves more than 2 weeks, or benefits changeSponsor or steering committeeNext steering committee, or by exception

Match the thresholds to your organisation's tolerances. A standard change request template keeps every request in the same format, which makes impact analysis and decisions faster.

The change management process step by step

Change management has no single mandated process, but most approaches follow a similar arc: understand the change, plan the people side, carry it out and make it stick.

  1. Define the change and its benefits. Write down what will be different for people, and why it matters to the organisation. This becomes the core message.
  2. Assess the impact. For each affected group, record what changes (process, systems, tools, roles, behaviours), how much, and when.
  3. Secure and equip the sponsor. Agree what the sponsor will say and do, and when they will be visible.
  4. Build a change network. Brief line managers first, because people look to their own manager to judge whether a change is real. Recruit local champions where groups are large.
  5. Plan communication and training. Create a communication plan and a training plan tied to the project schedule, so training happens shortly before people need the new skills.
  6. Manage resistance. Listen for concerns, identify their root cause (lack of awareness, fear of losing status, workload, skills) and respond to the cause rather than the symptom.
  7. Support go-live. Provide floor-walkers, a help channel and quick-reference guides for the first weeks.
  8. Measure and reinforce. Track adoption, celebrate early progress, fix barriers and retire the old way of working once usage reaches an agreed level.

Worked example: one CRM project, both disciplines

A distribution company is replacing spreadsheets and email with a customer relationship management (CRM) system for 180 sales and service staff. The budget is £420,000 and go-live is planned for week 30. All figures below are illustrative.

The change control track

During build, four change requests arrive. The project manager logs each one and analyses its impact before the right authority decides.

IDChange requestedImpact analysisDecisionOutcome
CR-01Integrate CRM with the email marketing tool+£14,000, +3 weeks on a non-critical path, no milestone impactApproved by change control boardSchedule and budget baselines updated
CR-02Rename two status fields to match sales terminology+£300, no schedule impactApproved by project manager under delegated limitClosed in 2 days
CR-03Add a mobile app for field sales+£95,000, +10 weeks, moves go-liveDeferred to phase 2 by sponsorRecorded so it is not raised again
CR-04Run a 4-week pilot with one regional team before full rollout+£8,000, +4 weeks; go-live moves to week 34; reduces adoption riskApproved by sponsorPlan, budget and communication plan updated

The change management track

In parallel, the change manager assesses who is affected and how much.

GroupSizeWhat changes for themImpactKey actions
Field sales95Log every opportunity and call in the CRM, often on the moveHighManager briefings, hands-on training in week 32, champions in each region
Inside sales45Replace shared spreadsheets with CRM pipelinesMediumShort training sessions, quick-reference guide
Customer service30Record cases in CRM instead of email foldersMediumTraining plus floor-walkers for two weeks
Sales managers10Run forecasts and pipeline reviews from CRM dashboardsHighEarly involvement in design, coaching on using dashboards in meetings

Success is measured weekly after go-live: the share of opportunities created in the CRM rather than spreadsheets, the number of active users, and help-channel queries by topic. The shared spreadsheets are switched to read-only once agreed usage levels are reached, so the old way is no longer available.

Where the two tracks meet

  • The change manager's impact assessment showed field sales were at high risk of low adoption. That insight became CR-04, the pilot, which went through change control because it changed the schedule and budget.
  • CR-01 changed what service staff would see, so the change manager updated the training content and communication plan.
  • Deferring CR-03 needed a clear message to field sales, who had asked for the mobile app; the change manager handled that conversation.
  • Adoption measures were reported in the same project status report as delivery measures, so the sponsor saw both.

Without change control, the project would have absorbed the mobile app and missed its date. Without change management, it would have delivered a working CRM that half the sales team ignored.

How change management and change control work together

The most reliable approach is to plan both at the start and connect them at defined points. The table shows what each does in each phase.

Project phaseChange controlChange management
InitiationAgree change authority and thresholds in the project charter or governance planDescribe the change and its benefits; identify affected groups; confirm the sponsor's role
PlanningBaseline scope, schedule and cost; set up the change logImpact assessment; communication, training and resistance plans built into the schedule
ExecutionLog, analyse and decide change requests; update baselinesEngage managers and champions; communicate; prepare training
DeploymentControl late changes tightly; freeze scope before go-liveTrain, support go-live, collect feedback, manage resistance
Closure and beyondClose the change log; capture lessons learnedMeasure adoption, reinforce, hand ongoing ownership to the business

Five practical links between the two

  1. Use the change management impact assessment early, so adoption needs such as training time or a pilot are in the original scope rather than added later.
  2. Route any change management activity that alters scope, cost or schedule through change control, like any other request.
  3. Add a 'people impact' line to the change request form, so every approved change is checked for training and communication needs.
  4. Record adoption risks in the RAID log or risk register alongside delivery risks.
  5. Plan adoption activities beyond project closure where benefits depend on them, with a named business owner.

Which do you need? A quick decision guide

SituationChange controlChange management
A client asks for an extra feature mid-projectYes: assess impact and decideOnly if the feature changes how users work
A new finance system replaces paper processesYes, for changes to the projectYes, substantial: many people change how they work
A security patch to a live serverYes, through the IT change processUsually minimal: a notice to users
A move to hybrid working with no system changesLight, if run as a projectYes, this is mainly a people change
A reorganisation of reporting linesLight, for the project planYes, high impact on roles and behaviour
An internal infrastructure upgrade invisible to usersYesLittle or none

A rule of thumb: the more the project changes people's daily work, the more change management it needs. The more there is to protect in contracts, budgets and fixed dates, the more formal change control should be.

The third meaning: IT change management and change enablement

In IT service management, 'change management' has traditionally meant controlling changes to live systems and services: assessing the risk of a deployment, scheduling it and approving it. ITIL 4 now calls this practice change enablement and assigns decisions to a change authority, which can be a peer reviewer, an automated pipeline check for standard changes, or a change advisory board (CAB) for higher-risk ones.

This IT meaning is much closer to project change control than to organisational change management, but it controls changes to operational services rather than to a project's baselines.

Change control board (CCB)Change advisory board (CAB)
ContextProjectsIT service management
Decides onChanges to scope, schedule, cost and deliverables of a projectChanges to live systems and services
Main concernImpact on project objectives and baselinesRisk to service stability and users
Typical membersSponsor, project manager, senior user and supplier representativesService owners, technical leads, operations, security

When someone says 'change management', ask whether they mean people adoption, IT release approval or project change control. The answer decides who should be in the room.

Change control and change management in Agile projects

Agile approaches expect requirements to change. New ideas go into the product backlog and the product owner reorders it, so most scope change is handled by backlog prioritisation rather than formal requests. Formal change control still applies to things the team cannot change on its own: the contract, the budget, fixed release dates or the overall product goal.

Change management matters just as much in Agile. Frequent releases mean users experience a stream of small changes rather than one big launch, which can make adoption easier, but only if communication and support keep pace. Organisations moving to Agile ways of working face a large people change of their own, covered in our guide to Agile transformation.

Tip

In Agile, involve users in sprint reviews. Seeing and shaping each increment builds awareness and desire, two of the ADKAR stages, before anything goes live.

Measuring success

Change control measuresChange management measures
Average time from request to decisionPercentage of target users actively using the new process or system
Number of changes approved, rejected and deferredProficiency: error rates or task times after training
Cost and schedule impact of approved changes against contingencyReadiness survey results before go-live
Unapproved changes found in auditsHelp-channel queries over time
Baseline stability: how often the baseline is re-plannedBenefits achieved against the business case

Report both sets together. A project that is on time and on budget but has low adoption is not yet a success.

Common mistakes

  • Treating them as the same thing. A change log does not prepare 1,000 people for a new way of working, and a training plan does not stop scope creep.
  • Starting change management at go-live. By then the schedule has no room for training, and design decisions that create resistance are already made.
  • Sending every change to the board. Without delegated limits, change control becomes slow, and people start bypassing it.
  • Forgetting the 'do nothing' option. Every impact analysis should show the consequence of not making the change.
  • Not recording rejected requests. The same idea comes back repeatedly if there is no record of why it was declined.
  • Leaving line managers out. People take their cues from their own manager more than from project emails.
  • Measuring delivery only. If adoption is not measured, nobody notices the benefits are not arriving until it is too late to act.
  • Ignoring people impact in change requests. An approved change that alters screens or processes needs updated training and communication too.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between change management and change control?

Change control is the formal process for deciding on changes to a project's scope, schedule, cost and deliverables. Change management helps the people affected by the project adopt the new way of working, so the benefits are realised.

Is change control part of change management?

Not in the project sense. They are separate disciplines that answer different questions. In IT service management, however, 'change management' (ITIL 4 change enablement) includes controlling changes to live systems, which is why the terms get mixed up.

Which comes first, change management or change control?

Change management analysis should start first, in initiation and planning, so adoption needs are built into the scope. Change control begins once baselines are agreed and runs until closure. After that, both run in parallel.

What is a change control board?

A group with authority to approve, reject or defer changes to a project above an agreed threshold. It usually includes the sponsor or their delegate, the project manager and representatives of users and suppliers.

What is the difference between a CAB and a CCB?

A change control board (CCB) decides on changes to a project's baselines. A change advisory board (CAB) assesses and approves changes to live IT services. Both control change, but in different contexts.

What are the five stages of ADKAR?

Awareness of the need for change, Desire to take part, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to do it in practice, and Reinforcement to sustain it. ADKAR is a Prosci model used in change management, not change control.

Who is responsible for change management on a project?

The sponsor is usually accountable for adoption, supported by a change manager where the change is large. Line managers carry much of the day-to-day work, and the project manager builds the activities into the plan.

Who approves changes under change control?

It depends on the size of the change. Small changes are often delegated to the project manager, larger ones go to a change control board, and the largest go to the sponsor or steering committee, as set out in the project's governance.

What is integrated change control?

The PMBOK Guide term for reviewing all change requests and managing approved changes across deliverables, documents and the project management plan, so a change in one area is reflected everywhere it affects. It is part of project integration management.

What is change enablement in ITIL 4?

ITIL 4's name for the practice that controls changes to IT services, previously called change management. It aims to maximise successful changes by assessing risk, authorising changes and managing the change schedule, with decisions made by an appropriate change authority.

Do Agile projects need change control?

Agile teams handle most scope change through backlog prioritisation, but they still need formal control for changes to contracts, budgets, fixed dates or the product goal. They also need change management to help users adopt each release.

Does a small project need both change management and change control?

Usually, but both can be light. Change control may be an email agreement between the project manager and sponsor recorded in a log. Change management may be a briefing, a short guide and a check on usage a month later.

How does configuration management relate to change control?

Change control decides whether a change is made. Configuration management tracks the versions of each item and ensures the approved change is applied to the correct version everywhere.

What documents does each discipline produce?

Change control produces change requests, impact analyses, a change log and updated baselines. Change management produces impact assessments, stakeholder and sponsor plans, communication and training plans, a resistance plan and adoption reports.
