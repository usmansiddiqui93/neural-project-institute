Project Timeline: How to Create One, With Examples

A project timeline is a visual, date-ordered view of a project's phases, milestones and key deliverables, showing stakeholders when the important things will happen. It is drawn from the project schedule, which calculates the dates, and presents them on a single page. This guide shows you what to include, the main types, how to build one in eight steps, and a worked example from activity list to finished timeline.

Key takeaways

  • A project timeline communicates dates; the project schedule calculates them. Build the schedule first.
  • Include phases, 5 to 15 milestones, a today line, status and the date it was last updated.
  • Choose the type of timeline for the audience: milestones for sponsors, phase bars for clients, Gantt-style for the team.
  • Dates come from durations and dependencies, so the critical path decides your end date.
  • When dates move, show baseline and forecast side by side and say what you are doing about it.

What is a project timeline?

A project timeline is a chronological, visual summary of a project's main phases, milestones and deadlines, usually on one page. It shows when key work starts and finishes and when decisions or deliveries are due, without the detail of every task. Its job is communication: giving sponsors, clients and the team a shared picture of the plan.

A timeline sits on top of the project schedule. The schedule holds the activities, durations, dependencies and resources, and calculates the dates. The timeline selects the dates that matter to its audience and presents them clearly. If the schedule changes, the timeline changes with it.

  • Created: during planning, once the scope and a first schedule exist.
  • Owned by: the project manager, with dates agreed by the people doing the work.
  • Used: at kick-off, in sponsor and client updates, at phase reviews and whenever dates change.
Tip

A timeline drawn from wishful dates rather than a schedule will be wrong by the first review. Derive every date from durations and dependencies, even if the calculation is a simple spreadsheet.

Why a project timeline matters

  • One shared picture. Everyone sees the same phases and dates, which reduces 'I thought it was due in May' conversations.
  • Early warning. A slipping milestone is visible at a glance, while there is still time to recover.
  • Better decisions. Showing approval points on the timeline makes it clear when you need decisions from sponsors or clients, and what happens if they are late.
  • Realistic expectations. A timeline built from real durations helps you push back on an impossible deadline with evidence.
  • Coordination. Teams, suppliers and other projects can plan their own work around your key dates.

What to include in a project timeline

Keep a timeline lean. Include what the audience needs to understand the plan and act on it, and leave the rest in the schedule.

ElementWhat it showsTip
Phases or workstreamsThe main blocks of work, such as design, build and testUse the same names as the plan and the status report
MilestonesZero-duration points such as approvals, deliveries and go-liveUsually 5 to 15; see our project milestones guide
Key deliverablesWhat will be handed over and whenName them as the client would recognise them
Critical dependenciesHand-offs that drive the end dateShow only those on or near the critical path
Decision pointsDates by which you need a decision from the audienceHighlight them; they are often why the timeline is shared
Today line and statusWhere the project is now, and what is on track or at riskUse a simple, consistent colour key
Baseline and forecastOriginal dates against current expected datesOnly needed once something has moved

Many teams keep two views of the same schedule: a milestone timeline for the sponsor and a Gantt-style timeline for the team.

How to create a project timeline in 8 steps

Step 1: Agree the purpose and audience

Decide who the timeline is for and what they need from it. A sponsor wants five dates and the decisions you need; a delivery team wants every hand-off. This choice drives the level of detail in every later step.

Step 2: Confirm the scope and deliverables

List what the project will deliver, using the project scope statement. Break the work down in a work breakdown structure so nothing is forgotten. Work missing from the WBS will be missing from the timeline.

Step 3: List the activities

Turn each work package into the activities needed to produce it. For a timeline you only need enough detail to calculate the dates reliably, which is usually activities of one to four weeks for a project lasting a few months.

Step 4: Estimate durations

Ask the people who will do the work. Use past projects where you can, and use three-point estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) for uncertain work, as in a PERT chart. Estimate effort and then duration, allowing for availability: ten days of effort from someone available half-time takes four weeks. Our effort estimation guide covers the techniques.

For example, if the build team says the work will take at best 3 weeks, most likely 4 and at worst 8, the PERT estimate is (3 + 4 x 4 + 8) / 6 = 4.5 weeks. Using 4.5 rather than the optimistic 3 is the difference between a timeline that holds and one that slips in the first month.

Step 5: Map dependencies

Record which activities must finish before others can start. Most links are finish-to-start, but some work can overlap. Include external dependencies such as supplier deliveries, approvals and other projects; these are the dates most likely to move.

Step 6: Calculate dates and find the critical path

Starting from the project start date, work forward through the dependencies to calculate each activity's earliest start and finish. The longest chain of dependent activities is the critical path, and it sets the earliest possible end date. Activities off the critical path have float, which tells you where there is room to absorb delays. Use a working calendar that includes public holidays and planned shutdowns.

Step 7: Select milestones and choose a format

Pick the phases and milestones that tell the story for your audience: usually phase ends, approvals, key deliveries and go-live. Choose a timeline type from the table above, mark today's date, add a short colour key and the last-updated date.

Step 8: Review, baseline and share

Walk the draft through with the team and the main stakeholders, check the dates against known holidays and commitments, and agree it. Save the agreed dates as the schedule baseline so you can show later changes honestly. Then share it, and update it whenever the schedule changes, at least as often as you report status.

Worked example: a 19-week website redesign

A marketing team is redesigning its company website. The project starts on Monday 1 February 2027 and the team works five-day weeks. To keep the arithmetic simple, this example ignores public holidays; in a real schedule the Easter bank holidays would move some dates.

1. Activities, durations and dependencies

ADiscovery and requirements3 weeksNone1 to 31 Feb to 19 Feb0
BContent audit2 weeksA4 to 522 Feb to 5 Mar4 weeks
CDesign (wireframes and visual design)4 weeksA4 to 722 Feb to 19 Mar0
DContent writing4 weeksB6 to 98 Mar to 2 Apr4 weeks
EBuild6 weeksC8 to 1322 Mar to 30 Apr0
FContent load2 weeksD and E14 to 153 May to 14 May0
GUser acceptance testing and fixes3 weeksF16 to 1817 May to 4 Jun0
HLaunch preparation and go-live1 weekG197 Jun to 11 Jun0

There are two paths to activity F. Path A, C, E takes 13 weeks; path A, B, D takes 9 weeks. The longer path is the critical path, so the critical path for the whole project is A, C, E, F, G, H = 19 weeks. Content audit and writing have four weeks of float: they can slip by up to four weeks without moving go-live.

2. Choose the milestones

MilestoneDateWhy the sponsor cares
M1 Requirements signed offFri 19 Feb 2027Sponsor approval needed; scope is fixed from here
M2 Design approvedFri 19 Mar 2027Sponsor approval needed; build cannot start without it
M3 Content readyFri 2 Apr 2027Not critical: may slip to 30 Apr without delaying launch
M4 Build completeFri 30 Apr 2027Last chance to see the site before content goes in
M5 Testing signed offFri 4 Jun 2027Go or no-go decision
M6 Go-liveFri 11 Jun 2027The date the business is planning around

3. Draw the one-page timeline

For the sponsor, the team shows a phase timeline by month with milestone diamonds. In a slide or spreadsheet it looks like this:

PhaseFebMarAprMayJun
Discovery■■ ◆M1
Design■■■ ◆M2
Content■■■◆M3
Build■■■ ◆M4
Content load and testing■■◆M5

Behind this six-row view sits the eight-activity schedule above, and on a larger project perhaps a few hundred activities. The sponsor sees the two approvals they own, the go-live date and the fact that content has some slack, which is exactly what they need.

When the timeline slips: updating and recovering

Suppose design approval (M2) takes two weeks longer than planned because the sponsor asks for a second round of changes. Design is on the critical path, so every later critical activity moves by two weeks. Content writing does not move, because it has float.

MilestoneBaselineForecastVariance
M2 Design approved19 Mar2 Apr+2 weeks
M3 Content ready2 Apr2 AprNone (float absorbs it)
M4 Build complete30 Apr14 May+2 weeks
M5 Testing signed off4 Jun18 Jun+2 weeks
M6 Go-live11 Jun25 Jun+2 weeks

Show baseline and forecast side by side rather than quietly redrawing the timeline. Then set out the options and their trade-offs, so the sponsor can decide:

  • Fast-track: start loading content into finished page templates while the rest of the build continues, overlapping E and F. This recovers about a week but adds rework risk.
  • Crash: add a second developer for the last three weeks of build. This costs money and recovers about a week, if the work can be split.
  • Re-scope: launch with the main pages on 11 June and add the remaining sections a fortnight later.
  • Accept: move go-live to 25 June and tell everyone planning around it now.

Our guide to fast-tracking vs crashing explains both techniques in more detail, and schedule variance shows how to measure slippage in earned value terms. Delays that you saw coming should already be in the risk register; see project risk management.

Notice that the approvals (budget, design, permissions, contract) are usually the milestones the sponsor cares about most. They are also the dates most likely to slip, so give each one its own duration in the schedule rather than assuming it happens overnight.

How to make a project timeline in Excel or PowerPoint

Spreadsheets and slides are fine for one-off or small-project timelines. The main drawback is that they do not recalculate when a dependency moves, so you must update them by hand.

In Excel (or Google Sheets)

  1. Create a table with three columns: phase or task name, start date and duration in days.
  2. Select the table and insert a stacked bar chart.
  3. Click the first data series (the start dates) and set its fill to no fill. The remaining bars now float at the right dates.
  4. Format the vertical axis and tick categories in reverse order so the first phase is at the top.
  5. Set the horizontal axis minimum to your project start date (and the maximum to the end date) so the bars fill the chart.
  6. Add milestones as a separate row with a duration of zero and a marker, or as labelled shapes.
  7. Add a vertical line or shape for today's date and a text box with the last-updated date.

In PowerPoint

  1. Draw a horizontal line or a row of month boxes across the slide.
  2. Add a bar per phase using rectangles, and a diamond per milestone with its date underneath.
  3. Use one colour per status (for example green, amber and red) and explain it in a small key.
  4. Copy the dates from the schedule, never from memory, and note the source and last-updated date on the slide.

Project timelines in Agile projects

Agile teams plan in short iterations and expect scope to change, so they rarely publish a fixed activity-by-activity timeline. They still need to tell stakeholders when value will arrive.

  • Release timeline: shows sprints or iterations and the planned release dates, with the main features expected in each. See Agile release planning.
  • Roadmap timeline: shows themes or outcomes by quarter, with dates becoming firmer as they get closer. Our product roadmap template is a starting point.
  • Fixed date, flexible scope: when a date is fixed, such as a regulatory deadline, the timeline holds the date and the team adjusts what is included in each release.

Hybrid projects often combine both: a firm timeline for hardware, contracts and go-live, and a sprint-based release timeline for the software inside it.

Tips for presenting a project timeline to stakeholders

  • Lead with the decision you need. 'We need design approval by 19 March to hold the June launch' is more useful than a list of dates.
  • Use plain language. Write 'Design approved', not 'WP3.2 complete'.
  • Show uncertainty honestly. If a date depends on a supplier or an approval, say so, and consider showing a range for later phases.
  • Keep one version. Generate the timeline from the schedule or a single master file so different decks never show different dates.
  • Pair it with a status update. Add the timeline to your project status report so movement is explained, not just shown.
  • Use colour sparingly. Reserve red and amber for real problems; if everything is coloured, nothing stands out.

Project timeline tools and templates

For small projects, a spreadsheet template is enough. For larger projects, or when several people update the plan, project management software is easier, because its timeline or Gantt view is generated from the live schedule and moves automatically when a dependency changes.

Frequently asked questions

What is a project timeline?

A project timeline is a visual, date-ordered summary of a project's main phases, milestones and deadlines. It shows stakeholders when the key things will happen, without the detail of every task.

What is the difference between a project timeline and a project schedule?

The schedule is the detailed model: activities, durations, dependencies and resources, from which dates are calculated. The timeline is a simplified view of the schedule's key dates for communication. Build the schedule first, then draw the timeline from it.

What is the difference between a project timeline and a Gantt chart?

A Gantt chart shows every activity as a bar with its dependencies and is used by the team to plan and track work. A timeline shows only phases and milestones and is used to communicate. A summary Gantt chart can serve as a timeline.

What should a project timeline include?

Phases or workstreams, key milestones and deliverables, critical dependencies, decision points, a today line, status, baseline and forecast dates once anything has moved, and the date it was last updated.

Who is responsible for creating the project timeline?

The project manager usually builds and maintains it, but the dates should come from the people doing the work, and the sponsor or client should agree the key milestones.

When is a project timeline created?

During planning, after the scope is agreed and a first schedule exists. A rough timeline may appear earlier in a business case or project charter, but treat those dates as estimates until the schedule is built.

How many milestones should a project timeline have?

Usually between five and fifteen. Fewer may hide important approvals; more turns the timeline into a task list. Larger projects can show milestones by phase.

Are milestones the same as deadlines?

Not quite. A milestone marks a significant point, such as an approval or delivery, and has no duration. A deadline is a date by which something must be done. Many milestones have deadlines, but not every deadline is a milestone.

How often should a project timeline be updated?

Whenever a key date changes, and at least as often as you report status, which is often weekly or fortnightly. Keep the baseline so you can show what has moved.

How do you estimate a project timeline?

Break the work into activities, estimate each duration with the people doing it, map the dependencies and calculate the dates forwards from the start. The longest chain of dependent activities, the critical path, gives the earliest end date.

How long should a project timeline be?

As long as the schedule says, not as long as the deadline allows. On the page, a stakeholder timeline should fit one slide or sheet. For long projects, show the next three to six months in detail and later phases as summary bars.

What is the timeline section of a project proposal?

A short overview of the proposed phases, milestones and end date, so the reader can judge whether the plan is realistic. Mark the dates as estimates that will be confirmed once the detailed schedule is built and the project is approved.

Can I make a project timeline in Excel?

Yes. A stacked bar chart with the start-date series hidden creates a simple timeline, or you can use a ready-made template. Excel does not recalculate dates when dependencies change, so update it by hand.

What does a project timeline look like in Agile?

Usually a release timeline or roadmap showing sprints, planned releases and the main features or outcomes in each, with near-term dates firm and later ones indicative.
