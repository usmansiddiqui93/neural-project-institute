Capacity Planning in Project Management: Steps and Example

Capacity planning in project management is the process of comparing the work your projects and day-to-day operations will demand with the productive time and skills your people can actually supply, so you can spot shortfalls before they delay delivery. This guide gives you the formulas, a step-by-step process, three worked examples and the decisions to make when demand and capacity do not match.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 30 September 2026
  • Last updated 4 November 2026
  • 18 min read

Key takeaways

  • Capacity planning compares demand (hours of work needed) with productive capacity (hours people can really give) for a team, skill or portfolio over a set period.
  • Productive capacity = people x hours x weeks, minus leave and holidays, multiplied by a productive-time factor for meetings, admin and support.
  • Plan by skill or role, not by headcount: a surplus of testers does not cancel out a shortage of developers.
  • Include business-as-usual work and weight pipeline projects by their likelihood, or the plan will look better than reality.
  • Do not plan to 100%. Keep a buffer, often 10-15% of productive capacity, for urgent work and estimating error.
  • Review the plan on a fixed cadence: monthly for portfolios, every sprint or fortnight for teams.

What is capacity planning in project management?

Capacity planning is the process of estimating how much productive work a team, department or organisation can deliver in a period, comparing it with the work requested, and acting on the difference. It answers one question before work starts: do we have enough of the right people, at the right time, to deliver what we have promised?

It works on two sides of a balance:

  • Supply (capacity): the hours of productive time available, by person, role or skill, after leave, holidays and non-project work are taken out.
  • Demand: the hours of work required by approved projects, likely future projects and the business-as-usual work that never goes away.

It looks weeks, months or years ahead and works with teams, roles and portfolios rather than individual tasks. Typical questions: can we start this project next month, which skill runs out first, and do we need to hire, contract or say no? Once capacity planning confirms the work fits, resource allocation assigns named people to specific tasks and project scheduling sets the dates.

Capacity, demand, allocation and utilisation

These terms are often used loosely. Agreeing what each one means is the first step to a capacity plan people trust.

TermMeaningExample
Gross capacityTotal contracted hours for the period5 people x 40 hours x 4 weeks = 800 hours
AvailabilityGross capacity minus leave, public holidays and training800 - 40 hours of leave = 760 hours
Productive capacityThe part of available time that can go on planned work, after meetings, admin and support760 x 80% = 608 hours
DemandHours of work requested by projects and business-as-usual activity680 hours across two projects and routine requests
AllocationWhich work is assigned to which person or teamAna: 24 hours a week on Project A
UtilisationAllocated or demanded hours divided by capacity, as a percentage680 / 608 = 112%
Capacity gapDemand minus productive capacity; positive means a shortfall680 - 608 = 72 hours short

Capacity planning vs resource planning vs resource allocation

The three activities overlap and the names vary between organisations, but the distinction below is the most useful in practice.

Main questionDo we have enough of each skill for all the work we want to do?What resources does this project need, and when?Who exactly does which task?
ScopeTeam, department or portfolioOne project or programmeOne project or team

When individual people are overloaded inside an approved plan, the fix is resource levelling, which moves tasks within float or extends the schedule. Capacity planning works one level up and decides how much work should enter the system in the first place. For a wider view of people, equipment and materials, see our guide to project resources.

Capacity planning and Agile teams

Agile teams turn the problem round: the team is fixed, so they ask how much work fits into its capacity each sprint, using availability and past velocity. Portfolio-level planning still decides which teams work on which initiatives. See our guide to agile capacity planning for the sprint-level formula.

Why capacity planning matters

Most organisations approve projects one at a time, each with a plausible plan. Trouble starts when those plans all draw on the same specialists in the same months. Capacity planning helps you:

  • Set realistic dates, checked against the hours actually available.
  • Protect people from overload, which shows up as long hours, then mistakes and resignations.
  • Find skill shortages early, while there is time to recruit, train or contract.
  • Make portfolio decisions on evidence: what can start now and what must wait.
  • Reduce multitasking and budget accurately, since capacity converts directly into cost.

Skipping it rarely causes a single dramatic failure. Instead, projects all run a little late and shared experts become bottlenecks. Our guide to managing multiple projects covers the day-to-day side.

Strategic, tactical and operational capacity planning

Capacity planning happens at three levels. Each has a different horizon, level of detail and owner, and they should feed each other.

LevelHorizonUnit of planningTypical decisionsUsual owner
Strategic1-3 yearsFTE by role or capabilityHiring plans, new teams, outsourcing, building a capabilityLeadership, HR, portfolio board
Tactical1-12 months, usually a quarterHours or FTE by skill, per monthWhich projects start, sequencing, contractors, trainingPMO, resource managers
Operational1-6 weeksHours by person, per week or sprintWho works on what, rebalancing, short-term overtimeProject managers, team leads

This guide concentrates on the tactical level, but the same method scales up and down.

Capacity planning formulas

You need only a handful of formulas. Use the same period (week, month or quarter) for every figure in the calculation.

MeasureFormula
Gross capacityNumber of people x contracted hours per week x weeks in the period
Available capacityGross capacity - (leave + public holidays + planned training)
Productive capacityAvailable capacity x productive-time factor
Total demandCommitted project hours + business-as-usual hours + (pipeline hours x probability)
Capacity gapTotal demand - productive capacity
UtilisationTotal demand / productive capacity x 100
Gap in FTECapacity gap / productive hours one FTE supplies in the period
Tip

Productive capacity = (people x hours x weeks - leave and holidays) x productive-time factor. Compare demand with this figure, never with contracted hours.

Calculate separately for each role that matters; decisions are made skill by skill. Converting gaps into full-time equivalents (FTE) makes them easier to discuss: "1.3 developers short" is clearer than "378 hours short".

How to do capacity planning step by step

This process works for a single team or a whole portfolio. The first cycle takes longest; after that it is a regular update.

  1. Set the scope and horizon, for example one department for the next quarter in monthly buckets.
  2. Define the roles or skills to plan, such as developers, testers and analysts. A skills matrix shows who can cover more than one role.
  3. Calculate gross capacity by role from actual contracts, so part-timers count at their real hours.
  4. Subtract known absence: leave, public holidays, training and long-term absence.
  5. Apply a productive-time factor, derived from timesheets where you have them.
  6. Collect demand from every source, by role and month: committed projects, business-as-usual and weighted pipeline projects.
  7. Compare demand with capacity and flag every role and period above your target utilisation.
  8. Decide how to close gaps and record the decision and who made it.
  9. Publish a one-page view for project managers, functional heads and the portfolio board, with assumptions.
  10. Review on a fixed cadence and compare planned with actual utilisation to improve your factors.

Estimating demand

Demand is where most capacity plans go wrong, because it is easy to count projects and forget everything else. Build it from four sources:

Demand sourceHow to estimate itCommon trap
Committed projectsRemaining effort by role and month from each project's estimate or scheduleUsing the original estimate instead of the remaining work
Pipeline projectsRough effort by role x probability of approvalLeaving them out until they are approved, then discovering there is no room
Business-as-usualAverage hours from the last 3-6 months of timesheets or ticket dataAssuming it is zero because it is not in a project plan
Support and incidentsHistorical average plus a margin for peaksIgnoring on-call rotas and release support

Remaining effort should come from the people closest to the work; see effort estimation for three-point and bottom-up techniques.

Choosing a productive-time factor

Meetings, email, admin, line management, support and context switching all take a share of contracted time. The best source for your factor is your own timesheets: hours booked to planned work divided by hours available over a few months. Without data, many teams start around 70-80% for specialists and lower for leads and managers, then adjust. Treat these as starting assumptions, not benchmarks.

Worked example 1: one month for a team of five

This is an illustrative example. A design team has five people working 40-hour weeks. The month has four working weeks. Planned leave totals 40 hours, and timesheets show about 20% of time goes on meetings, admin and support, so the productive-time factor is 80%.

StepCalculationHours
Gross capacity5 people x 40 h x 4 weeks800
Less leave800 - 40760
Productive capacity760 x 80%608
Demand sourceHours
Project A (website redesign)320
Project B (brand refresh)240
Business-as-usual requests120
Total demand680

Demand of 680 hours exceeds productive capacity of 608 hours by 72 hours, so utilisation is 680 / 608 = 112%, roughly 12% over. Expressed in FTE, one designer supplies 160 x 80% = 128 productive hours a month, so the gap is 72 / 128 = 0.56 FTE.

The team lead takes three options to the head of marketing:

  • Move 72 hours of Project B into next month, delaying the brand refresh by about two weeks.
  • Bring in a freelance designer for about two weeks to cover the shortfall.
  • Pause lower-priority business-as-usual requests for the month, with an agreed list of what waits.

Without the numbers the team would simply have worked late and missed some dates. With them, the business chooses the trade-off.

Worked example 2: a quarterly skill-based capacity plan

This example is also illustrative. A PMO is planning the next quarter for an IT delivery team of 11 people: 6 developers, 3 testers and 2 business analysts. The quarter has 12 working weeks after public holidays, the standard week is 37.5 hours, and each person averages 1.5 weeks of planned leave, leaving 10.5 available weeks each. Looked at as a whole, the team is only 4% over capacity, which might pass as "tight but manageable". By role, developers are 21% over while testers have spare time. One developer supplies 37.5 x 10.5 x 75% = about 295 productive hours a quarter, so the developer gap is 378 / 295 = 1.3 FTE. Even without Project Gamma, developers would be 78 hours short (about 0.26 FTE), so the shortage is real and not just a pipeline risk.

This is why planning by headcount fails: "11 people, 104% loaded" invites "work a bit harder", while "1.3 developers short from week one" invites a decision.

What to do when demand exceeds capacity

You have a limited set of levers, each with a cost. The right mix depends on priorities, budget and how long the gap lasts.

OptionHow it worksBest whenTrade-off
ReprioritiseStop, pause or defer lower-value workThe portfolio has more ideas than capacity, which is commonSomeone's project waits; needs a clear decision-maker
RescheduleMove the start or phases to a period with spare capacityDates are flexible or the peak is shortBenefits arrive later
Reduce scopeCut or postpone lower-priority featuresSome requirements are nice to haveLess delivered in this release
Rebalance skillsMove work to roles with spare time or cross-trained staffSkills overlap, for example testers automating testsLower productivity at first; needs training
Contractors or partnersBuy capacity for a fixed periodThe gap is temporary and the skill is available in the marketDay rates, onboarding time, knowledge leaving at the end
HireAdd permanent capacityThe gap is lasting and strategicRecruitment lead time; ongoing cost
OvertimeAsk people to work extra hoursShort, genuine peaks onlyFatigue, errors and attrition if it becomes normal

Inside individual projects, resource levelling and techniques from schedule compression help once the portfolio decision has been made. Watch for scope creep: unmanaged additions quietly increase demand after the capacity plan has been agreed.

Worked example 3: closing the developer gap

Continuing the illustrative example, the PMO models the options for the 378-hour developer shortfall.

OptionEffect on developersCost or consequence
A. Defer Project Gamma's start to next quarterDemand falls by 300 hoursGamma's benefits arrive about three months later
B. Testers take on 80 hours of test-automation scripting estimated as developer workDemand falls by 80 hoursUses tester surplus; small learning curve
C. Contract developer at 0.5 FTE for 10 weeksCapacity rises by about 159 hours (187.5 hours x 85% productive)Contractor cost for 10 weeks
D. Cut 120 hours of lower-priority Beta featuresDemand falls by 120 hoursFeatures move to a later release
E. Two hours' overtime a week for all developersCapacity rises by about 126 hoursUnsustainable over a quarter; not recommended

Options A and B together remove 380 hours, bringing developer demand to 1,770 hours against 1,772 hours of capacity. On paper the gap is closed, but utilisation is 100%, with nothing left for urgent defects or estimating error. The portfolio board therefore also approves option C. Developer capacity becomes about 1,931 hours against 1,770 hours of demand, a utilisation of about 92%, leaving roughly 160 hours of buffer. Option D is held in reserve if Gamma is approved early.

Tip

Closing the gap to exactly zero is not the goal. Aim for a plan that leaves a buffer, because real work always contains surprises.

What to do with surplus capacity

In example 2 the testers had about 175 spare hours. Decide how to use spare capacity rather than letting it fill with low-value work:

  • Pull forward approved work waiting on that skill, or absorb work from a constrained role.
  • Invest in improvement: automation, documentation or technical debt that frees capacity later.
  • Train and cross-skill, using a training plan.
  • Lend capacity to another team, or reduce contractor use.

A surplus that persists for several cycles suggests the skill mix no longer matches the work.

Capacity planning strategies: lead, lag and match

When demand is growing, there are three classic strategies for adding capacity. They come from operations management but apply equally to project teams.

StrategyWhat it meansWhen to use itMain risk
LeadAdd capacity before demand arrivesGrowth is near certain and delay is costly, for example a signed programme of workPaying for idle capacity if demand does not appear
LagAdd capacity only once demand is confirmedBudgets are tight or demand is uncertainOverload and delays while you recruit
MatchAdd capacity in small steps as demand growsMost project organisations; balances cost and riskNeeds frequent reviews to time each step
Fixed-team (Agile)Keep stable teams and prioritise work into their capacityProduct teams with continuous demandLow-priority work may wait a long time

Many organisations combine them: a permanent core for steady demand, contractors for confirmed peaks and early recruitment for strategic skills.

Utilisation targets and buffers

It is tempting to plan everyone at 100% of productive capacity. In practice, a fully loaded team has no room to absorb urgent work, estimating error or sickness, so queues form and every item waits longer. The same idea underpins WIP limits in Kanban and the shared buffers in critical chain project management.

  • Keep a buffer of unallocated productive capacity, often 10-15%, and protect it.
  • Plan shared specialists more lightly than generalists, because they are pulled in many directions.
  • In agencies, keep planned project utilisation separate from billable utilisation.
  • Watch the trend: planned at 90% but running at 110% every month means your estimates or factors are wrong.

There is no universal correct rate. Set a target per role and compare it with actuals each cycle.

Who owns capacity planning and how often to review it

Capacity planning works when ownership is clear and everyone knows when the numbers will be refreshed.

RoleResponsibility
Portfolio board or sponsorMakes the priority calls when demand exceeds capacity
PMO or resource managerOwns the capacity model, consolidates demand, runs the review and reports gaps
Functional or line managersProvide capacity by role: headcount, leave, skills, recruitment plans
Project managersProvide remaining demand by role and month, and flag changes early
Team leadsKeep operational allocation and actual utilisation up to date
Finance and HRTurn gaps into budgets, hiring plans and contractor approvals

In many organisations the project management office is the natural owner because it already sees the whole portfolio. Decisions about which projects to start belong to portfolio management; see program vs project vs portfolio management for how the levels fit together.

Plan levelTypical review cadence
StrategicAnnually, refreshed quarterly
Tactical (portfolio)Monthly
Operational (team)Weekly, fortnightly or every sprint
Any levelImmediately after a major change: a large project approved, cancelled or re-scoped, or a key person leaving

Comparing forecast with actual at each review improves your factors, estimates and project forecasting.

Capacity planning tools and templates

A spreadsheet is enough for one team or a small portfolio: a tab for capacity by role, a tab for demand by project and a summary of gaps and utilisation. Keep demand aligned with each project's project schedule template or project tracker.

Once several projects share people, project management software saves manual consolidation. Workload features often sit on higher plans:

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, shows team workload and capacity, accounts for working hours and holidays, and includes time tracking. Advanced resource and capacity planning, including role-based planning, is among its Pinnacle capabilities, which suits PMOs.
  • monday.com has a visual workload view on Pro, with resource management on Enterprise.
  • Asana includes workload, portfolios and time tracking on Advanced.
  • Smartsheet has workload tracking on Business and above, plus a Resource Management add-on.
  • Jira Premium adds Plans for cross-team roadmaps and capacity.

Whatever the tool, the inputs matter most: honest remaining estimates, real leave data and an evidence-based productive-time factor.

Common capacity planning mistakes

  • Counting contracted hours as capacity. Plans built on 40 productive hours a week are short before they start.
  • Planning by headcount instead of skill. Totals hide the bottleneck role, as example 2 shows.
  • Ignoring business-as-usual and support. Routine work is real demand even if no project plan lists it.
  • Leaving out the pipeline. Unapproved projects become approved projects; weight them by probability.
  • Using original estimates instead of remaining work. Late projects need more hours than their plans say.
  • Planning to 100%. There is no room for surprises, so planned work slips when they arrive.
  • Treating it as a one-off exercise. An unreviewed plan is out of date within weeks.
  • Hiding the gap. Absorbing shortfalls with overtime protects today's dates at the expense of next quarter's.

Frequently asked questions

What is capacity planning in project management?

It is the process of comparing the work demanded by projects and day-to-day operations with the productive time and skills available, for a team or portfolio over a set period, so that gaps can be fixed before they delay delivery.

How do you calculate capacity for capacity planning?

Multiply the number of people by their contracted hours and the weeks in the period, subtract leave, holidays and training, then multiply by a productive-time factor, such as 75-80%, to allow for meetings, admin and support. Do this separately for each role or skill.

What is an example of capacity planning?

Five designers have 800 contracted hours in a month; after leave and an 80% productive factor that is 608 hours. Demand is 680 hours, so the team is 72 hours (0.56 FTE) short and must defer work, add a freelancer or pause requests.

What is the difference between capacity planning and resource planning?

Capacity planning compares total supply with total demand across a team or portfolio, usually by role and over months. Resource planning works out what one project needs, and resource allocation assigns named people to specific tasks.

What is a good utilisation rate in capacity planning?

There is no single correct figure. Many teams plan productive capacity at roughly 85-90% so that a buffer of 10-15% remains for urgent work and estimating error. Set targets per role and compare them with actuals.

What are the three capacity planning strategies?

Lead adds capacity before demand arrives, lag waits until demand is confirmed, and match adds capacity in small steps as demand grows. Agile organisations often use a fourth approach: fixed teams with work prioritised into their capacity.

What is the difference between capacity and availability?

Availability is the time someone is at work and not on leave. Capacity, in the productive sense, is the part of that available time that can go on planned work once meetings, admin and support are taken out.

How often should capacity plans be updated?

Monthly is common for portfolio plans, weekly or every sprint for team plans, and annually with quarterly refreshes for strategic workforce plans. Update immediately after a major change in projects or people.

Who is responsible for capacity planning?

Usually the PMO or a resource manager owns the model, line managers supply capacity, project managers supply demand, and a portfolio board or sponsor makes the priority decisions when demand exceeds capacity.

How do you include projects that are not yet approved?

Estimate their effort by role and multiply it by the probability of approval. A 500-hour project with a 60% chance adds 300 hours of weighted demand. Replace the weighted figure with the full estimate once it is approved.

How does capacity planning work in Agile?

Agile teams estimate how much work fits each sprint from team availability, a focus factor and past velocity, then commit only to that. See our agile capacity planning guide for the formula.

What tools are used for capacity planning?

Spreadsheets work for small teams. Larger portfolios use the workload and resource features of tools such as Wrike, monday.com, Asana, Smartsheet or Jira, which often sit on higher-tier plans or add-ons.

How does FTE relate to capacity planning?

FTE expresses capacity and demand in full-time-person units, which makes gaps easy to communicate, for example "1.3 developers short next quarter". See our guide to full-time equivalent.
