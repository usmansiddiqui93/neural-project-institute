Project Cost Management: Processes, Tools and Examples

Project cost management is the work of planning, estimating, budgeting and controlling costs so that a project is completed within its approved budget. It turns scope and schedule into money, sets a baseline, and then tracks actual spending against it so problems are spotted early.

By Neural Project InstitutePublished 5 October 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Cost management covers planning, estimating, budgeting and controlling costs.
  • The cost baseline is the time-phased budget that performance is measured against.
  • Earned value shows whether money spent is buying the planned amount of work.
  • Forecast early and often so overruns are caught while they are still small.

The four core processes

The PMBOK Guide (6th edition) describes cost management as four processes. Later editions organise the material differently, but the underlying work is the same.

ProcessWhat it producesKey question
Plan cost managementA cost management planHow will we estimate, budget and control costs?
Estimate costsCost estimates and their basisWhat will each piece of work cost?
Determine budgetThe cost baseline and funding requirementsHow much do we need, and when?
Control costsPerformance measures, forecasts and change requestsAre we on budget, and where will we finish?

Types of project cost

Cost typeMeaningExample
DirectTraceable to the projectContractor fees, project software licences
IndirectShared overheads allocated to the projectOffice rent, finance team time
FixedDoes not change with volume of workA one-off equipment purchase
VariableChanges with the amount of workHourly labour, materials per unit
SunkAlready spent and cannot be recoveredMoney spent on a cancelled design
OpportunityThe value of the best alternative given upProfit from a project you chose not to run
Tip

Sunk costs should not drive decisions about the future. Ask what it will cost from today to finish, and whether the remaining benefits justify it.

How cost management works in practice

  1. Agree how costs will be estimated, measured and reported in the cost management plan.
  2. Break the work down with a work breakdown structure and estimate each work package.
  3. Add contingency reserves for known risks and roll the estimates up into a time-phased cost baseline.
  4. Secure funding, including any management reserve held by the sponsor.
  5. Record actual costs as work is done and compare them with the plan using earned value management.
  6. Forecast the final cost, take corrective action, and raise change requests when the baseline must change.

Worked example

A website rebuild has a cost baseline of $200,000 spread over eight months. At the end of month four the plan said $80,000 of work should be done (planned value). The team has completed work worth $70,000 (earned value) but spent $85,000 (actual cost).

  • Cost variance = EV - AC = 70,000 - 85,000 = -$15,000 (over budget).
  • Cost performance index = EV / AC = 70,000 / 85,000 = 0.82: each dollar spent is producing about 82 cents of planned work.
  • Estimate at completion = BAC / CPI = 200,000 / 0.8235 = about $242,857 if this efficiency continues.

The project manager now has a clear, early signal and can look for the cause before the overrun grows.

Tools and techniques

  • Cost estimation methods: analogous, parametric, bottom-up and three-point.
  • Reserve analysis for contingency and management reserves.
  • Earned value management and the S-curve.
  • Financial appraisal: cost-benefit analysis, net present value and return on investment.
  • Spreadsheets or project management software with budget and timesheet features.

Common mistakes

  • Estimating before the scope is clear, then treating the estimate as a promise.
  • Leaving out indirect costs, licences, training or the cost of internal staff.
  • Tracking spend only, without measuring how much work it bought.
  • Hiding contingency inside individual estimates instead of showing it openly.
  • Waiting for month-end finance reports instead of tracking costs weekly.

Frequently asked questions

What is project cost management?

It is the set of processes used to plan, estimate, budget and control project costs so the project finishes within its approved budget.

What are the four processes of project cost management?

In PMBOK 6 they are plan cost management, estimate costs, determine budget and control costs.

Why is cost management important in projects?

Without it, overspending is often discovered too late to fix. Good cost management gives early warning and supports better decisions about scope and priorities.

What is a cost baseline?

The approved, time-phased budget for the project's work, including contingency reserves but excluding management reserve. Performance is measured against it.

What is the difference between a budget and an estimate?

An estimate is a prediction of what work will cost. A budget is the approved amount of money, built from the estimates plus reserves.

What are direct and indirect costs?

Direct costs can be traced to the project, such as contractor fees. Indirect costs are shared overheads, such as office space, allocated to it.

Who is responsible for project cost management?

The project manager manages costs day to day, while the sponsor approves the budget and holds the management reserve.

What is earned value management?

A technique that compares planned value, earned value and actual cost to measure cost and schedule performance; see earned value management.

How do you control costs on a project?

Track actual costs against the baseline regularly, analyse variances, forecast the final cost and take corrective action or raise change requests early.

Do Agile projects use cost management?

Yes. Agile teams often fix cost per sprint or release and vary scope, tracking burn rate and value delivered rather than detailed task budgets.

What is a cost overrun?

Spending more than the approved budget. Common causes are unclear scope, optimistic estimates, scope creep and poor tracking.
