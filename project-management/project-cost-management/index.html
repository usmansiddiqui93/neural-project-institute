Project Cost Management: Processes, Steps and Examples

Project cost management is the work of planning, estimating, budgeting and controlling a project's costs so it delivers its scope within an approved budget. It runs through four connected processes: plan cost management, estimate costs, determine budget and control costs. This guide explains each one, shows how the PMBOK editions describe them, and follows a single project through all four with real numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Project cost management has four processes: plan cost management, estimate costs, determine budget and control costs.
  • PMBOK 8 places this work in a Finance performance domain, but the underlying work is the same as in earlier editions.
  • The cost baseline is the work package estimates plus contingency reserve; adding management reserve gives the total project budget.
  • Earned value compares the value of work done with what it cost, which shows overruns long before the money runs out.
  • Forecast the final cost every reporting period and act while the variance is still small.
  • In Agile delivery, team cost per sprint is fairly fixed, so scope becomes the main lever for staying on budget.

What is project cost management?

Project cost management is the set of processes used to plan, estimate, budget, fund, monitor and control the costs of a project so that it can be completed within its approved budget. It turns scope and schedule into money, sets a cost baseline, and then compares actual spending and progress against that baseline so problems are found early.

Cost is one side of the classic triple constraint: change the scope or the schedule and the cost usually changes too. That is why cost management cannot be done in isolation. It depends on a clear project scope, a realistic schedule and the resource plan, and it is pulled together through project integration management.

A project budget is one output of cost management, not the same thing: an estimate predicts cost, the budget is the approved money, and cost accounting records what was actually spent. Good cost management also covers how the budget was built, how it will be tracked and what happens when reality differs from the plan.

Why project cost management matters

Most projects are approved on the basis of a business case that compares costs with expected benefits. If the costs drift and nobody notices, the business case can quietly stop making sense. Cost management protects that decision. In practice it gives you:

  • Early warning. Earned value and regular forecasting show an overrun when it is a few per cent, not when the money has gone.
  • Better decisions about scope. When the sponsor can see what each piece of work costs, trade-offs become explicit rather than political.
  • Reliable funding. Finance teams need to know how much money is needed and when, especially for capital projects funded in tranches.
  • Better estimates next time. Recorded actual costs become the historical data that makes future estimates more accurate.

The four project cost management processes

The PMBOK Guide 6th edition, still the most widely taught description, sets out four processes. They follow each other in planning and then repeat during delivery.

ProcessKey questionMain inputsMain outputs
1. Plan cost managementHow will we estimate, budget, track and control costs?Project charter, organisational policies, finance rulesCost management plan
2. Estimate costsWhat will each piece of work cost?Scope baseline (WBS), schedule, resource needs, risk register, ratesCost estimates and their basis of estimate
3. Determine budgetHow much money do we need, and when?Cost estimates, schedule, risk register, contractsCost baseline and project funding requirements
4. Control costsAre we on budget, and where will we finish?Cost baseline, actual costs, progress dataPerformance measures, forecasts, change requests

1. Plan cost management

This process sets the rules before any numbers are produced. The output, the cost management plan, records the currency and units, how precise estimates should be, how progress will be measured, which variances trigger action, who can approve spending and how reserves are released. It is a component of the project management plan. Our cost management plan guide has a full example.

2. Estimate costs

Here the team predicts the cost of every work package in the work breakdown structure: labour, materials, equipment, services, licences, travel and any other cost. Common methods are analogous, parametric, bottom-up and three-point estimating. A good estimate states a range of accuracy and is backed by a basis of estimate that records assumptions, sources and exclusions.

3. Determine budget

The estimates are added up, contingency reserves are added for identified risks, and the result is spread over time to form the cost baseline: the approved, time-phased budget that performance is measured against. Adding management reserve gives the total project budget. The funding requirements then show how much money is needed in each period, which may be released in tranches at stage gates.

4. Control costs

During delivery, the project manager records actual costs, measures progress, compares both with the baseline and forecasts the final cost. Earned value management is the standard technique. When variances breach the agreed thresholds, the project manager investigates, takes corrective action and, if the baseline itself must change, raises a change request through change control. Our guide to cost control covers this in depth.

Cost management in PMBOK 6, 7 and 8

PMI has described the same work in different ways across recent editions of the PMBOK Guide. If you are studying, or your organisation uses an older template, this mapping helps.

EditionHow cost is organisedProcess or area names
6th edition (2017)Project Cost Management is one of ten knowledge areas, with four processesPlan Cost Management, Estimate Costs, Determine Budget, Control Costs
7th edition (2021)Principles-based. No process chapters; cost content sits mainly in the Planning and Measurement performance domainsEstimating, budgeting and earned value appear as concepts and methods
8th edition (published November 2025)Six principles, seven performance domains and 40 processes. Cost sits in the Finance performance domainPlan Financial Management, Estimate Costs, Develop Budget, Monitor and Control Finances

The 8th edition's Finance domain widens the view slightly: as well as costs, it covers funding sources, financing and the value the project creates for the organisation. The core steps of planning, estimating, budgeting and controlling are recognisably the same as in the 6th edition, which is why this guide uses the familiar four-process structure.

Tip

If you are preparing for an exam, check which edition and exam content outline your exam is based on, and learn the process names it uses. The concepts in this guide apply to all of them.

Types of project costs

Classifying costs helps you make sure nothing is missed in the estimate and that actual costs are recorded in the right place.

Cost typeMeaningProject example
DirectCan be traced to the projectContractor fees, project-specific software licences, materials
IndirectShared costs allocated to the projectOffice space, IT support, finance and HR time
FixedDoes not change with the amount of workA one-off equipment purchase, a fixed-price supplier contract
VariableRises or falls with the amount of workHourly labour, materials per unit, cloud usage
SunkAlready spent and cannot be recoveredMoney spent on a design that was later abandoned
OpportunityThe value of the best alternative given upBenefits from a project you chose not to run
Capital (CapEx)Spend on assets with a life beyond the current yearServers, buildings, capitalised software development
Operating (OpEx)Day-to-day spend charged in the year it occursTraining, subscriptions, maintenance

The CapEx and OpEx split matters because many organisations fund and approve the two differently. Agree with finance early which project costs can be capitalised.

Tip

Sunk costs should not drive decisions about the future. When a project is in trouble, ask what it will cost from today to finish and whether the remaining benefits justify that amount.

Contingency reserve vs management reserve

Reserves are where many budgets go wrong, either because they are hidden inside task estimates or because nobody agreed who can spend them.

Contingency reserveManagement reserve
CoversIdentified risks (known unknowns)Unforeseen work within scope (unknown unknowns)
How it is sizedRisk analysis, such as expected monetary value or Monte Carlo simulationOften a percentage set by organisational policy
Part of the cost baseline?YesNo
Who controls itUsually the project manager, within agreed rulesUsually the sponsor or management
Used in earned value?Moved into work package budgets when releasedAdded to the baseline only through change control

The budget is built up in layers:

LayerFormula
Work package estimatesSum of activity cost estimates
+ Contingency reserve= Cost baseline
+ Management reserve= Total project budget

Each contingency amount should trace back to a risk in the risk register, and the response to each risk should be described in your contingency plan.

How to manage project costs step by step

  1. Write the cost management plan. Agree units, precision, thresholds, earned value rules, reporting and approval limits with the sponsor and finance.
  2. Break down the scope. Build a WBS so every cost has a home, and map work packages to finance cost codes.
  3. Estimate effort and resources. Estimate hours first, then convert to cost using agreed rates; see effort estimation.
  4. Estimate other costs. Add materials, equipment, licences, travel, training and procurement costs, including supplier quotes for anything bought in.
  5. Analyse risks and size the contingency. Quantify identified risks and add contingency reserve, shown separately from the base estimate.
  6. Build the time-phased cost baseline. Spread costs across the schedule so you know how much should be spent by each date.
  7. Agree the budget and funding. Add management reserve, confirm funding tranches and get formal approval.
  8. Track actual costs and progress. Record actuals, including accruals for work done but not yet invoiced, at the same status date as progress.
  9. Analyse performance and forecast. Calculate CV, CPI, SV and SPI, update the estimate at completion and compare with thresholds.
  10. Act and close. Take corrective action, raise change requests where the baseline is no longer realistic, and at closure record final costs and lessons learned.

Worked example: one project through all four processes

A company is rebuilding its customer self-service portal over six months. Here is how the four processes work on this one project, with the numbers carried from step to step.

Process 1: the rules in the cost management plan

  • Costs in US dollars, rounded to the nearest $1,000 in reports. Internal and contractor labour costed at blended rates.
  • Progress measured monthly by work package using agreed percent-complete milestones.
  • Thresholds: a CPI or SPI below 0.95 needs a written explanation; below 0.90 is escalated to the sponsor.
  • The project manager may release contingency for risks listed in the risk register. Management reserve needs sponsor approval through change control.

Process 2: estimate the work packages

Work packageBasis of estimateEstimate
1. Discovery and design320 hours x $125$40,000
2. Build960 hours x $125$120,000
3. Integration with core systems400 hours x $125 + $10,000 middleware licence$60,000
4. Testing240 hours x $100 + $11,000 test tools and devices$35,000
5. Deployment and training120 hours x $125 + $10,000 venue and materials$25,000
Base estimate2,040 labour hours plus other costs$280,000

Size the contingency from the risk register

RiskProbabilityImpactExpected monetary value
R1. Core system supplier changes its API40%$30,000$12,000
R2. Key developer leaves mid-build20%$25,000$5,000
R3. Accessibility audit finds extra fixes30%$10,000$3,000
Contingency reserve$20,000

Expected monetary value (probability x impact) gives a pooled reserve. It will not cover the full impact of every risk if several occur at once, which is one reason a management reserve is also held.

Process 3: determine the budget

LayerAmount
Base estimate (work packages)$280,000
+ Contingency reserve$20,000
= Cost baseline$300,000
+ Management reserve (5%, company policy)$15,000
= Total project budget$315,000

The $280,000 of work package budgets is spread across the schedule to give planned value (PV) for each month. Contingency is held at project level and moved into a work package budget only when it is released.

Month123456
Planned value this month$30,000$45,000$55,000$60,000$55,000$35,000
Cumulative planned value$30,000$75,000$130,000$190,000$245,000$280,000

Finance agrees to release funding in two tranches: $150,000 at approval, covering the first three months plus some contingency, and $165,000 at a month-3 stage gate, which includes the management reserve. Plotted cumulatively, the planned value forms the project's S-curve.

Process 4: control costs at the end of month 3

Work packageBudget (BAC)% completeEarned value (EV)Actual cost (AC)Cost variance (CV)CPI
1. Discovery and design$40,000100%$40,000$42,000-$2,0000.95
2. Build$120,00050%$60,000$68,000-$8,0000.88
3. Integration$60,00020%$12,000$14,000-$2,0000.86
4. Testing$35,0000%$0$0$0-
5. Deployment and training$25,0000%$0$0$0-
Total$280,00040%$112,000$124,000-$12,0000.90
  • Cost variance: CV = EV - AC = 112,000 - 124,000 = -$12,000. The work done has cost $12,000 more than budgeted.
  • Cost performance index: CPI = EV / AC = 112,000 / 124,000 = 0.90. Each dollar is buying about 90 cents of planned work.
  • Schedule variance: SV = EV - PV = 112,000 - 130,000 = -$18,000, and SPI = 112,000 / 130,000 = 0.86. The project is also behind schedule.
  • Forecast if current efficiency continues: EAC = BAC / CPI = 280,000 / 0.903 = $310,000, a variance at completion of -$30,000.
  • Forecast if the overrun was a one-off: EAC = AC + (BAC - EV) = 124,000 + 168,000 = $292,000.
  • Efficiency needed to finish on $280,000: TCPI = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC) = 168,000 / 156,000 = 1.08, better than any work package has achieved so far.

A spend-only report would have said the project had spent $124,000 against a plan of $130,000 and was "$6,000 under budget". Earned value shows the opposite: less work has been done than planned, and the work that has been done cost more than budgeted. Our guides to cost variance, the cost performance index and the estimate at completion explain each measure.

The decisions that follow

  1. Release contingency for a risk that has occurred. The core system supplier has announced the API change (risk R1). The integration package is re-estimated at $15,000 extra. Under the plan's rules, the project manager moves $15,000 of contingency into work package 3, leaving $5,000 of contingency.
  2. Fix the build overrun at its cause. The build package is below the 0.90 escalation threshold for work packages. Analysis shows rework caused by unclear acceptance criteria, which is not a listed risk. The team agrees to add acceptance criteria to every story before development starts.
  3. Give the sponsor an honest range. Adding the $15,000 integration change to the two forecasts gives a likely final cost of $307,000 to $325,000, against a cost baseline of $300,000 and a total budget of $315,000.
  4. Agree a trigger for the next decision. If the cumulative CPI has not improved to at least 0.95 by month 4, the project manager will bring a change request to the steering group to either use the management reserve or defer a lower-value feature.

Notice that the four processes are connected: the rules from process 1 decided who could release contingency, the risk analysis in process 2 created the reserve, the time-phased baseline from process 3 made the schedule variance visible, and process 4 turned the data into decisions.

Estimate accuracy through the project life cycle

Estimates get more accurate as the project is better understood. That is normal and should be planned for, not treated as a failure. The PMBOK Guide 6th edition quotes these typical ranges:

EstimateTypical accuracy rangeWhen it is producedUsed for
Rough order of magnitude (ROM)-25% to +75%Initiation, before scope is detailedDeciding whether the idea is worth pursuing
Definitive estimate-5% to +10%Detailed planning, once the WBS is completeSetting the cost baseline

Many organisations also use an intermediate budget estimate between the two. In engineering and construction, AACE International's recommended practices define a five-level estimate classification, from Class 5 (concept screening) to Class 1 (check estimate), with the expected range narrowing as project definition improves.

Two practical rules follow. First, always quote an estimate with its range, so a sponsor does not treat an early figure as a promise. Second, re-estimate at each stage gate and update the business case if the cost moves materially. A feasibility study usually works with ROM figures; the baseline should not.

Report CPI and SPI with their trend: a steady decline matters more than one noisy month.

Who is responsible for project costs?

RoleTypical cost responsibilities
SponsorApproves the budget and business case, holds the management reserve, decides on major changes
Project managerWrites the cost management plan, builds the baseline, tracks and forecasts costs, manages contingency within agreed limits
Control account or work package managersEstimate and manage the budget for their part of the work, explain variances
Project teamProvide effort estimates, record time accurately, flag issues that will affect cost
Finance or project controlsSet cost codes, record actuals and accruals, check capitalisation and support forecasting
Steering group or change control boardApprove baseline changes and use of management reserve above the project manager's authority

Write the approval limits down in the cost management plan, including the value above which a change must go to the sponsor or board.

Project cost management in Agile

Agile teams still manage costs, but the emphasis shifts. A stable, dedicated team costs roughly the same every sprint, so cost is close to fixed and scope becomes the main variable.

Example: costing an Agile release

  • A team of seven at an average of $800 per person per day, over a two-week sprint of ten working days, costs 7 x 10 x 800 = $56,000 per sprint. Add $4,000 per sprint for licences and cloud services: $60,000 per sprint.
  • The release is planned at eight sprints for a backlog of 400 story points: a budget of 8 x 60,000 = $480,000.
  • After four sprints the team has completed 160 points, 40% of the backlog, against a plan of 50%. Actual cost is 4 x 60,000 = $240,000.
  • Release-level earned value is 40% x 480,000 = $192,000, so CPI = 192,000 / 240,000 = 0.80.
  • At the current velocity of 40 points per sprint, the full backlog needs ten sprints, or $600,000. Within the $480,000 budget, the team can expect to deliver about 320 points.

The decision is a product one: the product owner prioritises the backlog so that the most valuable 320 points are delivered within budget, or the sponsor agrees extra sprints. Either way, the conversation happens at sprint four rather than at the end. Track this alongside the measures in our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs.

Common project cost management mistakes

  • Treating an early estimate as a commitment. Quote ranges and re-estimate at each stage.
  • Hiding contingency in task estimates. Padding cannot be managed or released; show reserves openly.
  • Tracking spend only. Spend against plan says nothing about how much work the money bought. Use earned value.
  • Not linking the WBS to finance codes. Actual costs then land in the wrong place or in a single project bucket.
  • Leaving out accruals. Work done but not yet invoiced makes the project look cheaper than it is.
  • No agreed thresholds. Without them, either every small variance becomes a crisis or real problems are ignored.
  • Reporting variances without forecasts. Always say where the project is likely to finish, not just where it is.
  • Absorbing scope creep. Extra work accepted without change control raises cost but not the budget.
  • Changing the baseline informally. Every change to the cost baseline should go through change control so performance remains measurable.

Frequently asked questions

What is project cost management?

The processes used to plan, estimate, budget and control a project's costs so it is completed within its approved budget.

What are the four processes of project cost management?

In the PMBOK Guide 6th edition: plan cost management, estimate costs, determine budget and control costs.

How does PMBOK 8 describe cost management?

It places cost in the Finance performance domain, with four processes: Plan Financial Management, Estimate Costs, Develop Budget, and Monitor and Control Finances.

What is the difference between a cost baseline and a project budget?

The cost baseline is the time-phased work package estimates plus contingency reserve. The project budget adds management reserve on top.

What is the difference between contingency reserve and management reserve?

Contingency reserve covers identified risks, sits inside the cost baseline and is usually managed by the project manager. Management reserve covers unforeseen work, sits outside the baseline and is controlled by the sponsor.

What are direct and indirect project costs?

Direct costs can be traced to the project, such as contractor fees. Indirect costs are shared overheads allocated to it, such as office space.

Why is project cost management important?

It gives early warning of overruns, supports decisions on scope, makes sure funding is available when needed and protects the business case.

Who is responsible for project cost management?

The project manager day to day, with work package managers, finance and the sponsor, who approves the budget and holds the management reserve.

What tools are used in project cost management?

Estimating methods, reserve analysis, earned value management, forecasting, variance analysis and cost-benefit analysis, supported by spreadsheets or project management software.

How does earned value help with cost management?

It compares the budget for work done with its actual cost and with planned work, separating cost efficiency (CPI) from schedule progress (SPI) and giving a forecast of the final cost.

What causes project cost overruns?

Unclear or changing scope, optimistic estimates, missing cost categories, rework, delays, price increases and unfunded risks.

How is cost managed in Agile projects?

Team cost per sprint is treated as roughly fixed and scope varies. Cost is tracked at release level with spend per sprint, velocity and the share of the backlog delivered.

How does cost management relate to the triple constraint?

Cost is one of the three constraints with scope and time. Changing either of the others usually changes cost.

What is the difference between cost management and cost control?

Cost control is the fourth process within cost management, which also includes planning, estimating and budgeting.
