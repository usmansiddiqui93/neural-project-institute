Critical Chain vs Critical Path: Differences and Example

Critical chain vs critical path is a question of what limits your schedule: the critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks assuming unlimited resources, while the critical chain is the longest chain once resource limits are included, with safety time pooled into buffers that protect the finish date. This guide compares the two methods, schedules one project both ways step by step and shows how to track progress with buffers.

Key takeaways

  • The critical path considers task dependencies only; the critical chain considers dependencies and resource availability.
  • Critical path schedules hide safety inside each task estimate; critical chain cuts estimates to focused durations and pools the safety in buffers.
  • Critical chain uses three buffers: a project buffer at the end, feeding buffers where other chains join, and resource buffers as early warnings.
  • Critical path tracking watches dates and float; critical chain tracking compares buffer consumed with chain completed, often on a fever chart.
  • Critical chain suits resource-constrained, multi-project environments with heavy multitasking; the critical path remains the default for most projects.

What is the difference between critical chain and critical path?

The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent activities in a project, calculated as if every resource were available whenever needed. The critical chain is the longest sequence once both task dependencies and resource dependencies are considered. Critical chain also removes safety time from individual estimates and pools it into buffers that protect the project's finish date.

In short, the critical path answers 'what is the longest chain of logic?' and the critical chain answers 'what is the longest chain of logic and people, and how do we protect it?' On a project where nobody is double-booked, the two chains are often the same sequence of tasks. The differences then come from how the estimates are made, where the contingency sits and how progress is tracked.

The critical path method in brief

The critical path method (CPM) was developed in the late 1950s and is the basis of almost every scheduling tool. You list the activities, estimate their durations, set their dependencies and draw a network diagram. A forward and backward pass then gives each activity early and late dates, and the difference between them is float. Activities with zero float form the critical path.

What CPM assumes

  • Resources are available whenever an activity is ready to start, unless you level them separately.
  • Each activity has a single duration estimate, usually one the estimator is fairly confident of meeting.
  • Contingency is either built into those estimates or held as a separate reserve.
  • Progress is judged by comparing actual dates with the baseline and watching float.

When resources are limited, CPM schedulers apply resource levelling after the calculation, which can lengthen the schedule and create a different longest path. Critical chain builds that step into the method from the start.

The critical chain method in brief

Critical chain project management (CCPM) was introduced by Eliyahu Goldratt in his 1997 book *Critical Chain*. It applies his theory of constraints, which holds that any system's output is limited by a small number of constraints, to project scheduling. In a project, the constraint is the longest chain of dependent tasks and resources.

The method rests on four ideas:

  1. Resource dependencies count as much as task dependencies. If one developer must do two tasks, those tasks are effectively in sequence, even if the logic would let them run in parallel.
  2. Estimates should be focused, not padded. Each task is planned at a duration that is achievable with focused work but not guaranteed, often around half of a conventional estimate.
  3. Safety belongs to the project, not the task. The time removed from tasks is partly returned as shared buffers, placed where they protect the finish date.
  4. Work like a relay race. Start a task as soon as its inputs are ready, work on it without multitasking, and pass it on as soon as it is done.

Why padded estimates do not protect the critical path

Critical chain starts from an observation about behaviour. A CPM schedule may contain a lot of safety, yet projects still run late. Goldratt argued that the safety is wasted through a few predictable habits.

BehaviourWhat happensEffect on the schedule
Student syndromePeople start a task late because the estimate feels generousThe safety is used up before work begins, so any problem causes a delay
Parkinson's lawWork expands to fill the time availableTasks rarely finish early; early finishes are not reported
MultitaskingPeople switch between several tasks or projectsEvery task takes longer to complete, even if total effort is the same
Merge biasA task waits for several predecessors to finishDelays on any one path pass on; early finishes on the others do not help

The net result is that delays accumulate along the path while gains are lost. Critical chain's response is to stop protecting each task and protect the chain as a whole, where a delay on one task can be offset by an early finish on another.

Tip

This only works if people are not blamed for missing a focused estimate. If they are, they will quietly pad the estimates again and the method stops working.

The three critical chain buffers

BufferWhere it goesWhat it protectsIs it time in the schedule?
Project bufferAt the end of the critical chain, before the finish milestoneThe committed project finish dateYes
Feeding bufferWhere a non-critical chain joins the critical chainThe critical chain from delays on the feeding chainYes
Resource bufferBefore a critical chain task that needs a new resourceAvailability of that resource when the chain arrivesNo: it is an alert to get the resource ready

Buffers are not padding by another name. They are visible, owned by the project manager and consumed in a controlled way. Teams do not 'spend' the buffer on their own task; it is there to absorb the variation that will happen somewhere along the chain.

How to size a buffer

There is no single correct method. Two are widely used, and both start from the safety removed from each task (the conventional estimate minus the focused estimate).

Method 1: 50% of the removed safety (cut and paste)

Goldratt proposed a simple rule: make the buffer half of the safety removed from the chain it protects. If 29 days of safety were removed from the critical chain, the project buffer is 14.5 days, rounded to 15.

Method 2: root square error

This method, associated with Lawrence Leach, takes the square root of the sum of the squares of each task's removed safety. It assumes that not every task will hit its worst case at once, so it produces smaller buffers on long chains with many tasks.

A paper presented at a PMI research conference (Tenera, 2008) compared these with a simulation-based method and found the 50% rule produced the largest buffers. For high-risk chains you can also size buffers using Monte Carlo simulation of the chain. Whichever method you choose, use it consistently so buffer reports mean the same thing across projects.

How to build a critical chain schedule step by step

  1. Build the network. List activities from the work breakdown structure and set their dependencies, exactly as you would for CPM.
  2. Collect two estimates. Ask for a conventional, safe estimate and a focused estimate for each task. A common shortcut is to halve the conventional estimate, but asking for both makes the safety visible.
  3. Schedule tasks as late as possible. Place each task as late as its successors allow, so work is not started early and left half-finished.
  4. Resolve resource conflicts. Where one person or team is needed for two tasks at the same time, sequence them. The longest chain of task and resource dependencies is the critical chain.
  5. Add the project buffer. Size it from the safety removed on the critical chain and place it before the finish milestone. The end of the buffer is your committed date.
  6. Add feeding buffers. At each point where a non-critical chain joins the critical chain, add a buffer sized from that feeding chain's removed safety.
  7. Add resource buffers. Before any critical chain task that needs a new resource, set an alert so the resource is ready when the previous task finishes.
  8. Agree working rules. No multitasking on critical chain tasks, hand over work as soon as it is done, and report the remaining duration of each active task, not its percentage complete.

Worked example: one project scheduled both ways

A small team is building a customer portal. All figures are illustrative working days.

Step 1: the critical path

Using the conventional estimates and ignoring resources, the forward pass gives: A 0 to 10, B 10 to 22, C 22 to 38, D 22 to 34, E 38 to 46 and F 10 to 20. The critical path is A, B, C, E, finishing on day 46. D has 4 days of float and F has 26.

Step 2: the resource conflict

The schedule has Sam building the back end and the front end at the same time, from day 22 to day 34. That is not possible. Levelling Sam's work puts C from day 22 to 38 and D from day 38 to 50, so testing runs from day 50 to 58. The realistic CPM finish is day 58, and the longest path is now A, B, C, D, E. It includes a resource link (C to D), which the original critical path did not show.

Step 3: the critical chain

Critical chain uses the focused estimates and keeps Sam's tasks in sequence. The critical chain is A, B, C, D, E: 5 + 6 + 8 + 6 + 4 = 29 days of work. The safety removed from the chain is 58 − 29 = 29 days.

  • Project buffer: 50% of 29 = 15 days, placed after E. Committed finish: 29 + 15 = day 44.
  • Feeding buffer: F's removed safety is 5 days, so its feeding buffer is 3 days (2.5 rounded up). F is scheduled to finish by day 26 so the buffer ends on day 29, when the chain reaches launch. F therefore starts on day 21.
  • Resource buffer: an alert to the tester a few days before D is due to finish, so testing can start the moment D is handed over.

Comparing the results

ScheduleHow it is builtFinishComments

CPM, resource-levelledConventional estimates, Sam sequencedDay 58Realistic; safety hidden in every task

The critical chain commitment is shorter because it does not assume that every task will need its full safety. If every task really did run to its conventional estimate, the chain would overrun its buffer, and the buffer report would make that visible early.

Tracking progress: float vs buffer management

On a CPM project you track whether activities start and finish on their baseline dates and whether float is being eroded, often supported by earned value management and schedule variance. On a critical chain project you ask two questions at each update:

  • How much of the chain is complete? (planned chain duration − remaining chain duration) ÷ planned chain duration.
  • How much buffer has been consumed? (projected chain finish − planned chain finish) ÷ project buffer.

Team members report the remaining duration of each task they are working on, not percentage complete. The comparison is plotted on a fever chart, with chain completed on the horizontal axis and buffer consumed on the vertical axis, divided into green, amber and red zones.

Status updates for the portal project

This example uses a simple illustrative rule: green if buffer consumed (%) is no higher than chain completed (%), amber if it is up to 20 points higher, and red if it is more than 20 points higher. Many tools use sloped zone boundaries instead; agree yours before the project starts.

At day 30 the project manager saw amber, found that Sam was splitting time with support work, and moved that duty to a colleague. The chain recovered and the project finished on day 38, six days inside its committed date of day 44, with buffer to spare.

When to use critical chain or critical path

Your situationBetter fitWhy
Contract specifies dates for individual activitiesCritical pathYou need activity-level dates the client can see
Key specialists are shared across several projectsCritical chainResource dependencies drive the schedule
Estimates are routinely padded and projects still finish lateCritical chainPooling safety addresses the behaviour behind late finishes
Heavy multitasking across projectsCritical chainThe method's working rules reduce task switching
Small project with a dedicated teamCritical pathResource conflicts are rare and CPM is simpler
Regulated or audited schedules using standard toolsCritical pathReviewers expect a conventional CPM schedule
Organisation willing to change how performance is judgedCritical chainThe method depends on not penalising focused estimates

Many teams find a middle path works: keep a CPM schedule for logic and reporting, but sequence shared resources, remove obvious padding and show an explicit project buffer before the finish date.

Critical chain across multiple projects

Critical chain is often adopted at portfolio level, where its benefits are most visible. The usual approach is to identify the most heavily loaded resource across all projects, sometimes called the drum, and stagger project start dates so that resource is never asked to work on two projects at once.

  1. List active and planned projects and the resources each one needs.
  2. Identify the resource that limits throughput across the portfolio.
  3. Schedule that resource's work in sequence across projects, rather than in parallel.
  4. Release new projects only when the drum resource has capacity, instead of starting everything at once.
  5. Report each project's buffer status on a single portfolio fever chart so the PMO can see which projects need help first.

This links closely to capacity planning and to our guide on managing multiple projects. Starting fewer projects at once can mean each one finishes sooner.

Combining the two in practice

You do not have to choose one method completely. A pragmatic combination looks like this:

  1. Build the network and run a normal CPM calculation to understand the logic.
  2. Level shared resources and note where resource links change the longest path.
  3. Ask estimators to separate their likely duration from their safety margin.
  4. Move the margin on the longest path into a visible project buffer before the final milestone.
  5. Track remaining duration on the longest path and report buffer consumption alongside float and milestone dates.
  6. Keep the project baseline for formal reporting, but manage day to day from the buffer.

Common mistakes

  • Cutting estimates without changing behaviour. Halving estimates while still punishing people who miss them simply produces late tasks and unhappy teams.
  • Treating the buffer as spare time. If teams see the buffer as theirs to spend, it disappears early in the project.
  • Forgetting feeding buffers. A delay on a non-critical chain can still hit the critical chain where the two merge.
  • Reporting percentage complete. Critical chain needs remaining duration; percentage complete hides how long a task will really take.
  • Ignoring resource links in CPM. A critical path calculated with double-booked people is unrealistic; always level shared resources before committing to a date.
  • Starting every project at once. In a multi-project environment, critical chain only helps if new work is released in line with the constrained resource's capacity.
  • Never re-planning. Both the critical path and the critical chain can move as work progresses, so recalculate after significant changes.

Tools and templates

Nearly every scheduling tool calculates the critical path. Fewer support critical chain natively; some enterprise portfolio tools include buffer management and fever charts, while in others you can add buffers as zero-work activities and track them manually.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between critical chain and critical path?

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks, assuming resources are always available. The critical chain also includes resource dependencies, uses focused task estimates and protects the finish date with pooled buffers instead of safety inside each task.

Who created critical chain project management?

Eliyahu Goldratt, who introduced it in his 1997 book Critical Chain, applying his theory of constraints to projects.

What are the three types of buffer in critical chain?

The project buffer at the end of the critical chain, feeding buffers where non-critical chains join it, and resource buffers, which are alerts that make sure a resource is ready when the chain needs it.

How do you calculate the project buffer?

Two common methods are half of the safety removed from the critical chain tasks (Goldratt's 50% rule) and the square root of the sum of the squared safety removed from each task (root square error). For removed safety of 5, 6, 8, 6 and 4 days, they give 15 and 13 days respectively.

What is a feeding buffer?

A buffer placed where a non-critical chain joins the critical chain. It absorbs delays on the feeding chain so they do not push back the critical chain.

What is a fever chart in critical chain?

A chart that plots buffer consumed against chain completed. Green, amber and red zones show whether the project is on track, needs a recovery plan or needs action now.

What is student syndrome?

The habit of starting a task at the last possible moment because the estimate feels generous, which uses up the safety before work begins.

What is Parkinson's law in scheduling?

The idea that work expands to fill the time available, so tasks rarely finish early even when they could. Critical chain counters it by using focused estimates and pooled buffers.

Is critical chain better than critical path?

Neither is better in every case. Critical chain tends to help where shared resources, multitasking and padded estimates cause delays. The critical path is simpler, widely supported and suits projects with dedicated teams or contractual activity dates.

Is critical chain the same as resource levelling?

No. Resource levelling is one step within critical chain. Critical chain adds focused estimates, buffers, buffer management and working rules such as avoiding multitasking.

Can the critical chain change during a project?

Yes. If a feeding chain is badly delayed, or resources change, a different chain can become the longest. Re-check the chain after significant changes, just as you would recalculate the critical path.

Does critical chain work with Agile?

They can coexist. Agile teams use fixed-length iterations and a prioritised backlog, so critical chain is less common inside a team. It is more often used for release or portfolio scheduling, where shared specialists and dependencies between teams limit delivery.

Is critical chain on the PMP exam?

The critical chain method has appeared in PMI's scheduling material and in PMP preparation courses. Check the current PMP Examination Content Outline and your study materials for how it is covered; see our PMP certification guide.

How is critical chain different from PERT?

PERT uses three estimates per task to calculate an expected duration along the critical path. Critical chain uses focused estimates, adds resource dependencies and moves contingency into buffers.
