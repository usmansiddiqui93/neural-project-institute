Project Management Processes: The 5 Groups Explained

Project management processes are the repeatable sets of activities a project manager uses to start, plan, deliver, control and close a project. PMI groups them into five process groups (initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing) and, in its classic model, ten knowledge areas, giving 49 processes in all. You rarely need every one of them, so the real skill is choosing the right ones for each project.

Key takeaways

  • The five process groups are initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing.
  • Process groups describe the type of management activity; the ten knowledge areas describe the subject being managed.
  • The classic PMBOK model has 49 processes; PMBOK 8 (November 2025) has 40, grouped into five focus areas.
  • Process groups are not phases: they repeat within every phase and overlap.
  • Monitoring and controlling runs from start to finish, not just at the end.
  • Tailor the processes to the project's size, risk and context rather than applying all of them by default.

What are project management processes?

Project management processes are defined sets of activities that turn inputs, such as a business case or a progress report, into outputs, such as a charter, a schedule or an approved change. Each process has a clear purpose, uses particular tools and techniques, and produces something the next process needs. Together they form the management system around the project's work.

It helps to separate two kinds of process on any project. Project management processes organise and control the work: planning, tracking, reporting and change control. Product-oriented processes create the deliverable itself: designing a building, writing code or running a marketing campaign. This guide covers the first kind, which are broadly the same whatever the industry.

The most widely used model comes from PMI (the Project Management Institute). Its PMBOK Guide arranged processes in two directions: by process group, which says when and why a process is used, and by knowledge area, which says what subject it deals with. Every process sits in exactly one of each. Even though PMI's newer standards are organised differently, this two-way structure is still the clearest way to see what a complete approach to a project needs.

Processes are different from project management principles, which guide behaviour and judgement, and from methodologies, which package processes, roles and rules into a complete way of working such as PRINCE2.

The five project management process groups

A process group is a logical grouping of processes by the type of management activity. The five groups apply to any project, of any size, using any method.

Process groupPurposeKey outputsQuestion it answers
InitiatingDefine a new project or phase and obtain authorisation to startProject charter, stakeholder registerShould we do this, and who is involved?
PlanningEstablish the total scope, refine objectives and define the course of actionProject management plan, scope baseline, schedule, budget, risk registerHow will we deliver it?
ExecutingComplete the work defined in the plan to meet the requirementsDeliverables, work performance data, change requests, issue logAre we doing the work?
Monitoring and controllingTrack, review and regulate progress and performance; manage changesWork performance reports, approved changes, forecastsAre we on track, and what must change?
ClosingFormally complete the project, phase or contractFinal report, accepted deliverables, lessons learned, archived recordsIs it finished and handed over?

Initiating

Initiating processes give the project a formal start. The sponsor confirms the business need, the project charter is written and approved, and the project manager is named and given authority. Stakeholders are identified early so their interests shape the plan. In a multi-phase project, a short initiating step at the start of each phase checks that the project is still worth continuing.

Planning

Planning processes work out what will be delivered and how. They produce the project management plan and its subsidiary plans, the scope statement and work breakdown structure, the schedule, the budget, the quality and resource plans, and the risk register. Planning is iterative: as you learn more, you return and refine the plan, which is often called progressive elaboration.

Executing

Executing processes do the work and manage the people doing it. The project manager directs the team, acquires and develops resources, runs quality assurance, communicates with stakeholders, carries out procurements and implements agreed risk responses. Most of the project's time and money is spent here.

Monitoring and controlling

Monitoring and controlling processes compare actual performance with the plan, analyse variances, forecast the outcome and decide on corrective action. They include scope validation, schedule and cost control, quality control and change control. Unlike the other groups, monitoring and controlling runs throughout the project, from the moment the charter is signed.

Closing

Closing processes finish the project or phase properly: deliverables are accepted, contracts are closed, the team is released, records are archived and lessons learned are captured. Projects that are cancelled early still need closing, so the organisation keeps the knowledge and closes the accounts.

How the process groups interact

The process groups are often drawn as five boxes in a row, which suggests that you finish initiating, then plan, then execute. In practice they overlap heavily. Planning continues while execution starts; monitoring and controlling runs from day one; and an approved change can send you back to planning in the middle of delivery.

PMI has long linked the process groups to the plan-do-check-act cycle: planning is "plan", executing is "do", and monitoring and controlling covers "check" and "act". Initiating starts the cycle and closing ends it. The cycle repeats many times on a real project, often every reporting period.

Process groups vs project phases

The most common confusion is between process groups and phases. A phase is a time-bound part of the project life cycle, such as feasibility, design, build and handover. A process group is a type of management activity. All five process groups are used within each phase.

Process groupsProject phases
What they describeTypes of management activityStages of the project's life over time
NumberAlways fiveVaries by project and industry
SequenceOverlap and repeatUsually sequential, ending in a gate or review
ExampleMonitoring and controllingDetailed design
Defined byPMI's standardsYour organisation, industry or method

For example, a construction project's design phase has its own short initiation (confirm the brief and authorise design), planning (design programme and budget), execution (produce drawings), monitoring and controlling (design reviews and cost checks) and closing (sign-off at the design gate).

The ten knowledge areas

A knowledge area is a field of project management defined by its required knowledge and described by its processes. The PMBOK Guide used ten from its fifth edition (2013) onwards.

Knowledge areaWhat it coversRead more
IntegrationCoordinating all parts of the project: charter, plan, direction, change control and closureIntegration management
ScopeDefining and controlling what is and is not includedProject scope
ScheduleActivities, sequence, durations and the schedule modelProject scheduling
CostEstimating, budgeting and controlling costsProject cost management
QualityPlanning, assuring and controlling qualityQuality management
ResourcesIdentifying, acquiring and managing people, equipment and materialsProject resources
CommunicationsPlanning and managing project informationCommunication management
RiskIdentifying, analysing and responding to uncertaintyRisk management
ProcurementBuying goods and services from outside the project teamProcurement management
StakeholdersIdentifying and engaging the people affected by the projectStakeholder management

Integration is the odd one out: it is less a subject than the job of holding the others together. When you change the scope, integration is what makes sure the schedule, budget, risks and communications change with it.

How many processes are in each group?

Process groupNumber of processesShare of the 49
Initiating24%
Planning2449%

Monitoring and controlling1224%

Planning holds almost half of the processes. That does not mean planning should take half the project's time: on most projects execution takes far longer. It reflects the fact that each knowledge area needs its own planning thinking, even if much of it can be done in a single workshop.

Three patterns make the matrix easier to remember. Every knowledge area starts with a Plan ... Management process. Most have one control or monitor process. And only integration has a closing process, because closing the project or phase happens once, for everything.

Tip

If you are studying for the PMP or CAPM, learn the logic of the matrix rather than memorising every input and output. Current PMI exams are based on exam content outlines that draw on several sources, not on a single edition of the PMBOK Guide.

Inputs, tools and techniques, and outputs (ITTOs)

In the classic model, each process is described by its ITTOs: the inputs it needs, the tools and techniques used to carry it out, and the outputs it produces. Outputs from one process become inputs to others, which is what links the 49 processes into a system.

ElementExample: Develop Project Charter
InputsBusiness documents (business case, benefits management plan), agreements, organisational policies and templates
Tools and techniquesExpert judgement, data gathering such as interviews and brainstorming, meetings, conflict management and facilitation
OutputsProject charter, assumption log

The charter then becomes an input to Develop Project Management Plan, Identify Stakeholders and several planning processes. If the charter is weak, every process downstream inherits the problem, which is why experienced project managers spend time getting it right.

Treat ITTOs as a checklist of what good practice looks like, not as paperwork you must produce. A small internal project might combine the charter and the plan into a single page; the thinking still happens even if the documents are lighter.

How PMI's processes have changed: PMBOK 6, 7 and 8

If you have seen different numbers of processes in different places, it is because PMI's standard has changed shape twice since 2017.

EditionPublishedHow processes are treated
PMBOK Guide 6th edition201749 processes in 5 process groups and 10 knowledge areas, each described with ITTOs
PMBOK Guide 7th edition2021Replaced processes with 12 principles and 8 performance domains; the 49 processes moved to *Process Groups: A Practice Guide* (November 2022) for predictive projects
PMBOK Guide 8th editionNovember 2025Six principles, seven performance domains and 40 processes, presented as non-prescriptive guidance within five focus areas

According to PMI and published summaries, the eighth edition's seven performance domains are governance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources and risk, and its five focus areas are initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing, the same names as the old process groups. In effect, the domains have taken over the role of the knowledge areas and the focus areas the role of the process groups.

Published summaries list processes such as these for some of the domains (names may differ slightly from the guide itself):

Performance domainExample processes in PMBOK 8
ScopePlan Scope Management, Elicit and Analyze Requirements, Define Scope, Develop Scope Structure, Validate Scope, Monitor and Control Scope
FinancePlan Financial Management, Estimate Costs, Develop Budget, Monitor and Control Finances
RiskPlan Risk Management, Identify Risks, Perform Risk Analysis, Plan Risk Responses, Implement Risk Responses, Monitor Risks
ResourcesPlan Resource Management, Estimate Resources, Acquire Resources, Lead the Team, Monitor and Control Resources

The practical message is the same in every edition: these are processes you select and tailor, not a sequence you must follow in full. The classic 49-process matrix above remains the best-known reference, which is why we use it in this guide. For more on the editions, see our PMBOK Guide overview.

Worked example: one project through the five process groups

Here is how the processes play out on a typical mid-sized project: a company moving 120 staff to a new office within six months, with a budget of £450,000. The project has three phases (planning and fit-out design, fit-out works, move and settle in), and each phase uses all five process groups. The table summarises the project as a whole.

Process groupWhat the project manager doesMain outputs
InitiatingConfirms the business case with the operations director (sponsor), agrees objectives (move by 30 June, no more than one day of lost working time) and identifies stakeholders: staff, IT, facilities, landlord, fit-out contractorSigned charter, stakeholder register
PlanningBreaks the work into a WBS (fit-out, IT and telecoms, furniture, move logistics, communications), builds the schedule and critical path, estimates costs, plans procurement for the fit-out and removals, and runs a risk workshopProject plan, schedule, £450,000 budget with £30,000 contingency, risk register, procurement plan, communication plan
ExecutingTenders and appoints the contractor and removals firm, holds weekly site meetings, runs staff briefings, orders IT equipment, implements risk responses such as booking a backup removals dateContracts, fit-out progress, issue log, change requests
Monitoring and controllingTracks progress against the schedule and spend against the budget each fortnight, reviews risks, assesses a change request for extra meeting rooms (£18,000, approved from contingency), and forecasts the final cost and dateStatus reports, approved and rejected changes, updated forecasts
ClosingConfirms that the fit-out snags are cleared, closes the contracts, releases the team, runs a lessons-learned session and hands the building over to facilitiesAcceptance certificate, final report, lessons learned

Notice how the groups overlap. The removals firm is procured (executing) while the detailed move plan is still being written (planning). The extra meeting rooms send the project back through planning (update the scope, schedule and budget) after change control approves them. And monitoring starts in week one, when the first schedule milestone is checked.

The same project in knowledge-area terms: integration is the charter, plan and change control; scope is the WBS; schedule and cost are the programme and budget; quality is the snagging standard; resources are the internal move team; communications is the staff briefing plan; risk is the register; procurement is the two contracts; and stakeholders covers staff, the landlord and the IT department.

How to tailor project management processes

PMI's guidance has always said that not every process is needed on every project, and that the project manager and team decide which processes are appropriate and how rigorously to apply them. That decision is called tailoring. The table shows a typical starting point at three scales; adjust it for your context.

Process areaSmall project (weeks, low risk)Medium project (months)Large or regulated project
InitiatingOne-page brief agreed by emailSigned charterBusiness case, charter and formal gate approval
ScopeBullet list of deliverables and exclusionsScope statement and WBSRequirements specification, WBS and WBS dictionary
ScheduleTask list with datesGantt chart with dependenciesIntegrated schedule with critical path and baseline
CostBudget lineEstimate and budget by work packageCost baseline with earned value
RiskTop five risks in the status updateRisk register reviewed fortnightlyRisk management plan, quantitative analysis, contingency reserves
Change controlSponsor agrees changes by emailChange log and request formChange control board
CommunicationsWeekly updateCommunication plan and status reportsCommunication plan, dashboards and governance reporting
ClosingShort wrap-up noteClosure report and lessons learnedFormal acceptance, audit and benefits handover

Step by step: build the process set for your project

  1. Assess the project. Note its size, duration, budget, risk, complexity, regulatory requirements, number of suppliers and how stable the requirements are.
  2. Check what is mandatory. Your organisation's governance, PMO standards, contracts or regulators may require certain processes and documents. These are not optional.
  3. Walk through the five process groups. For each, decide which processes you need and how formally. Use the matrix above as a checklist so nothing is forgotten by accident.
  4. Decide the documents. For each chosen process, decide whether it needs a separate document, a section in the plan, or just a conversation and a note in the log.
  5. Set the control rhythm. Agree how often you will report, review risks and forecast, and what thresholds trigger escalation.
  6. Record the decisions. Write the tailoring choices into the project management plan so the team and sponsor know what to expect.
  7. Review at each phase gate. Add rigour if risks grow; remove processes that are not adding value.

The aim is enough process to keep control and keep stakeholders informed, and no more. Too little and problems surface late; too much and the team spends its time feeding the method instead of delivering.

Processes in Agile and PRINCE2

The five process groups are method-neutral, so they map onto other ways of working.

PRINCE2 processes

PRINCE2 has its own seven processes, which are the same in PRINCE2 7 (2023). They are built around management stages and the project board rather than knowledge areas.

PRINCE2 processClosest PMI process groups
Starting up a projectInitiating (pre-project checks and project brief)
Directing a projectRuns throughout: the project board's decisions and authorisations
Initiating a projectInitiating and planning (project initiation documentation)
Controlling a stageExecuting and monitoring and controlling
Managing product deliveryExecuting (team managers deliver work packages)
Managing a stage boundaryPlanning and monitoring and controlling (stage plan, end-stage report)
Closing a projectClosing

Agile projects

Agile teams use the same management activities in a shorter loop. Product vision and initial backlog creation play the role of initiating; release and sprint planning are planning; the sprint itself is executing; daily scrums, reviews and burn-down charts are monitoring and controlling; and the retrospective and release are a form of closing, repeated every iteration. The difference is that planning happens just in time and change is expected rather than controlled through a formal board.

Common mistakes with project management processes

  • Treating process groups as phases. Planning does not stop when execution starts, and monitoring is not a final step.
  • Applying all 49 processes to a small project. The result is documents nobody reads. Tailor first.
  • Skipping initiating. Without a charter and named sponsor, the project has no clear authority, and scope disputes start early.
  • Planning once and never again. The plan should be updated as you learn; an out-of-date plan is worse than none because people trust it.
  • Weak change control. Accepting changes informally is the main route to scope creep.
  • Monitoring without controlling. Reporting a variance is not enough; someone has to decide what to do about it.
  • Closing badly. Unclosed contracts, unrecorded lessons and a team that drifts away cost the organisation more than a short closure meeting.
  • Confusing documents with management. A perfect risk register does not reduce risk; acting on it does.

Frequently asked questions

What are the five project management process groups?

Initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. They describe the types of management activity used in every project and every phase.

What are the 49 project management processes?

The 49 processes defined in the PMBOK Guide sixth edition and PMI's Process Groups: A Practice Guide, arranged in five process groups and ten knowledge areas. The full matrix is shown above.

What are the ten knowledge areas of project management?

Integration, scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement and stakeholders.

What is the difference between a process group and a knowledge area?

A process group describes the type of activity, such as planning or controlling; a knowledge area describes the subject, such as cost or risk. Every process belongs to one of each.

Which process group has the most processes?

Planning, with 24 of the 49 processes in the classic model, followed by monitoring and controlling with 12.

Are the project management process groups sequential?

No. They overlap and repeat. Planning continues during execution, and monitoring and controlling runs throughout the project.

Are process groups the same as project phases?

No. Phases divide the project life cycle over time, such as design and build. All five process groups are used within each phase.

Does the PMBOK Guide still use process groups?

The seventh edition dropped them in favour of principles and performance domains and moved the 49 processes to a separate practice guide. The eighth edition, published in November 2025, brings back 40 processes within five focus areas with the same names as the process groups.

How many processes are in the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

According to PMI and published summaries, 40 processes, presented as non-prescriptive guidance alongside six principles and seven performance domains.

What are ITTOs in project management?

Inputs, tools and techniques, and outputs: the way the classic PMBOK model describes each process. They show what a process needs, how it is done and what it produces.

What does tailoring project management processes mean?

Choosing which processes to use and how formally to apply them, based on the project's size, risk, complexity and any mandatory requirements.

What is the most important project management process group?

None works alone, but many practitioners see planning and monitoring and controlling as the groups that most often decide success, because they set the baseline and catch problems early.

Do Agile projects use the project management process groups?

Yes, in a shorter, repeating loop: backlog creation, sprint planning, the sprint, reviews and retrospectives cover the same activities each iteration.

What are the seven PRINCE2 processes?

Starting up a project, directing a project, initiating a project, controlling a stage, managing product delivery, managing a stage boundary and closing a project.
