Six Sigma in Project Management: DMAIC, Belts and Tools

Six Sigma is a data-driven method for improving processes by finding and removing the causes of defects and reducing variation, delivered through structured projects that usually follow five phases: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve and Control (DMAIC). For project managers it offers a disciplined way to run improvement projects and a toolkit for quality problems on any project. This guide explains what "six sigma" means, walks through DMAIC with a full worked example, shows how to calculate sigma level and covers belts, tools and limitations.

Key takeaways

  • Six Sigma reduces defects and variation using data, not opinion. A process at six sigma quality produces about 3.4 defects per million opportunities.
  • DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control) improves existing processes; DMADV designs new ones.
  • Every Six Sigma project is still a project: it needs a charter, a sponsor, a scope, a schedule and stakeholder management.
  • Sigma level comes from DPMO: defects divided by (units x opportunities), multiplied by one million.
  • Belts (White, Yellow, Green, Black, Master Black) describe training and role; there is no single official certification body.
  • Six Sigma is strongest on repeatable processes with measurable output and weakest on one-off, highly innovative work.

What is Six Sigma?

Six Sigma is a quality improvement methodology that uses statistical analysis to reduce variation and defects in a process, so that its output consistently meets customer requirements. It works through structured improvement projects, usually following the DMAIC cycle, led by trained practitioners known as Green Belts and Black Belts, with results measured in quality and money.

Six Sigma was introduced at Motorola in 1986 by engineer Bill Smith and became widely known after General Electric, under Jack Welch, made it central to its business strategy from 1995. It builds on earlier quality thinking from Walter Shewhart, W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran, and the international standard ISO 13053, first published in 2011, describes the DMAIC methodology and its tools.

Is Six Sigma a project management methodology? Not exactly. It is a quality improvement method that is delivered through projects. A Six Sigma project still needs everything a normal project does, and Six Sigma tools are useful on ordinary projects whenever quality is the problem. It sits alongside the wider project quality management processes rather than replacing them.

What does "six sigma" mean?

Sigma (σ) is the statistical symbol for standard deviation, a measure of how much a process varies. The more standard deviations you can fit between the average of a process and the nearest limit the customer will accept, the fewer outputs fall outside that limit. "Six sigma" describes a process so consistent that, by Six Sigma convention, it produces no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO).

Sigma levelDefects per million opportunities (DPMO)Yield (defect-free)
2308,53869.1%
366,80793.3%

523399.977%
63.499.99966%

The gap between levels is not linear. Moving from three to four sigma cuts defects by about 90%; moving from five to six sigma removes most of the rest. In practice few processes need six sigma. The value lies in using data to understand variation and choosing a target that makes commercial sense.

The 1.5 sigma shift

The figures above include a convention called the 1.5 sigma shift: they assume a process mean will drift by up to 1.5 standard deviations over time, so a "six sigma" process is treated as performing at 4.5 sigma in the long term. Without the shift, six sigma would mean around two defects per billion. The convention is associated with Mikel Harry and is debated by statisticians, including Donald Wheeler, who regard it as arbitrary. Treat sigma levels as a common reporting language, not a precise law of nature.

The core principles of Six Sigma

  • Focus on the customer. Quality is defined by what the customer needs, expressed as critical-to-quality (CTQ) requirements.
  • Use data, not assumptions. Decisions are based on measurement and analysis, and claims about causes are tested.
  • Understand the process. Outputs (Y) are a function of inputs and process steps (X); fix the Xs and the Y follows.
  • Reduce variation. A consistent process is easier to improve and to predict than one that is right on average but erratic.
  • Remove root causes, not symptoms. Fixes target the few causes that create most of the defects.
  • Sustain the gain. Every project ends with controls so the process does not drift back.
  • Show the money. Projects are selected and judged on measurable financial and customer benefits.

How Six Sigma fits into project management

A Six Sigma improvement effort is a project in the full sense: temporary, with a defined goal, a team, a budget and a finish. Frank T. Anbari's paper for PMI (2002) describes typical Six Sigma projects as lasting three to six months, with larger problems split into several smaller projects.

AspectTypical projectSix Sigma project
GoalDeliver an agreed outputImprove a measurable process metric by a target amount

Normal project management disciplines still apply. You will need stakeholder management to win support from process owners, a RACI matrix to clarify who decides, and scope discipline to stop a defect-reduction project becoming a redesign of the whole department. Most organisations hold a tollgate review at the end of each DMAIC phase, where the champion confirms the phase deliverables before the team moves on.

Using Six Sigma tools on everyday projects

You do not need a formal Six Sigma programme to benefit from its tools. On ordinary projects they help whenever the same problem keeps recurring:

  • Recurring defects in deliverables, such as failed test cases or rejected documents: count them by type, build a Pareto chart and fix the top one or two causes first.
  • Repeated schedule slippage on similar tasks: measure actual against estimated durations, look at the spread as well as the average, and ask why the variation is so wide.
  • Stakeholder complaints: turn vague dissatisfaction into specific CTQ requirements and agree how each will be measured.
  • Handover problems between teams: map the process with a SIPOC to agree inputs, outputs and owners.
  • Post-project reviews: use the 5 whys to get beneath symptoms when capturing lessons learned.

DMAIC: the five phases step by step

DMAIC is the Six Sigma method for improving an existing process. Each phase answers one question and ends with defined deliverables.

PhaseKey questionTypical toolsTollgate deliverables
DefineWhat is the problem and why does it matter?Charter, SIPOC, voice of the customer, CTQ treeSigned charter, scope, CTQs, team
MeasureHow bad is it today?Data collection plan, measurement system analysis, process mapValidated baseline and sigma level
AnalyseWhat causes it?Pareto chart, fishbone diagram, 5 whys, hypothesis tests, regressionVerified root causes
ImproveWhat fixes the causes?Solution selection, pilots, design of experiments, FMEAPiloted solution with measured results
ControlHow do we keep the gain?Control charts, control plan, standard workControl plan, handover to process owner, benefits sign-off

1. Define

  1. Write a problem statement: what is wrong, where, when, how big and how you know. Our problem statement template gives the structure.
  2. Capture the voice of the customer and translate it into measurable CTQ requirements.
  3. Draw a SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers) to agree the start and end of the process in scope.
  4. Set a measurable goal, such as "reduce the defect rate from 4.8% to below 1% within six months".
  5. Name the champion, process owner and team, and get the charter signed.

2. Measure

  1. Define each defect and each opportunity precisely, so two people counting the same work get the same answer.
  2. Check the measurement system: if the data is unreliable, fix that first.
  3. Collect baseline data over a representative period.
  4. Map the process as it really runs, including rework loops; a swimlane diagram shows handoffs clearly.
  5. Calculate the baseline DPMO, yield and sigma level.

3. Analyse

  1. Stratify the defects by type, location, shift, supplier or product, and build a Pareto chart.
  2. Brainstorm possible causes with a fishbone diagram and drill down with the 5 whys.
  3. Test the likely causes with data, using hypothesis tests or regression where the volume allows.
  4. Agree the verified root causes; record the root cause analysis and evidence.

4. Improve

  1. Generate solutions that address each verified root cause.
  2. Select solutions using effort, impact, cost and risk; use FMEA to anticipate how the new process could fail.
  3. Pilot on a small scale and measure the result against the baseline.
  4. Refine, then plan the full roll-out with an action plan.

5. Control

  1. Write a control plan: what is measured, how often, by whom and what happens if it goes out of limits.
  2. Introduce control charts, standard work and training so the new method becomes routine.
  3. Hand the process formally to its owner and agree how benefits will be tracked.
  4. Close the project and capture lessons learned.
Tip

Some organisations add a Recognise step before Define to choose the right problem in the first place, and some add Standardise and Integrate after Control. The core five phases stay the same.

Worked example: reducing invoice errors with DMAIC

This example is illustrative: the organisation and figures are invented to show the method and the calculations.

Define

A shared-services finance team processes 2,000 supplier invoices a month. Suppliers complain about late and incorrect payments, and staff spend hours on rework. The finance director acts as champion and a Green Belt leads a five-person team. The charter sets the goal: cut invoice defects by at least 75% within five months. The team identifies five opportunities for a defect on each invoice: missing or wrong purchase order (PO) number, wrong cost code, wrong amount or duplicate, wrong supplier details, and late approval.

Measure

Over one month the team finds 480 defects across 2,000 invoices, with 410 invoices having at least one defect.

MeasureCalculationBaseline
Opportunities2,000 invoices x 510,000
DPMO480 / 10,000 x 1,000,00048,000
Sigma levelFrom DPMO, with 1.5 shiftAbout 3.2
First-pass yield(2,000 - 410) / 2,00079.5%
Cost of rework480 defects x 25 minutes x £30 per hour£6,000 a month

Analyse

A Pareto analysis of the 480 defects shows that two defect types cause two-thirds of the problem:

Defect typeCountShareCumulative
Missing or wrong PO number20542.7%42.7%
Wrong cost code11824.6%67.3%
Wrong amount or duplicate7215.0%82.3%
Wrong supplier details4910.2%92.5%
Late approval367.5%100%

A fishbone session and the 5 whys trace the PO problem to suppliers who are never told the PO number, because buyers raise some orders by email instead of in the purchasing system. The cost-code errors trace to a free-text field. Data confirms both: 88% of PO defects come from email orders, and cost-code errors fall sharply on invoices from the one department that already uses a drop-down list.

Improve

The team pilots three changes for six weeks: a rule that all orders are raised in the purchasing system, an automatic email sending the PO number to the supplier, and a drop-down list of valid cost codes. A duplicate-check rule is added to the finance system at little extra cost.

Control

The accounts payable manager takes ownership. A weekly p-chart tracks the proportion of defective invoices, with a rule that two weeks above the upper control limit triggers a review. Procedures and training are updated, and the champion signs off the benefits with the finance controller. The project closes after five months, on target.

The example shows why Six Sigma pairs well with project management: the team needed a clear charter, sponsor support to change purchasing rules across departments, a pilot plan and a formal handover. Without those, the analysis alone would not have changed the process.

How to calculate DPMO and sigma level

  1. Define a unit: the thing you produce or process, such as an invoice, a part or a patient admission.
  2. Define the opportunities: the distinct ways one unit can fail to meet a CTQ requirement. Count only meaningful, measurable ones; inflating opportunities flatters the result.
  3. Count the defects found in a sample of units.
  4. Calculate DPO: defects / (units x opportunities per unit).
  5. Calculate DPMO: DPO x 1,000,000.
  6. Convert to sigma level: use a sigma table, or in Excel or Google Sheets use =NORM.S.INV(1 - DPMO/1000000) + 1.5.
MetricFormulaUse
DPU (defects per unit)Defects / unitsSimple internal tracking
DPMODefects / (units x opportunities) x 1,000,000Comparing processes of different complexity
First-pass yieldUnits with no defects / total unitsShare of work right first time
Sigma levelNORM.S.INV(1 - DPMO/1,000,000) + 1.5Common reporting language

Process capability: Cp and Cpk

When output is a measurement rather than a pass or fail, such as a delivery time or a dimension, Six Sigma uses process capability indices to compare the spread of the process with the specification limits.

IndexFormulaWhat it tells you
Cp(USL - LSL) / 6σWhether the process spread fits within the limits, if it were perfectly centred
CpkLower of (USL - mean) / 3σ and (mean - LSL) / 3σHow capable the process actually is, allowing for an off-centre mean

Example: a warehouse promises delivery within 48 hours (an upper limit only). Delivery times average 40 hours with a standard deviation of 3 hours. Cpk = (48 - 40) / (3 x 3) = 0.89. A Cpk below 1 means a noticeable share of deliveries will breach the promise, so the team should either reduce variation or move the average down. Many organisations treat a Cpk of 1.33 as a minimum target; a six sigma process corresponds to a short-term Cpk of 2.

DMADV and Design for Six Sigma

When there is no process to improve, or the existing one is beyond repair, Six Sigma uses DMADV: Define, Measure, Analyse, Design, Verify. It is part of Design for Six Sigma (DFSS), which aims to build quality into a new product, service or process from the start.

DMAICDMADV
PurposeImprove an existing processDesign a new process, product or service

Six Sigma roles and belts

Six Sigma borrows martial-arts belt names to show training level and role. ASQ describes the roles as follows:

RoleTypical responsibilities
Executive leadershipSets the strategic focus of the Six Sigma programme
ChampionTurns goals into a deployment plan, selects projects, secures resources and removes roadblocks
Master Black BeltTrains and coaches Black and Green Belts; acts as an internal consultant
Black BeltLeads complex problem-solving projects, often full time, and coaches teams
Green BeltLeads smaller projects alongside a normal job; supports data collection and analysis for Black Belt projects
Yellow BeltProject team member who understands the basics and reviews improvements
White BeltAwareness of basic concepts; supports local problem-solving teams

Process owners, who run the process day to day, are not belts but are vital: they own the result after the project ends.

Six Sigma certification

No single body owns Six Sigma, so certificates vary in rigour. Well-known options include ASQ (Certified Six Sigma Yellow, Green and Black Belt, and Master Black Belt) and IASSC, alongside many universities and training providers. Some certifications are exam-only; others, such as ASQ's Black Belt, also require evidence of completed projects. Check the current requirements with the certifying body before you enrol.

Most people start with a Yellow Belt for awareness or go straight to a Green Belt if they will lead projects. If your main goal is project leadership rather than process improvement, compare options in our guide to project management certifications.

Lean, Lean Six Sigma and Agile

Lean and Six Sigma attack different problems. Lean removes waste so that work flows faster; Six Sigma reduces variation so that results are consistent. Lean Six Sigma combines them, usually inside the DMAIC structure, using Lean tools such as value stream mapping alongside Six Sigma statistics. Our Lean vs Six Sigma guide compares them side by side, and Lean project management covers Lean in more depth.

Six Sigma and Agile project management can also work together. Agile teams can use Six Sigma's root-cause tools to tackle recurring defects, and a Six Sigma team can run DMAIC phases in short, time-boxed iterations with frequent stakeholder reviews.

Critics have also pointed out that much of Six Sigma repackages earlier quality methods (Joseph Juran made this point) and that the 1.5 sigma shift is a convention rather than a measured fact. Neither criticism undermines the core idea: define the problem precisely, measure it, find the real causes and keep the fix in place.

How to choose a good Six Sigma project

  • A repeatable process with enough volume to produce data, not a one-off event.
  • A clear, measurable problem, such as an error rate, cycle time or complaint rate, with a known baseline or one you can collect quickly.
  • An unknown cause. If the solution is already obvious, just implement it.
  • Meaningful benefit to customers or costs, ideally confirmed by a short cost-benefit analysis or a business case.
  • A scope one team can finish in roughly three to six months.
  • A committed champion and process owner.

Industries with high-volume, repeatable processes tend to get the most from Six Sigma; see our guide to manufacturing project management.

Common Six Sigma mistakes

  • Starting with a solution. "Implement a new system" is not a Six Sigma problem statement; "reduce invoice defects by 75%" is.
  • Scope too big. "Improve customer satisfaction" will not finish; pick one process and one metric.
  • Trusting bad data. Skipping measurement system checks leads to precise analysis of the wrong numbers.
  • Inflating opportunities to make the sigma level look better.
  • Jumping from Measure to Improve without verifying root causes.
  • No control plan. The process quietly drifts back within months.
  • Ignoring change management. People must adopt the new way of working; plan communication and training, and use change control for any agreed changes to project scope.

Frequently asked questions

What is Six Sigma in simple terms?

A way of improving a process by measuring how often it goes wrong, finding the real causes with data and fixing them so results are consistent. It is usually run as a project following DMAIC.

What are the five steps of Six Sigma?

Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve and Control (DMAIC). They take a project from a clear problem statement to a verified, controlled improvement.

Why is it called Six Sigma?

Sigma is the symbol for standard deviation. A process with six standard deviations between its average and the nearest specification limit produces about 3.4 defects per million opportunities, using the standard 1.5 sigma shift.

What does 3.4 defects per million opportunities mean?

It is the defect rate of a process at six sigma: out of every million chances to make a defect, only 3.4 result in one, a defect-free rate of 99.99966%.

Is Six Sigma a project management methodology?

It is a quality improvement methodology delivered through projects. Each Six Sigma project still needs a charter, sponsor, plan and stakeholder management.

What is the difference between DMAIC and DMADV?

DMAIC improves an existing process. DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyse, Design, Verify) designs a new process, product or service and is part of Design for Six Sigma.

What are the Six Sigma belts?

White Belt (awareness), Yellow Belt (team member), Green Belt (leads smaller projects part time), Black Belt (leads complex projects and coaches) and Master Black Belt (trains, coaches and guides the programme).

Which Six Sigma belt should I start with?

Yellow Belt for awareness, or Green Belt if you expect to lead improvement projects. Check each certifying body's requirements, as they vary.

How do you calculate sigma level?

Calculate DPMO as defects / (units x opportunities) x 1,000,000, then convert it with a sigma table or the spreadsheet formula =NORM.S.INV(1 - DPMO/1000000) + 1.5.

How long does a Six Sigma project take?

Typically three to six months for a DMAIC project. Larger problems are usually split into several smaller projects.

What is the difference between Lean and Six Sigma?

Lean removes waste to make work flow faster; Six Sigma reduces variation to make results consistent. Lean Six Sigma combines the two.

Can Six Sigma be used with Agile?

Yes. Agile teams can use Six Sigma's root-cause and measurement tools, and Six Sigma teams can run DMAIC in short iterations with frequent reviews.

Is Six Sigma still relevant?

Yes, especially in manufacturing, healthcare, finance, logistics and shared services, and often as Lean Six Sigma. Its data-driven problem solving is useful in most industries.

Who created Six Sigma?

Engineer Bill Smith introduced it at Motorola in 1986. General Electric popularised it from 1995 under Jack Welch.
