RAID Log: Meaning, Example and How to Create One

A RAID log is a single register in which a project records its Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies, each with an owner, an action and a status. It gives the project manager, team and sponsor one place to see everything that could knock the project off course. This guide explains each letter, the columns to use, a full worked example and how to run a RAID review.

Key takeaways

  • A RAID log tracks risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies together, so one review covers all four.
  • Some teams read the A as Actions and the D as Decisions; agree your version at the start.
  • Every entry needs a clear description, a rating, a named owner, an action, a date and a status.
  • Items move between categories: a false assumption becomes a risk, and a risk that happens becomes an issue.
  • Review the log on a fixed rhythm, usually weekly, and report only the top items to the sponsor.
  • A short, current log is far more useful than a long one nobody reads.

What is a RAID log?

A RAID log is a project management document that records the risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies affecting a project in one place. Each entry has a description, a rating, an owner, an agreed action, a due date and a status. The log is created during initiation or planning, reviewed regularly and kept up to date until project closure.

Think of it as the project's early-warning system and memory in one. Because all four categories sit side by side, a single review can spot connections, such as a late dependency that is about to turn a risk into an issue.

The RAID log is not a formal product in PMI's PMBOK Guide or in PRINCE2. It is a widely used practice, especially in the UK, in consultancies and in PMOs, that combines several registers those methods describe. That makes it flexible: you can keep it as a light summary on a small project or as the main control log on a medium-sized one.

It works closely with other project controls. The project charter and business case are usually the first sources of risks and assumptions, the project status report summarises the top RAID items, and on larger projects a detailed risk register and issue log sit behind it.

What does RAID stand for?

In its most common form, RAID stands for risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies. Each letter answers a different question about the project.

LetterItemQuestion it answersExample
RRisksWhat might go wrong (or better than planned)?The supplier may deliver the hardware late
AAssumptionsWhat are we relying on without proof?Users will be available for testing in May
IIssuesWhat is going wrong today?The test server has been down for two days
DDependenciesWhat do we need from others, and what do they need from us?Data migration needs the finance team's new chart of accounts

Common variations

The letters are used in different ways. Asana's guide, for example, reads the D as decisions, and the University of Waterloo's IST project office uses risks, actions, issues and decisions. Other versions include:

  • RAIDD: risks, assumptions, issues, dependencies and decisions, so nothing is lost.
  • RAIDO or RAIDOO: adds opportunities, which some teams prefer to keep apart from threats.

None of these is wrong. What matters is that everyone uses the same version: decide at kick-off and write the definitions at the top of the log. If you need actions and decisions as well, add them as extra tabs.

Risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies explained

Each category needs slightly different information. Here is what to record, with a weak and a strong entry for each.

Risks

A project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, would affect at least one project objective such as scope, time, cost or quality. Write risks in a cause, event, effect format so the owner knows what to watch and what to protect.

Weak entryStrong entry
Supplier riskBecause the supplier is also delivering to two larger customers (cause), the server hardware may arrive after 1 March (event), delaying installation and go-live by up to three weeks (effect).

Rate each risk for probability and impact, then agree a response: avoid, reduce, transfer, accept or, for opportunities, exploit and enhance. What remains after the response is your residual risk, and any fallback plan belongs in a contingency plan.

Assumptions

An assumption is something the plan treats as true without proof, such as a resource being available, a licence covering extra users or a regulation staying the same. Assumptions are dangerous because they are invisible until they fail. Record them so they can be tested: give each one a validation owner and a date by which it will be confirmed or disproved.

Weak entryStrong entry
Licences are fineThe current CRM licence covers 50 extra users at no additional cost. Validate with procurement by 15 May; if false, budget impact is about one month of licence fees per user.

Most assumptions start in the business case and charter, where they are often listed alongside project constraints. When an assumption is proved false, raise it as a risk or an issue straight away and close the assumption with a note.

Issues

An issue is a problem that is happening now or is certain to happen. Unlike a risk, there is no probability to assess; you rate it by priority or severity and resolve it. Typical issues are a failed delivery, a defect or a missing team member.

Weak entryStrong entry
Testing problemsIntegration tests have failed since 3 June because the security certificates on the test environment expired. Blocks 12 test cases on the critical path. Infrastructure to renew certificates by 7 June.

Keep the full history of large issues in a dedicated issue log if you have one, and escalate any issue that breaks the agreed tolerances for time, cost or scope. Issues that need a change to the baseline go through change control.

Dependencies

A dependency is a relationship in which your project relies on something it does not fully control, or something else relies on your project. Inside the schedule these are task dependencies; in the RAID log you mainly track external ones: other projects, suppliers, other departments, regulators and client decisions.

Weak entryStrong entry
Need brand guidelinesInbound: website build needs final brand guidelines from the marketing rebrand project by 30 April. Provider: marketing PM. If late, page templates slip day for day. Status: at risk, draft due 14 May.

Record the direction (inbound or outbound), the provider and receiver, the date needed and the impact if it is late. Outbound dependencies, what others need from you, are easy to forget and are often the ones that damage relationships between teams.

Why use a RAID log?

A RAID log costs little to set up and pays back quickly if kept current. Its main benefits are:

  • One view of project health. The sponsor and team see threats, problems, assumptions and dependencies together instead of in four documents.
  • Earlier warning. Regular reviews catch risks and late dependencies before they become issues.
  • Clear accountability. Every item has a named owner, an action and a date, so it cannot drift.
  • Better decisions and reporting. Recorded assumptions and decisions explain why the plan looks as it does, and the top items feed straight into the status report.
  • Useful lessons. At closure the log shows what was foreseen, what was missed and what worked, which feeds lessons learned.

It also has limits, and it helps to be honest about them:

  • It takes discipline: a log that is not updated gives false comfort.
  • It can become a dumping ground for minor worries, hiding what matters.
  • On large or regulated projects it may not hold enough detail, so separate registers sit behind it.
Tip

As a rough guide, if your RAID log has more than about 40 open items on a medium-sized project, it is probably acting as a to-do list. Move routine tasks to the plan or board and keep the log for things that genuinely threaten objectives.

What to include: RAID log columns

A practical RAID log uses one set of core columns for every entry, plus a few fields that apply to only one type. A single table with a Type column is easiest to filter and sort; a workbook with one tab per type gives more room for detail. Both work.

ColumnWhat to record
IDUnique reference, ideally prefixed by type
TypeRisk, assumption, issue or dependency
Date raisedWhen the item was logged
Raised byWho identified it
DescriptionClear statement using the formats above
Category or areaWorkstream, deliverable or theme
ImpactEffect on time, cost, scope, quality or benefits
ProbabilityRisks only (and assumptions, if you rate the chance they are false)
Score or RAGProbability x impact for risks; priority for issues
OwnerOne named person responsible for the item
Action or responseWhat will be done, by whom
Due or review dateWhen the action is due or the item is next checked
StatusOpen, in progress, monitoring, closed (or confirmed/false for assumptions)
Date closed and notesWhen and why it was closed
LinksRelated items, tasks or change requests

Type-specific fields

TypeExtra fields worth adding
RisksTrigger or early warning sign, response strategy, contingency, residual rating
AssumptionsValidation method, validation date, consequence if false
IssuesSeverity, escalation level, resolution, date resolved
DependenciesDirection (inbound/outbound), provider, receiver, date needed, date committed

A simple scoring scale

Use a scale everyone understands. A common approach is to rate probability and impact from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high) and multiply them. The thresholds below are an example; set your own with the sponsor and record them at the top of the log.

Score (P x I)RatingWhat happens
15 to 25RedOwner acts now; reported to the sponsor in the next status report
8 to 14AmberResponse agreed and tracked at every RAID review
1 to 7GreenMonitored; reviewed monthly or at stage gates

For issues, a three-level priority (high, medium, low) tied to escalation rules is usually enough, for example: high issues that threaten a milestone go to the sponsor within 24 hours. How to create a RAID log step by step

You can build a working RAID log in an afternoon. The steps below suit most projects; scale the detail to the size and risk of yours.

  1. Agree the scope and letters. Decide whether D means dependencies or decisions, whether you track actions, and whether the RAID log is the main register or a summary of separate ones.
  2. Choose a format. A shared spreadsheet is enough for most single projects. Use a project management tool if many owners will update items or you need a programme roll-up.
  3. Set the columns and scales. Start with the core columns above, define your probability and impact scales and agree the escalation thresholds with the sponsor.
  4. Gather the first items. Review the charter, business case, work breakdown structure, schedule, contracts and lessons from similar projects. Then run a 60 to 90 minute RAID workshop (sometimes called RAID analysis) with the team and key stakeholders.
  5. Write each entry properly. Use cause-event-effect for risks, testable statements for assumptions, evidence for issues and both parties for dependencies. Remove duplicates.
  6. Assign one owner per item. The owner is the person best placed to act, not always the project manager. Confirm each owner has accepted the item; a RACI matrix helps where ownership is unclear.
  7. Agree actions and dates. Every open item needs a next action and a date for the next check, even if the action is simply to monitor.
  8. Set the review rhythm. Put a recurring RAID review in the calendar, decide what goes in the status report and publish the log where everyone can see it.

Involve the people doing the work: they usually know about the risk that will hurt you long before a steering group does. Good stakeholder management also gets easier when people see their concerns recorded and acted on.

RAID log example

Here is a worked example for a fictional project: a company is replacing its customer relationship management (CRM) system for a 120-person sales team, with go-live planned for 1 September. The log below shows a snapshot from week 10, using a 1 to 5 scale for probability (P) and impact (I).

IDTypeDescriptionPIOwnerActionDueStatus
R-01RiskBecause old CRM data has many duplicates, migration may overrun its 3 weeks, delaying user acceptance testing.44Data leadRun a trial migration on 10% of records; book a cleansing contractor if trial exceeds 3 days20 JunOpen: trial booked
R-02RiskBecause late June is quarter end, training attendance may be low, reducing adoption.33Sales ops managerMove training to early July; record short videos15 JunMonitoring
A-01AssumptionThe existing licence agreement allows 30 extra users at no cost during parallel running.4ProcurementConfirm in writing with the vendor12 JunFalse: raised as I-02
A-02AssumptionThe sales director will sign off the new pipeline stages by the end of June.3PMAgenda item at the steering group on 24 Jun24 JunUnconfirmed
I-01IssueFinance integration fails for invoices over 50 lines, blocking order-to-invoice testing.4Integration developerFix pagination in the API call; retest14 JunIn progress
I-02IssueParallel running licences cost an extra amount not in the budget (from A-01).3PMRequest contingency release from sponsor18 JunEscalated
D-01DependencyInbound: single sign-on depends on the IT security project's identity upgrade, needed by 1 Aug.5PMFortnightly check with IT security PM; track their milestone1 AugOn track

Reading the log takes a minute: R-01 scores 16 (red) and goes in the status report; I-01 is on the critical path; A-01 has already been disproved and moved on as I-02; D-02 is an outbound dependency that another team is waiting for. That is the kind of picture a sponsor needs in a single glance.

How items move between categories

RAID items change type over time, and keeping them together lets you follow each one.

What happensMoveExample from the log
An assumption is proved falseAssumption becomes a risk or an issueA-01 licences were not free, so I-02 was raised
A risk occursRisk becomes an issue; close the riskIf the trial migration overruns, R-01 becomes an issue
A dependency is lateDependency becomes an issue, or a risk if lateness is likely but not certainD-02 marked at risk; if the field list misses 30 Jun, raise an issue
An issue reveals a wider threatIssue spawns a new riskI-01 suggests other large transactions may fail, so a new risk is logged
A decision resolves an itemRecord the decision and close the itemSponsor releases contingency, I-02 closed

When you move an item, close the original with a note such as 'Converted to I-02 on 12 Jun' rather than deleting it. The history matters at audits and for lessons learned.

How to run a RAID review meeting

The log only works if it is reviewed. Most project teams review RAID weekly, often as part of the regular status meeting; fast-moving or high-risk projects may look at red items daily, and steady projects may review fortnightly with a deeper look at each stage gate.

TimeAgenda itemOutput
5 minNew items raised since the last reviewAccepted, merged or rejected; owner agreed
10 minRed risks and high-priority issuesActions confirmed or escalated
5 minAssumptions due for validationConfirmed, or converted to risks/issues
5 minDependencies due in the next four weeksStatus checked with the other team
5 minItems to close and items to escalateClean log; escalation list for the sponsor

A few practices keep the meeting short and useful:

  • Ask owners to update their items before the meeting, so the review is about decisions rather than data entry.
  • Invite the owners of dependencies from other teams when their items are due.
  • Finish by agreeing the three to five items that go into the status report.
  • Record decisions in a decisions tab or the minutes.

At steering group or board level, present a one-page summary: counts by type and rating, the top red items, the trend since last month and any decisions needed. That fits naturally with the escalation routes in your project governance arrangements.

RAID log vs risk register, issue log and decision log

People often ask whether they need a RAID log as well as other registers. The table shows what each one covers.

RAID logRisk registerIssue logDecision log
CoversRisks, assumptions, issues, dependencies (or variants)Risks only, often threats and opportunitiesCurrent problems, concerns and change requestsDecisions, who made them and why
Detail per itemModerateHigh: causes, triggers, responses, residual risk, contingencyHigh: impact analysis, resolution history, escalationLow to moderate
Best forSmall and medium projects; programme summariesLarge, complex or regulated projectsProjects with many issues or formal change controlProjects where decisions must be traceable

A sensible rule of thumb: on small and medium projects, one RAID log is enough. On large, high-risk or regulated projects, keep a detailed risk register and issue log and use the RAID log as the summary view across them. Methods such as PRINCE2 describe separate risk and issue records, so teams using those methods often treat RAID as a reporting layer rather than a replacement.

Whichever you choose, avoid keeping the same item in two places with different statuses. If you have separate registers, the RAID summary should pull from them, not duplicate them. A risk register template works well behind a RAID summary for larger projects.

RAID logs in Agile teams and programmes

Agile teams manage much of what a RAID log holds through their normal ways of working. Impediments come up in the daily scrum and sit on the board, risks are reduced by delivering in small increments, and assumptions are tested with real users each sprint.

A RAID log still earns its place when several teams depend on each other, when work involves suppliers or other departments, or when governance needs a record. Common patterns are:

  • Programme RAID: in scaled frameworks, cross-team risks and dependencies identified at PI planning are tracked on a programme board and a shared RAID view.
  • Lightweight RAID: a short list reviewed at sprint planning or the retrospective, holding only items the team cannot resolve on its own board.
  • Decision records: a decisions tab captures architecture and product choices and the reasoning behind them.

Keep it lean: if an item can live as a card on the board, put it there.

Common RAID log mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Vague one-word entries such as 'resources'Owners cannot act on themUse cause-event-effect and add the impact
No named owner, or 'the team' as ownerNobody actsOne person per item, who has agreed to own it
Every item rated highNothing stands outUse defined scales and challenge ratings in the review
Assumptions recorded but never testedHidden risks stay hiddenGive each assumption a validation date
Issues logged as risks (or the reverse)Wrong response and urgencyAsk: has it happened? If yes, it is an issue
Only inbound dependencies trackedOther teams are let downLog what others need from you too
Log updated only before steering meetingsOut of date most of the timeOwners update continuously; weekly review
Using it as a task listReal threats get buriedMove routine tasks to the plan or board
Not linked to the plan or budgetActions never happenPut responses in the schedule and contingency in the budget

The most damaging mistake is cultural: punishing people who raise bad news. If raising a risk feels like admitting failure, the log will look calm until the project misses its date.

Tools for keeping a RAID log

A spreadsheet is the most common RAID tool, and for a single project it is often all you need. Our RAID log template is ready to use in Google Sheets or Excel.

Move to a project management tool when several owners update items, when items need to link to tasks, or when a PMO wants to roll up RAID across many projects. Features worth looking for:

  • Custom fields for type, probability, impact and status, and intake forms so anyone can raise an item.
  • Automations that remind owners of due dates and flag red items.
  • Dashboards that roll up RAID across projects, with links to the tasks that resolve each item.

Most leading platforms can do this with custom fields and dashboards. Wrike, our top-ranked tool, suits teams that want request forms, cross-project dashboards and configurable workflows in one place; Smartsheet suits teams who prefer a spreadsheet layout; Jira suits software teams who log impediments as issues.

Frequently asked questions

What is a RAID log in project management?

A RAID log is a single register for a project's risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies. Each item has an owner, action, date and status, and the log is reviewed regularly so problems are dealt with early.

What does RAID stand for?

Most often risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies. Some organisations use actions instead of assumptions, or decisions instead of dependencies, and RAIDD adds decisions as a fifth category.

What is the purpose of a RAID log?

To give the project team and sponsor one up-to-date view of what could go wrong, what is going wrong, what the plan relies on and what it depends on, with clear owners and actions for each.

Who is responsible for the RAID log?

The project manager, or a project coordinator or PMO analyst on their behalf, maintains the log. Each item has its own owner, who keeps that entry up to date and carries out the action.

How often should a RAID log be reviewed?

Weekly is typical, often as part of the status meeting. High-risk projects may review red items daily, and steady projects may review fortnightly plus a fuller review at stage gates.

What is the difference between a RAID log and a risk register?

A risk register covers risks only, usually in more detail. A RAID log also covers assumptions, issues and dependencies, giving a broader but less detailed view.

What is the difference between a risk and an issue?

A risk is uncertain and might happen in future; an issue is happening now or is certain. When a risk occurs, it becomes an issue.

What is the difference between an assumption and a risk?

An assumption is something you treat as true for planning without proof. The possibility that it is false creates a risk, so test assumptions and convert false ones into risks or issues.

What is a dependency in a RAID log?

A link where your project relies on something outside its control, such as another project's deliverable, a supplier or a decision, or where another team relies on you. Record the provider, receiver, date needed and impact if late.

What is a RAIDD log?

A RAID log that also records decisions: risks, assumptions, issues, dependencies and decisions.

What is RAID analysis?

RAID analysis is the exercise of identifying and assessing a project's risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies, often in a workshop at the start of the project. Its output is the first version of the RAID log.

Can I create a RAID log in Excel or Google Sheets?

Yes. A spreadsheet with one table and a type column, or one tab per category, is the most common format. Our free RAID log template is available for Google Sheets, Excel and PDF.

Do Agile teams use RAID logs?

Some do, mainly for cross-team dependencies, programme risks and decisions. Day-to-day impediments are usually handled on the team board instead.

What happens to the RAID log at project closure?

Open items are closed or handed to the operational owner, every item gets a final status, and the log is archived. This is part of project closure, and patterns from the log feed the lessons learned review.
