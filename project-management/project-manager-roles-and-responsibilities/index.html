Project Manager Roles and Responsibilities: A Complete Guide

A project manager's role is to deliver a project's agreed objectives within scope, time, cost and quality, and their core responsibilities are planning the work, leading the team, managing stakeholders, controlling risk and change, and reporting progress until the project is closed. They are accountable for the outcome, but they rarely do all the work themselves. This guide sets out each responsibility in practical terms, how it changes by phase, seniority and methodology, and what the job looks like week to week.

Key takeaways

  • A project manager is accountable for delivering agreed objectives, not for doing every task personally.
  • Responsibilities fall into eight areas: planning, schedule and resources, budget, risk, stakeholders, team leadership, quality and change, and reporting.
  • The focus shifts across the lifecycle, from defining the project at initiation to handover and lessons learned at closure.
  • Authority comes from the project charter and agreed tolerances, and often depends on influence more than line management.
  • In Scrum, project management work is shared between the Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers.
  • Honest, early communication and escalation matter as much as a good plan.

What is a project manager?

A project manager is the person who leads a project from start to finish and is accountable for achieving its objectives. They turn a goal into a plan, organise the people and resources to deliver it, track progress, deal with risks, issues and changes, and keep the sponsor and stakeholders informed until the work is handed over and the project formally closed.

PMI's PMBOK Guide defines the project manager as 'the person assigned by the performing organization to lead the team that is responsible for achieving the project objectives'. The important word is lead. The specialists on the team design, build and test; the project manager makes sure the right work happens in the right order, with the right people, and that the people paying for it know where it stands.

The core roles a project manager plays

RoleWhat it means in practice
PlannerDefines scope, breaks down the work, estimates, schedules and budgets
LeaderBuilds the team, sets direction, motivates people and resolves conflict
CommunicatorKeeps the sponsor, stakeholders and team aligned with honest, regular updates
IntegratorConnects scope, schedule, cost, risk and people so that a change in one is reflected in the others
Problem solverRemoves blockers, makes or escalates decisions and adapts the plan as the project learns

The 8 core responsibilities of a project manager

Job descriptions often list fifteen or twenty duties. They group naturally into eight areas. For each one, the outputs show what the responsibility produces in practice.

1. Scope and planning

Clarify what the project must deliver and why, then turn it into a realistic plan. This means agreeing objectives, defining project scope, building a work breakdown structure and pulling everything together in a project management plan. Outputs: project charter (with the sponsor), scope statement, WBS, plan.

2. Schedule and resources

Sequence the work, estimate durations, build the schedule and secure the people, equipment and suppliers needed. Rebalance workload when people are over-allocated. Outputs: schedule baseline, resource plan, milestone list.

3. Budget and cost control

Estimate costs, set the project budget, track actual spend against plan and forecast the final cost. Raise problems as soon as forecasts move outside tolerance. Outputs: cost baseline, cost reports, forecasts.

4. Risk and issue management

Identify what could go wrong (or better than planned), agree responses and owners, and keep a current risk register. When problems happen, log them in the issue log, make sure someone is fixing them and escalate what the project cannot resolve. Outputs: risk register, issue log, contingency plans.

5. Stakeholders and communication

Find out who is affected, what they need and how much influence they have, then keep them engaged. Agree who gets what information, how and when, in a communication plan. Our guide to stakeholder management covers the techniques. Outputs: stakeholder register, communication plan, meeting cadence.

6. Team leadership

Form the team, make roles clear, set expectations, coach, give feedback and resolve conflict. In most organisations team members report to someone else, so this is leadership through influence rather than authority. Outputs: team charter or ways of working, a responsibility matrix, regular one-to-ones.

7. Quality and change control

Agree acceptance criteria, build reviews and testing into the plan and make sure deliverables meet them. Assess every change request for its effect on scope, time, cost and risk, and route it through change control so that scope creep does not happen by default. Outputs: quality plan, change log, acceptance records.

8. Governance and reporting

Work within the organisation's project governance: stage gates, approvals, PMO standards and audit requirements. Report status honestly and regularly in a project status report, including bad news. Outputs: status reports, gate papers, decision log.

Tip

If you can only do three things well, make them: a clear plan, current risk and issue logs, and honest weekly reporting. Most project failures show up first as gaps in one of these.

Project manager responsibilities by phase

The balance of work changes as the project moves through its lifecycle. The table uses the five phases common to most methods; our guide to project management phases explains each in detail.

PhaseKey responsibilitiesTypical outputs
InitiationClarify the business need with the sponsor, outline scope and objectives, identify stakeholders, confirm feasibilityProject charter, initial stakeholder list
PlanningDefine scope, build the WBS, estimate, schedule, budget, plan risk, quality, communication and procurementProject management plan, baselines, risk register
ExecutionLead the team, assign work, manage suppliers, hold meetings, remove blockers, engage stakeholdersDeliverables, meeting notes, updated logs

Monitoring and control is not a separate stage that happens after execution. It runs alongside execution every week until closure.

What a project manager does day to day

Most of a project manager's time goes on communication: meetings, conversations, messages and reports. The rest goes on planning, updating plans and logs, and solving problems. A realistic week on a mid-sized project looks like this.

WhenTypical activities
MondayReview the schedule, risks and issues; agree the week's priorities with the team; check resource clashes
DailyShort stand-up or check-in to track progress and remove blockers; answer team and supplier questions
Tuesday to WednesdayStakeholder and supplier meetings; decision follow-ups; review deliverables against acceptance criteria
ThursdayUpdate the risk register, issue log and forecasts; assess change requests; prepare for any board or gate meeting
FridaySend the status report; one-to-ones with team members; plan next week and look two to four weeks ahead

The look-ahead matters. Experienced project managers spend part of every week on what is coming next month: approvals that need booking, suppliers that need notice, people who will be needed. That is how risks are spotted before they become issues.

Tip

Block time in your calendar for thinking and planning. If every hour is a meeting, you will be reacting to problems rather than preventing them.

Worked example: a supplier delay

Responsibilities make most sense when you see them working together. In this example, a project manager is running a nine-month office move for 200 staff. In month five, the furniture supplier says delivery will be three weeks late.

  1. Understand the problem. The PM confirms the facts with the supplier: which items, how late and why. They check the contract for delivery terms.
  2. Assess the impact. Using the schedule, the PM finds that desks are on the critical path. A three-week delay would push the move date back by two weeks and clash with the lease end.
  3. Log it. The risk 'supplier capacity may delay furniture' was already in the risk register, with a trigger of 'supplier misses the mid-point check'. The PM marks it as occurred and opens an entry in the issue log with an owner and due date.
  4. Develop options. With the team, the PM prepares three options: accept the delay and extend the lease by a month; split the delivery so two floors move on time; or source desks for one floor from a second supplier at extra cost.
  5. Escalate the decision. Extending the lease and the extra supplier cost both exceed the PM's tolerance, so the PM takes the options, costs and a recommendation to the sponsor within two days.
  6. Act on the decision. The sponsor chooses the split delivery. The PM raises a change request, updates the schedule and budget baselines and briefs the team.
  7. Communicate. The PM tells affected staff about the revised plan, updates the status report and records the supplier performance for lessons learned.

Notice what the PM did not do: they did not hide the problem, make a decision above their authority or try to fix it alone. They used the plan and logs to understand the impact, involved the team in options, and gave the sponsor what they needed to decide quickly.

Who is accountable for what

Confusion about who decides what causes many project problems. A RACI matrix makes this explicit. The table shows a typical split; adjust it to your organisation and record it early. You can start from our free RACI matrix template.

Approve the business case and fundingCAIC
Write the project charterRAIC
Build the schedule and planA/RICC
Do the specialist workIIRC
Prioritise requirementsCICA/R
Approve changes beyond toleranceRAIC

R = responsible (does the work), A = accountable (makes the final decision), C = consulted, I = informed.

Project manager authority

Responsibility without authority is a common complaint among project managers. The amount of authority varies widely, so clarify it at the start rather than discovering it during a crisis.

  • Where it is written. The project charter should name the project manager and state what they may decide: spending limits, resource decisions, supplier management and when they must escalate. Our project charter template includes these fields.
  • Tolerances. PRINCE2 formalises this: the project board sets tolerances for time, cost, scope and other measures, and the project manager runs the project day to day within them. If a forecast moves beyond tolerance, the project manager escalates to the board rather than acting alone. See our PRINCE2 guide.
  • Organisation structure. In a functional organisation, team members report to department heads and the project manager has limited authority. In a matrix organisation, authority is shared. In a project-based organisation, the project manager often controls budget and team directly.
  • Influence. Even with formal authority, project managers get most done through trust, credibility and relationships with the sponsor and line managers.

A project management office, where one exists, usually sets the standards a project manager works within and may approve plans or reports before they reach the sponsor.

How the role differs by methodology

ApproachHow project manager responsibilities change
Predictive (waterfall)The PM leads detailed up-front planning, controls changes against baselines and reports progress against the plan
Agile (Scrum)The Scrum Guide has no project manager role. The Product Owner owns the backlog and value, the Scrum Master owns Scrum effectiveness and the Developers plan their own work. See Scrum team roles
Agile at scale or in large organisationsMany organisations keep a project or delivery manager for budgets, dependencies, vendors, governance and reporting that sit outside a single team
HybridThe PM runs a predictive plan for contracts, milestones and approvals while teams deliver in iterations; integrating the two is the main challenge
PRINCE2The PM manages day to day within board tolerances, maintains the project log, and produces plans and reports for the project board at each stage

In Agile environments, much of the coordination, stakeholder and governance work still needs doing. What changes is who does it and how plans are made.

Skills behind the responsibilities

Each responsibility depends on skills. PMI groups them in its Talent Triangle under three headings: Ways of Working (methods and techniques such as predictive, Agile and hybrid), Power Skills (communication, leadership, collaboration, problem solving) and Business Acumen (understanding strategy, value and the wider organisation). PMP holders earn professional development units across all three.

  • Planning and estimating for scope, schedule and budget.
  • Communication, written and spoken, for status reports, meetings and difficult conversations.
  • Leadership and influence to motivate people who do not report to you.
  • Negotiation with suppliers, line managers and stakeholders.
  • Risk thinking: spotting what could go wrong before it does.
  • Commercial awareness of contracts, costs and value.
  • Tool fluency: most PMs use project management software for schedules, boards and reporting; see our ranking of project management tools.

For self-assessment and development plans, see Project Management Skills: Hard, Soft and How to Build Them.

Project manager job description outline

If you are writing or reviewing a job description, this outline covers the responsibilities in a form hiring managers and candidates recognise. Adjust the scale and sector details.

  • Purpose of the role: lead [type] projects from initiation to closure, delivering agreed scope within time, budget and quality.
  • Planning: define scope and objectives with the sponsor; produce and maintain plans, schedules and budgets.
  • Delivery: lead a cross-functional team of [number]; coordinate suppliers; remove blockers.
  • Control: track progress, manage risks, issues and changes, and forecast cost and completion.
  • Stakeholders: engage stakeholders at all levels and report status to the sponsor and board.
  • Governance: follow PMO standards and stage gates; maintain project records.
  • Closure: secure acceptance, hand over to operations and capture lessons learned.
  • Requirements: experience leading projects of similar size; knowledge of [method]; a certification such as PMP or PRINCE2 is often preferred.

Your first 30 days on a new project

Whether you are new to the role or taking over a project mid-way, the first month sets the tone. This sequence works for most projects and turns the responsibilities above into a starting plan.

  1. Meet the sponsor first. Agree what success looks like, the top three priorities, your authority and tolerances, and how they want to be kept informed.
  2. Read everything that exists. Business case, charter, plans, contracts, the risk register, the issue log and the last few status reports. Note gaps and contradictions.
  3. Meet each key stakeholder and team member. Ask what is going well, what worries them and what they need from you. Listen more than you talk.
  4. Confirm the baseline. Check that scope, schedule and budget are realistic. If they are not, say so early with evidence, rather than inheriting a plan you cannot deliver.
  5. Refresh the risk and issue logs. Run a short risk workshop, assign owners and agree actions for the top risks and open issues.
  6. Agree roles and rhythms. Confirm who decides what (a RACI), and set the meeting, reporting and review cadence.
  7. Fix one visible problem. Resolve a blocker the team has been waiting on. Early, practical help builds credibility faster than a new template.
  8. Send your first honest status report. Give the sponsor a clear view of where the project really stands, including any re-planning you recommend.
Tip

When you take over a project, record the state you found it in. A short 'handover baseline' note protects you and helps the sponsor see the effect of later decisions.

How a project manager's performance is judged

Sponsors and PMOs judge project managers on more than whether the project finished on time. Knowing the measures helps you focus on what matters.

MeasureWhat good looks like
Delivery against baselinesScope, schedule and cost within agreed tolerances, or changes approved formally
Forecast accuracyForecasts that are stable and honest, with no late surprises
Stakeholder confidenceSponsors and key stakeholders trust the reports and feel involved in decisions
Risk and issue handlingRisks spotted early, issues resolved or escalated quickly, few repeat problems
Team healthPeople know their roles, workload is sustainable and the team would work with the PM again
Benefits and qualityDeliverables are accepted first time and support the benefits in the business case

Note that a project can be cancelled and still be well managed. Recommending that a project stops, when the business case no longer holds, is one of the most valuable things a project manager can do.

Common project manager mistakes

  • Doing the specialist work yourself. Coordination suffers. Fix: delegate, and protect time for planning and stakeholders.
  • Reporting green until the last minute. Late bad news destroys trust. Fix: report amber early with a recovery plan.
  • Accepting changes informally. A favour here and there becomes scope creep. Fix: route every change through change control.
  • Treating the plan as fixed. Plans go out of date quickly. Fix: re-plan when facts change and re-baseline formally.
  • Unclear roles. People assume someone else is deciding. Fix: agree a RACI at kick-off.
  • Neglecting the sponsor. Sponsors who are surprised stop supporting the project. Fix: a short, regular one-to-one.
  • Risk register as a one-off exercise. Fix: review risks every week or fortnight with their owners.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main roles and responsibilities of a project manager?

Planning the project, managing schedule, resources and budget, controlling risks, issues, quality and changes, leading the team, engaging stakeholders and reporting progress until formal closure.

What is the most important responsibility of a project manager?

Keeping the project aligned with its agreed objectives and making sure the sponsor always has an honest picture of progress, so decisions are made in time. Clear communication supports every other responsibility.

What does a project manager do day to day?

Checks progress, removes blockers, meets stakeholders and suppliers, updates plans and logs, assesses changes, makes or escalates decisions and reports status. Much of the day is spent communicating.

Who does a project manager report to?

For the project, usually the project sponsor or project board. For their own role and career, often a line manager, head of delivery or the PMO.

What authority does a project manager have?

It is set in the project charter and varies by organisation. It usually covers day-to-day decisions within agreed tolerances for cost and time; larger decisions go to the sponsor or board.

What is the difference between a project manager and a project sponsor?

The sponsor owns the business case, funding and benefits and makes major decisions. The project manager is accountable for delivering the project within the limits the sponsor sets.

What is the difference between a project manager and a product manager?

A project manager delivers a defined project with a start and end. A product manager owns a product's direction and value for its whole life, across many projects or releases.

Do Agile teams have a project manager?

Scrum has no project manager role; the work is shared between the Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. Many organisations still use a project or delivery manager to coordinate budgets, dependencies and reporting across teams.

Does a project manager need technical knowledge?

Enough to understand the work, ask good questions, judge estimates and spot risks. Deep technical skills matter more in roles such as technical or IT project manager.

What should a project manager not do?

They should not do all the specialist work themselves, hide problems, accept changes informally or make decisions beyond their agreed authority.

What skills does a project manager need?

Planning, communication, leadership, negotiation, risk management and commercial awareness, plus familiarity with methods and tools. PMI groups these as Ways of Working, Power Skills and Business Acumen.

How do I become a project manager?

Build experience coordinating work, learn the fundamentals, and consider a certification such as CAPM or PMP. Read How to Become a Project Manager for a step-by-step route.
