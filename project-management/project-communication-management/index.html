Project Communication Management: Processes and Examples

Project communication management is the work of making sure the right people get the right project information, in the right format and at the right time, and that they understand it and act on it. It has three parts: planning communication, delivering it and monitoring whether it works. This guide explains each process, the methods and formulas you need, and a full worked example you can adapt.

Key takeaways

  • Communication management has three processes: plan communications management, manage communications and monitor communications.
  • In the PMBOK Guide 8th edition the three communication processes sit inside the Stakeholders performance domain, according to published summaries.
  • Match the method to the message: interactive for decisions and sensitive topics, push for things people must receive, pull for reference material.
  • Communication channels grow quickly with team size: n(n - 1) / 2, so 10 people have 45 channels and 12 people have 66.
  • Sending is not communicating. Build in feedback, measure whether messages land and adjust the plan.
  • Keep one source of truth for status, decisions and documents so people are not chasing information across tools.

What is project communication management?

Project communication management is the set of processes a project uses to plan, create, distribute, store and monitor project information. Its aim is simple: every stakeholder gets the information they need, in a form they can use, when they need it, and the project team can confirm that messages have been understood and acted on.

In practice it covers all the information that flows around a project: status reports, steering committee papers, meeting notes, change decisions, risk escalations, team updates, supplier correspondence and announcements to end users. It covers formal and informal communication, and people inside and outside the organisation.

Two related documents are often confused:

  • The communication management plan is the part of the project management plan that sets the rules: audiences, information needs, methods, frequency, owners, escalation routes, templates, storage and how the plan will be reviewed.
  • The communication plan (or communication matrix) is the working schedule that comes out of it: a table of who receives what, how, when and from whom. On smaller projects the two are usually one short document.

The project manager sits at the centre of this flow. You will often see the claim that project managers spend 90% of their time communicating; it is widely repeated, but we could not trace it to a solid study, so treat it as a figure of speech. What is clear is that most project management work, from running meetings to negotiating scope and reporting to the sponsor, is communication.

Why communication management matters

Many project problems that look technical start as communication problems. A sponsor is surprised by a delay the team knew about for weeks. A supplier delivers to the old specification because a change decision never reached them. Scope is disputed because an agreement was never recorded.

Good communication management prevents these failures. It:

  • Keeps stakeholders informed and engaged, so support does not quietly fade.
  • Gets decisions made on time, because the people who decide have what they need in a form they can act on.
  • Reduces misunderstanding about scope, priorities and responsibilities.
  • Makes risks and issues visible while they are still cheap to fix.
  • Creates a record of decisions and agreements that protects everyone when memories differ.

The best-known evidence comes from PMI's Pulse of the Profession in-depth report on communications, published in May 2013. It found that US$135 million was at risk for every US$1 billion spent on projects, and that 56% of that amount (US$75 million) was at risk because of ineffective communications. The same report found that one in five projects was unsuccessful because of ineffective communications, and that 55% of project managers agreed effective communication with all stakeholders was the most critical success factor. The data is more than a decade old, so treat it as an indication, not a current measure.

The three communication management processes

The PMBOK Guide sixth edition defines Project Communications Management as one of ten knowledge areas, with three processes. They form a cycle that repeats throughout the project: you plan, you deliver, you check, and you feed what you learn back into the plan.

ProcessKey questionMain inputsMain outputs
Plan communications managementWho needs what information, why, when, how and from whom?Project charter, stakeholder register, organisational standardsCommunication management plan
Manage communicationsAre we creating, sending and storing information as planned?Communication plan, work performance reports, issue and change logsProject communications (reports, presentations, notes), updated records
Monitor communicationsIs communication working, and what should change?Communications sent, feedback, stakeholder engagementWork performance information, change requests, plan updates

Plan communications management

Planning starts from your stakeholder analysis. For each person or group you work out their information needs, how much detail they want, how they prefer to receive it, how often, and who on the team is responsible. You also set the rules that apply to everyone: where documents live, which template a status report uses, how quickly urgent issues are escalated, and any constraints such as confidentiality, language or time zones. Planning happens early, but it is revisited at every phase.

Manage communications

This is the delivery process: writing and sending reports, running meetings, publishing updates, answering questions and storing records so people can find them later. Managing communications is not only about pushing information out. It includes choosing words carefully, checking understanding during conversations and making sure the record of decisions is complete.

Monitor communications

Monitoring checks whether communication is achieving its purpose. Are decisions being made on time? Are people asking the same questions repeatedly? Are stakeholders surprised by things that were reported? When the answer shows a gap, you adjust the plan: a different method, a different frequency, a different messenger or a different level of detail.

Communication management in PMBOK 7 and PMBOK 8

If you are studying for an exam or working with an organisation that follows PMI standards, it helps to know how the topic has moved between editions of the PMBOK Guide.

EditionStructureWhere communication sits
6th edition (2017)Ten knowledge areas, five process groupsIts own knowledge area, Project Communications Management, with three processes
7th edition (2021)Twelve principles, eight performance domains, no processesNo separate area; communication runs through the Stakeholders and Team performance domains
8th edition (November 2025)Six principles, seven performance domains, processes reintroduced in a non-prescriptive formInside the Stakeholders performance domain, alongside the stakeholder engagement processes

According to published summaries of the 8th edition, the Stakeholders performance domain contains seven processes: identify stakeholders, plan stakeholder engagement, plan communications management, manage stakeholder engagement, manage communications, monitor stakeholder engagement and monitor communications. The practical message is that communication is treated as one of the main tools of stakeholder management, not as a separate discipline.

Communication methods: interactive, push and pull

The PMBOK Guide groups communication methods into three types. Choosing the right one for each message is one of the simplest ways to improve communication on a project.

MethodHow it worksExamplesBest forWatch out for
InteractiveTwo-way, real-time exchange between two or more peopleMeetings, phone and video calls, workshops, instant messaging conversationsDecisions, complex or sensitive topics, conflict, building relationshipsCostly in people's time; decisions need recording afterwards
PushSent to specific recipients, who do not have to ask for itEmails, status reports, memos, newsletters, notificationsInformation people must receive, such as decisions, deadlines and warningsSending does not prove it was read or understood
PullStored centrally for people to access when they chooseIntranet pages, shared drives, project dashboards, knowledge bases, recorded briefingsReference material, large audiences, detail some people want and others do notPeople may never look; needs signposting

Which method for which message?

Use this rule of thumb when you are unsure:

MessageRecommended methodWhy
A decision is needed from the sponsorInteractive, followed by a short written record (push)Discussion surfaces concerns; the record prevents later disputes
Weekly progressPush (status report) plus pull (live dashboard)Regular rhythm for those who must know; detail for those who want it
Bad news, such as a missed milestoneInteractive first, then pushPeople should hear bad news from you directly, with options
Policy, process or training materialPull, announced by pushLarge audience, used when needed
Changes affecting end usersPush and interactive briefings through line managersPeople trust messages from their own manager

Types of project communication

Beyond the three methods, it is useful to think about the type of communication, because each has its own expectations about tone, formality and record-keeping.

TypeDescriptionExamples
FormalStructured, planned and usually recordedSteering committee papers, contracts, change requests, sign-offs
InformalUnplanned, conversationalCorridor chats, quick calls, chat messages
WrittenPermanent and easy to shareReports, emails, meeting notes, documentation
VerbalFaster, with tone and immediate feedbackStand-ups, presentations, phone calls
InternalWithin the project or organisationTeam updates, briefings to department heads
ExternalWith customers, suppliers, regulators or the publicSupplier progress meetings, client reports, press statements

A useful habit is to follow informal communication with a formal record when it matters. If a decision is agreed in a hallway conversation, send a two-line note confirming it, and log it in the decision or issue log.

The sender-receiver communication model

The basic communication model in project management texts describes how a message travels from one person to another, and where it can go wrong.

  1. Encode. The sender turns an idea into words, numbers or images.
  2. Transmit. The message is sent through a channel: email, meeting, report, call.
  3. Decode. The receiver interprets the message, through their own knowledge and assumptions.
  4. Acknowledge. The receiver confirms they have received it. This is not the same as agreeing or understanding.
  5. Feedback. The receiver responds, showing whether the meaning came across as intended.

Anything that distorts the message along the way is called noise: jargon, a poor connection, a distracted reader, cultural differences, or a 40-page report when the reader wanted one page. The interactive version of the model stresses the last two steps: communication is only complete when the sender has checked that the receiver understood.

Communication channels formula

Every pair of people on a project is a potential communication channel. The number of channels grows much faster than the number of people, which is why communication gets harder as projects grow.

The formula is n(n - 1) / 2, where n is the number of people (including the project manager).

People (n)CalculationChannels
55 x 4 / 210
88 x 7 / 228
1010 x 9 / 245
1212 x 11 / 266
2020 x 19 / 2190

Worked example: channels added

Exam questions often ask how many channels are added when people join. Calculate both totals and subtract:

  1. A team has 10 people: 10 x 9 / 2 = 45 channels.
  2. Two people join, so n = 12: 12 x 11 / 2 = 66 channels.
  3. Channels added = 66 - 45 = 21.

Read the question carefully: "a project manager and 9 team members" means n = 10.

The number is not a target. Its practical lesson is that you cannot rely on everyone talking to everyone. As teams grow you need structure: clear roles (a RACI matrix helps), a predictable meeting rhythm, team leads who pass information on, and one place where status and decisions are published.

How to manage project communication step by step

This process works for most projects, whether you follow a predictive, agile or hybrid approach. On a small project it can take an hour; on a large programme it is a workshop and a formal document.

  1. Identify stakeholders. Start from the project charter and list everyone affected or able to influence the project. Record them in a stakeholder register.
  2. Analyse their information needs. For each group, ask what decisions they make, what they need to know to make them, how much detail they want and how they prefer to receive it.
  3. Set communication objectives. Decide what each communication must achieve: inform, consult, gain approval, build support or prompt action. The objective drives the method.
  4. Choose methods, frequency and owners. Match interactive, push or pull to each message, set a rhythm and name the person responsible for every item.
  5. Agree rules and escalation routes. Decide where documents live, which templates to use, response times for questions, and how issues move up to the sponsor or governance board.
  6. Write the plan and agree it with the sponsor. Keep it short. A one-page matrix plus a few rules is usually enough.
  7. Deliver consistently. Send reports on the agreed day, using the same format each time, so people know where to look and can spot changes quickly.
  8. Monitor and adjust. Gather feedback, watch the indicators in the measurement section below and update the plan at each phase or when stakeholders change.

Questions to ask in a communication requirements analysis

  • What decisions does this person or group make, and when?
  • What happens if they find out late?
  • How much detail do they want: headline, summary or full data?
  • Which format do they actually read: email, dashboard, meeting, phone call?
  • Who do they trust to deliver bad news?

Worked example: communication management on an ERP rollout

This example is illustrative. A 9-month ERP rollout has a steering committee, 4 department heads, a 12-person project team, an external implementation partner and 600 end users across two sites.

Step 1: the communication matrix

AudienceWhat they needMethodFrequencyOwner
Steering committeeProgress against milestones, top risks, decisions neededMeeting plus one-page RAG report (push)Monthly, plus by exceptionProject manager
SponsorEarly warning, decisions, political issuesOne-to-one (interactive)Weekly, 30 minutesProject manager
Department headsImpact on their teams, readiness actions, training datesBriefing call (interactive) and action list (push)FortnightlyChange lead
Project teamTasks, blockers, prioritiesStand-up and task board (interactive and pull)DailyWorkstream leads
Implementation partnerDeliverables, issues, acceptance statusJoint progress meeting (interactive), shared RAID log (pull)WeeklyProject manager
End usersWhat is changing, when, what they must do, trainingNewsletter (push), intranet page (pull), team briefingsMonthly, weekly in the last 6 weeksChange lead

Step 2: rules and escalation

  • All decisions are recorded in the decision log within one working day of being made.
  • Status, risks and issues live in one RAID log in the project tool; email attachments are not the record.
  • Red issues are escalated to the sponsor the same day by phone, then in writing.
  • Questions from department heads receive a reply within two working days, even if the reply is "we are checking".

Step 3: monitor and adjust

Three months in, the project manager reviews the indicators. Newsletter open rates are low, the service desk is already fielding questions about go-live, and a readiness survey shows that only a minority of end users can say what is changing for them.

SignalDiagnosisChange to the plan
Newsletter rarely openedWrong channel for end users; email overloadDepartment heads deliver a 5-minute briefing in existing team meetings, using a script and FAQ from the project
Repeated questions to the service deskInformation exists but is hard to findSingle intranet page with a search-friendly FAQ, linked from every message
Steering committee asks for more detail on data migrationOne-page report too thin for the highest riskAdd a monthly data migration annex with trend charts
Partner surprised by a scope decisionDecision log not shared with partnerGive the partner read access to the decision log and add a weekly decisions summary

The next readiness survey shows a clear improvement, and repeated service desk questions fall. That is the monitor process doing its job: the plan changed because the evidence showed it was not working.

Status reports, meetings and escalation

Most project communication runs through three routines. Getting them right covers a large share of what stakeholders need.

Status reports

A good project status report is short and leads with what the reader must know or do. A reliable structure:

  1. Overall status (RAG) and a one-sentence summary.
  2. Decisions or help needed, with a date by which they are needed.
  3. Progress since last report against milestones, not a list of activities.
  4. Top risks and issues, with owner and next action.
  5. Forecast: are the end date and budget still achievable?
  6. Next period: the main things that will happen.

Meetings

Meetings are the most expensive communication method, so each one needs a clear purpose, an agenda and a record. Use a meeting agenda template and capture decisions and actions with a meeting notes template. On agile teams, the regular events described in our guide to agile meetings and ceremonies replace many traditional status meetings; the daily scrum, for example, is for the developers to inspect progress and adapt their plan, not a report to a manager.

Write your tolerances into the plan so everyone knows when an issue must move up a level. Changes to baselines should follow your change control process, and the decision should be communicated to everyone affected.

How to measure whether communication is working

The monitor process needs evidence, not impressions. Pick a handful of indicators that fit your project and review them at each phase gate or monthly.

IndicatorWhat it tells youHow to collect it
Decision turnaroundWhether decision makers get what they need in timeDays from request to decision in the decision log
Repeated questionsWhether information is clear and easy to findService desk tickets, inbox, FAQ page searches
SurprisesWhether bad news travels earlyCount of issues stakeholders say they heard about late
Attendance and engagementWhether meetings and briefings are valuedAttendance, questions asked, actions completed on time
Stakeholder feedbackWhether people feel informedShort pulse survey: two or three questions each phase
ReadinessWhether end users understand what is changingReadiness survey or quiz before go-live
Tip

Keep the feedback survey short. Two questions work well: "How well informed do you feel about the project?" (1 to 5) and "What one thing would make our communication more useful to you?"

Communication barriers and how to overcome them

BarrierWhat it looks likeHow to reduce it
Information overloadPeople stop reading updates; important messages are missedFewer, shorter messages; put the action in the subject line; use dashboards for detail
Jargon and acronymsBusiness users nod along but do not understandPlain language; a project glossary; test messages on someone outside the team
Distance and time zonesDelays, people left out of decisionsAgreed core hours, written decision records, recorded briefings; see remote project management
Language and cultureSilence mistaken for agreement; directness mistaken for rudenessCheck understanding explicitly; written follow-ups; agree team norms
Tool sprawlDecisions spread across email, chat and documentsAgree one source of truth for status and decisions; define what each channel is for
Unclear ownershipEveryone assumes someone else told the supplierName an owner for each communication in the plan

Tools for project communication

Tools do not fix communication on their own, but good project management software keeps conversations attached to the work and status in one place. Agree what each tool is for before the project starts.

  • Wrike (our top-rated tool) keeps comments, @mentions, approvals and files on each task, offers dashboards across projects and includes AI comment summaries. Agree notification habits early, as reviewers mention notification volume.
  • monday.com gives every item an updates thread with comments, @mentions and files, plus dashboards and guest access, and is quick for teams to pick up.
  • Asana lets project owners publish status updates, and its portfolios give leaders one view of the health of many projects.
  • Microsoft Planner lives inside Teams and Outlook, which suits organisations that already communicate there.

Common mistakes in project communication

  • One message for everyone. Executives need decisions and exceptions; teams need detail. Tailor the same facts for each audience.
  • Reporting activity, not outcomes. "We held six workshops" says little. Say what has changed, what is at risk and what you need.
  • Hiding or softening bad news. Raise problems early, with options and a recommendation. Late bad news damages trust far more than early bad news.
  • No feedback loop. Sending is not communicating. Check understanding and measure whether messages land.
  • Too many channels. Decisions in chat, documents in email and status in a spreadsheet leave everyone hunting. Agree one source of truth.
  • Plan written once and forgotten. Stakeholders change between phases. Review the plan at every phase gate and when key people change.
  • Forgetting the end. At closure, tell people what was delivered, thank contributors and share lessons learned, including lessons about communication.

Frequently asked questions

What is project communication management?

It is the planning, delivery and monitoring of project information so that stakeholders receive what they need, when they need it, in a useful format, and understand it.

What are the three processes of project communication management?

Plan communications management, manage communications and monitor communications. They come from the PMBOK Guide sixth edition and, according to published summaries, also appear in the 8th edition's Stakeholders performance domain.

What is the difference between a communication plan and a communication management plan?

The communication management plan sets the approach and rules: audiences, methods, owners, escalation and storage. The communication plan, or matrix, is the working schedule of who receives what, how and when. Small projects often combine them.

How do you calculate communication channels?

Use n(n - 1) / 2, where n is the number of people. A team of 8 has 8 x 7 / 2 = 28 channels. To find channels added when people join, calculate both totals and subtract.

What is the difference between push and pull communication?

Push communication is sent to specific recipients, such as an email or status report. Pull communication is stored centrally for people to access when they need it, such as an intranet page or dashboard.

What is interactive communication?

Real-time, two-way exchange such as meetings, calls and workshops. It is best for decisions and for complex or sensitive topics, and should be followed by a written record of what was agreed.

How much of a project manager's time is spent communicating?

A large share. The often-quoted figure of 90% could not be traced to a solid study, but most project management work, including meetings, reporting, negotiating and coordinating, is communication.

What should a communication management plan include?

Stakeholder groups and their information needs, the information to be shared, methods, frequency, owners, escalation routes and timescales, templates, where information is stored, constraints such as confidentiality, and how the plan will be reviewed.

How do I write a good project status report?

Keep it short and lead with overall status and any decisions needed. Then cover progress against milestones, top risks and issues, the forecast and what happens next, using the same format every time.

What are common communication barriers in projects?

Information overload, jargon, time zones and distance, language and cultural differences, unclear ownership, too many tools and a culture where bad news is delayed.

How do you monitor project communication?

Track a few indicators, such as decision turnaround, repeated questions, surprises and short survey scores, ask for feedback, and adjust the plan when the evidence shows something is not working.

Where is communications management in the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

There is no separate knowledge area. Published summaries place plan, manage and monitor communications in the Stakeholders performance domain, one of the edition's seven performance domains.

What is the role of the kick-off meeting in communication?

It sets a shared starting point. The kick-off should explain the project purpose, objectives, scope, key milestones, roles and how the team will communicate, so everyone starts with the same understanding.
