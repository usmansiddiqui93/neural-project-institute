Service Level Agreement (SLA): Examples, Template & Metrics

A service level agreement (SLA) is a documented agreement between a service provider and its customer that defines the service, the measurable levels it must meet, how performance is reported and what happens when targets are missed. Projects meet SLAs in two ways: when they buy a service from a supplier, and when they hand a new service over to an operations team. This guide covers what to include, the key metrics, a full illustrative SLA and worked calculations for availability, credits and response clocks.

Key takeaways

  • An SLA turns "good service" into measurable targets, with agreed reporting and remedies when targets are missed.
  • The three common types are customer-based, service-based and multi-level SLAs; internal SLAs between departments follow the same structure.
  • Know the related terms: SLIs are the measurements, SLOs are the targets and OLAs are the internal agreements that support the SLA.
  • Precise definitions (when the clock starts, which hours count, what is excluded) matter more than ambitious numbers.
  • An end-to-end target can never be better than the services it depends on, so check suppliers and OLAs before you promise it.
  • Projects that create a service should agree its SLA during design and make SLA readiness a handover criterion.

What is a service level agreement?

A service level agreement (SLA) is a documented agreement between a service provider and a customer that describes the service to be delivered and the level of service the customer can expect. It sets measurable targets, such as availability or resolution times, explains how they are measured and reported, and states the remedies if they are missed.

That definition follows the one used in ITIL 4, the widely used IT service management framework, which describes an SLA as a documented agreement identifying both the services required and the expected level of service. ITIL places SLAs within the service level management practice, whose purpose is to set clear, business-based targets so that a service can be assessed, monitored and managed against them.

Every SLA answers five questions:

  • What service? What is included, what is excluded and who uses it.
  • How good? The measurable service levels, such as 99.9% availability or a four-hour fix for critical incidents.
  • Measured how? The data source, the measurement period and the business hours that count.
  • Reported how? The report, its frequency and the review meeting.
  • What if targets are missed? Service credits, improvement plans, escalation and, ultimately, termination rights.

SLAs can be external, forming part of a contract with a supplier, or internal, agreed between departments such as IT and finance. External SLAs are usually legally enforceable through the contract they sit in; internal ones are management commitments rather than contracts.

Why SLAs matter in projects

SLAs are often seen as an operations topic, but projects create and buy most new services, so project managers shape the SLAs that operations teams live with for years.

  • When a project buys a service. Hosting, software as a service, support, maintenance and facilities management are bought with SLAs attached. The SLA is part of project procurement management: it defines what you are paying for after go-live.
  • When a project delivers a service. A new system, help desk or shared service needs agreed service levels before operations accepts it. The SLA becomes a set of acceptance criteria for the handover.
  • When teams depend on each other. Internal agreements, such as a procurement team turning purchase requests round in five working days, keep a project's critical activities predictable.

In project terms an SLA is both a procurement tool and a quality standard. It defines what "good" looks like once the service is live, which links it directly to project quality management.

Where SLAs fit in the project lifecycle

Leaving the SLA until the week before go-live is one of the most common causes of difficult handovers. Service levels affect architecture, staffing and cost, so they belong in planning.

Project phaseSLA activity
Initiation and business caseAgree the business need for the service: who relies on it, when and what an outage costs
RequirementsCapture service levels as non-functional project requirements, such as availability, response time and support hours
ProcurementPut supplier SLAs, measurement rules and service credits into the tender and the contract
Design and buildDesign for the target: redundancy, monitoring, backup and support model
TestingRun operational acceptance tests: monitoring, alerting, failover, backup restore and support processes
TransitionAgree the SLA and supporting OLAs, train the support team and run a period of early life support
ClosureConfirm SLA readiness as a handover criterion during project closure and record lessons learned
Tip

Ask early: "What does one hour of downtime cost the business, and when?" The answer tells you whether you need 99% or 99.99%, and the price difference between them can be large.

Types of SLA

Most sources describe three main types. They differ in who the agreement is written for.

TypeWhat it coversExampleBest for
Customer-based SLAAll services provided to one customer or customer groupAn agreement covering email, payroll system and help desk for the finance departmentKey customers with a distinct set of needs
Service-based SLAOne service, on the same terms for every customerA standard IT help desk SLA that applies to all staffStandard services with many users
Multi-level SLALayers of terms: corporate-wide, customer-specific and service-specificCompany-wide security and hours, plus faster response for the trading floorLarge organisations with varied needs

You will also hear about internal SLAs, between departments in the same organisation, and supplier or vendor SLAs, which a provider publishes or negotiates for its product. Cloud providers, for example, usually publish standard service-based SLAs with fixed credit terms.

SLA, SLO, SLI, OLA and related terms

These terms are often mixed up. The distinction matters because each one has a different owner and audience.

TermMeaningExample
SLA (service level agreement)The agreement with the customer, often with consequences99.9% monthly availability, with service credits if missed
SLO (service level objective)A specific target, often stricter than the SLA, used to run the service99.95% availability as an internal target
SLI (service level indicator)The actual measurement used to judge the targetPercentage of successful requests in the month
Error budgetThe amount of failure an SLO allows (100% minus the SLO)0.05% of the month, about 21.6 minutes
OLA (operational level agreement)An agreement between internal teams that supports the SLADatabase team restores a backup within 2 hours
Underpinning contractA contract with an external supplier that supports the SLAHosting provider guarantees 99.95% availability
KPI (key performance indicator)Any important performance measure, not necessarily agreed with a customerCustomer satisfaction score

SLIs, SLOs and error budgets come from site reliability engineering, a practice popularised by Google. Setting the SLO tighter than the SLA gives the team an early warning before a contractual breach.

SLA vs contract, MSA and SOW

In supplier relationships the SLA is usually one schedule within a wider contract. The master services agreement (MSA) sets the legal terms, such as liability, confidentiality and termination. A statement of work (SOW) describes a specific piece of project work with deliverables and dates. The SLA describes ongoing service performance after that work is delivered. A typical managed service has all three: an MSA, an SOW for transition and an SLA for the running service.

What to include in an SLA

These sections cover what most SLAs need. Use them as a checklist when you draft or review one.

SectionWhat it containsWhat good looks like
Parties, term and reviewWho the agreement is between, start date, duration and review dateNamed owners on both sides; review at least annually
Service descriptionWhat the service does, who can use it, what is included and excludedSpecific enough that both sides agree on borderline requests
Service hoursWhen the service is available and when support is providedSeparate the two, e.g. service 24/7, support 08:00 to 18:00 Monday to Friday
Service levelsMetrics, targets, measurement period and data sourceEach target has an SLI and a formula
Priority definitionsWhat counts as priority 1, 2, 3 and 4Defined by business impact and number of users affected
ResponsibilitiesWhat the provider and the customer each must doIncludes customer duties, such as reporting incidents through the agreed channel
Reporting and reviewsReport content, frequency and meetingsMonthly report and service review with agreed attendees
RemediesService credits, improvement plans, escalation and termination triggersCredit tiers with a cap, plus a route for repeated failure
ExclusionsPlanned maintenance, customer-caused faults, events outside the provider's controlMaintenance windows and notice periods stated
Escalation and contactsNames, roles and escalation pathKept current in an appendix
Change processHow the SLA itself is changedChanges go through formal change control

Many SLAs also cover security, data protection, backup and disaster recovery. Two common recovery measures are the recovery time objective (RTO), how quickly a service must be restored after a disaster, and the recovery point objective (RPO), how much data loss is acceptable, expressed as time.

Common SLA metrics

Choose a small set of metrics that reflect what users experience. Five to eight is plenty for most services.

MetricWhat it measuresExample targetWatch out for
Availability (uptime)Percentage of time the service is usable99.9% per calendar monthHow "usable" is defined and what is excluded
Response timeTime from incident logged to acknowledgement and start of workP1 within 15 minutesAn automatic reply should not count as a response
Resolution timeTime from incident logged to fix or workaroundP1 within 4 hoursWhether the clock pauses while waiting for the customer
Target complianceShare of tickets meeting their target95% of P3 tickets resolved within targetA single very late ticket can hide inside a good percentage
Performance (latency)Speed of the service for users95% of page loads under 2 secondsAverages hide slow outliers; use percentiles
First contact resolutionShare of issues resolved at the first contact70% for service desk callsCan encourage closing tickets too quickly
Mean time to restore (MTTR)Average time to restore service after a failureUnder 1 hourAverages can be skewed by one long outage
Customer satisfactionUser rating after a ticket or survey4.2 out of 5Low response rates

Response and resolution measures are close cousins of the flow metrics in Kanban; our guide to cycle time vs lead time explains how to measure elapsed time through a process consistently.

How to write an SLA step by step

Whether you are agreeing an SLA with a supplier or for a service your project is handing over, follow these steps.

  1. Identify the service and its users. Describe what the service does, who uses it and when. Involve the people who will rely on it, using your stakeholder management plan.
  2. Understand the business impact. Ask what an outage or slow response costs, and at which times it hurts most. This sets the level of service worth paying for.
  3. Choose the metrics. Pick measures users care about, such as successful transactions or time to resolve, not only whether a server is switched on.
  4. Define each metric precisely. State the formula, data source, measurement period, service hours and exclusions. Decide when the clock starts, pauses and stops.
  5. Set targets from evidence. Use baseline performance, supplier commitments and business need. A target the service has never achieved is a promise, not a plan.
  6. Check the supply chain. Confirm that OLAs and supplier contracts support every target you promise to the customer.
  7. Agree priorities and responsibilities. Define priority levels by impact, and write down what the customer must do as well as the provider. A RACI matrix helps here.
  8. Agree remedies and escalation. Set service credit tiers, a cap, an improvement-plan trigger and the escalation path.
  9. Set up measurement and reporting before go-live. Monitoring, ticket categories and report templates should be tested during the project, not built afterwards.
  10. Sign off and schedule reviews. Get approval from both sides, publish the SLA where users can find it and set the first review date.

Example SLA for a new service

This example is illustrative. A project is replacing a company's customer portal and handing it to the internal IT operations team, with hosting bought from an external provider.

  • Service: Customer portal for account management and online payments, used by external customers and the customer service team.
  • Service hours: Portal available 24/7. Support 08:00 to 18:00 Monday to Friday, excluding public holidays, with on-call cover for priority 1 incidents outside those hours.
  • Availability: 99.9% per calendar month, measured by synthetic login checks every minute from two locations.
  • Performance: 95% of page loads under 2 seconds during support hours.
  • Planned maintenance: Sunday 02:00 to 04:00 with five working days' notice; excluded from availability.
  • Reporting: Monthly service report by the fifth working day; quarterly service review with the business owner.
PriorityDefinitionResponse targetResolution target
P1 CriticalPortal unavailable or payments failing for all users15 minutes, 24/74 hours
P2 HighMajor function unavailable or many users affected1 business hour8 business hours
P3 MediumSingle user or minor function affected; workaround exists4 business hours3 business days
P4 LowQuestion, request or cosmetic issue1 business day10 business days

Targets like these should reflect business need and what the organisation is willing to pay for, not a template copied from another service.

Worked examples: calculating SLA performance

SLA disputes often come down to arithmetic and definitions. These illustrative calculations show the common cases.

Availability and allowed downtime

Availability = (total minutes - downtime minutes) / total minutes. A 30-day month has 43,200 minutes and a 365-day year has 525,600.

Availability targetAllowed downtime per 30-day monthAllowed downtime per year
99%432 minutes (7.2 hours)87.6 hours
99.5%216 minutes (3.6 hours)43.8 hours
99.9%43.2 minutes8.76 hours
99.95%21.6 minutes4.38 hours
99.99%About 4.3 minutesAbout 53 minutes

Each extra "nine" cuts the allowance by a factor of ten, and usually adds significant cost in redundancy, monitoring and on-call staff.

Availability with exclusions

In a 30-day month the portal had 90 minutes of unplanned outage and 150 minutes of maintenance. If the maintenance happened inside the agreed window, it is excluded: availability = (43,200 - 90) / 43,200 = 99.79%. If the maintenance overran the window or was not notified, it counts: (43,200 - 240) / 43,200 = 99.44%. The same month either meets a 99.5% SLA comfortably or misses it, depending on one definition.

Service credits

Assume a monthly hosting fee of $20,000 and these illustrative credit tiers, capped at 50% of the monthly fee:

Monthly availabilityCredit
99.9% or higherNone
99.5% to below 99.9%10% of the monthly fee
99.0% to below 99.5%25% of the monthly fee
Below 99.0%50% of the monthly fee

At 99.79% the credit is 10%, or $2,000. At 99.44% it is 25%, or $5,000. Notice that the credit is small compared with what a long outage may cost the business. Service credits encourage performance; they rarely compensate for losses, which is why the design of the service matters more than the penalty clause.

The business-hours clock

A P2 incident has an 8 business hour resolution target, and support hours are 08:00 to 18:00 Monday to Friday. An incident logged at 16:30 on Friday uses 1.5 hours on Friday, so the remaining 6.5 hours run from 08:00 on Monday: it is due at 14:30 on Monday. If the SLA lets the clock pause while waiting for the customer, and the team waits two hours for a screenshot on Monday morning, the deadline moves to 16:30.

Compliance is then counted across all tickets. If 226 of 240 P3 incidents in a month were resolved within target, compliance is 94.2%, which misses a 95% target even though most users were well served.

End-to-end availability depends on suppliers

When a service depends on several components in series, and any one failing takes the service down, their availabilities multiply. If the hosting is 99.95%, the database service 99.9% and the network 99.99%, the best end-to-end availability you can expect is 0.9995 x 0.999 x 0.9999 = about 99.84%, assuming failures are independent. That allows roughly 69 minutes of downtime a month, so promising customers 99.9% would be a promise your suppliers cannot support. Fix it with redundancy, better underpinning contracts or a lower customer target.

Error budgets

If the internal SLO is 99.95% per month, the error budget is 0.05%, or 21.6 minutes. If 15 minutes are used by day ten, the team has 6.6 minutes left and might pause risky releases until the month resets. The SLA at 99.9% is still met, which is exactly why the SLO is set tighter.

Monitoring, reporting and service reviews

An SLA without regular reporting is just a document. Agree the reporting rhythm before go-live.

  • Monthly service report: performance against each target, trend over the last few months, major incidents and their root causes, credits due, and actions in progress.
  • Service review meeting: the provider and customer review the report, agree actions, discuss upcoming changes and log issues in an issue log with owners and dates.
  • Annual SLA review: check whether targets still match business need, retire metrics nobody uses and add ones that matter.

Beware the watermelon SLA: green on the outside, red on the inside. Every metric is met but users are unhappy, often because the metrics measure what is easy to count rather than what users experience. ITIL guidance recommends balancing operational metrics with customer satisfaction and feedback. Some organisations add experience measures, sometimes called experience level agreements (XLAs), for the same reason.

For internal services, tools that combine request intake with task tracking make SLAs easier to monitor. Wrike, for example, turns request forms into assigned tasks with statuses and due dates, and its dashboards show overdue work; teams with formal IT service management needs often add a dedicated service desk tool. What happens when an SLA is breached

A missed target is a management event, not just a refund. A sound SLA sets out a proportionate sequence:

  1. Verify the data. Agree the facts, including whether any exclusion applies.
  2. Apply the remedy. Calculate and issue any service credit as the SLA defines it, usually on the next invoice.
  3. Find the root cause. For major incidents, require a written incident review with causes and actions.
  4. Agree an improvement plan. For repeated misses, the provider commits to specific actions and dates.
  5. Escalate. If the plan fails, use the agreed escalation path to senior management on both sides.
  6. Use contractual rights. For persistent or severe breaches, the contract may allow termination or other remedies. Take legal advice before relying on them.

On a project, a supplier breach during early life support is also a project risk. Record it in the risk register and report it in your project status report so the sponsor sees it before handover.

Common SLA mistakes

  • Vague definitions. "Respond promptly" or "high availability" cannot be measured or enforced.
  • Measuring the infrastructure, not the service. A server can be up while users cannot log in.
  • Promising more than suppliers support. End-to-end targets must be backed by OLAs and underpinning contracts.
  • Too many metrics. Thirty targets dilute attention. Focus on the few that matter to users.
  • Targets with no baseline. Measure current performance before committing to improvements.
  • Forgetting customer responsibilities. If the customer must report incidents through the service desk, say so.
  • Relying on credits for protection. Credits are usually capped and small; design resilience into the service instead.
  • Never reviewing the SLA. Business needs change. Review at least annually and capture issues as lessons learned.

SLA readiness checklist for project handover

Before operations accepts a new service from your project, check that:

  • The SLA is agreed and signed by the service owner and the business owner.
  • Supporting OLAs and supplier contracts cover every target.
  • Monitoring measures each SLI and alerts the right team.
  • Ticket categories and priorities match the SLA definitions.
  • The support team is trained, with knowledge articles and an escalation list.
  • The first monthly report has been produced from real data during testing or early life support.
  • Open defects and known errors are documented and accepted.

Frequently asked questions

What is a service level agreement?

A documented agreement between a service provider and a customer that defines the service, the measurable levels it must meet, how performance is reported and what happens if targets are missed.

What should an SLA include?

The parties and term, service description, service hours, measurable service levels, priority definitions, responsibilities, reporting, remedies, exclusions, escalation contacts and how the SLA is changed.

What are the three types of SLA?

Customer-based SLAs cover all services for one customer, service-based SLAs cover one service for all customers, and multi-level SLAs combine corporate, customer and service-level terms.

What is the difference between an SLA and an SLO?

An SLA is the agreement with the customer, often with consequences. An SLO is a specific target, usually stricter, that the provider uses to run the service and stay inside the SLA.

What is the difference between an SLA and an OLA?

An SLA sets commitments to the service's customers. An OLA is an operational level agreement between internal teams that supports those commitments, such as a database team's restore time.

What is the difference between an SLA and a KPI?

An SLA is an agreement that contains targets. A KPI is any key measure of performance, which may or may not form part of an SLA.

How much downtime does a 99.9% availability SLA allow?

About 43 minutes in a 30-day month, or about 8.8 hours over a year, before any agreed exclusions such as planned maintenance.

What is a service credit in an SLA?

A discount or refund the provider gives the customer when agreed service levels are missed, usually a percentage of the monthly fee and capped at a maximum.

What is the difference between response time and resolution time?

Response time is how quickly the provider acknowledges an incident and starts work. Resolution time is how quickly it is fixed or a workaround is in place.

Is an SLA legally binding?

When it forms part of a contract with an external supplier it usually is, through that contract. Internal SLAs between departments are management commitments rather than legal contracts. Take legal advice on contractual terms.

Who writes an SLA?

Usually the provider drafts it, but the customer, service owner and often procurement and legal teams shape the targets, definitions and remedies before both sides sign.

What happens if an SLA is breached?

Typically the data is verified, any service credit is applied, the root cause is investigated and an improvement plan is agreed. Repeated or severe breaches can trigger escalation or contractual remedies.

When should a project define the SLA for a new service?

During requirements and design, because service levels affect architecture, staffing and cost. The SLA should be agreed and tested before handover to operations.
