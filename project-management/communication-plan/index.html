Project Communication Plan: How to Write One (With Examples)

A project communication plan is a short document that sets out who needs information about your project, what they need, why, how and when they will get it, and who is responsible for sending it. This guide shows you what to include, how to write one in eight steps, a column-by-column template and two filled-in examples you can adapt.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 2 October 2026
  • Last updated 4 November 2026
  • 18 min read

Key takeaways

  • A communication plan answers six questions for every audience: who, what, why, how, when and who sends it.
  • Build it from your stakeholder analysis and RACI matrix, grouping people with similar information needs.
  • The core of most plans is a communication matrix: one row per communication item, kept to a page or a sheet.
  • Every plan needs a feedback route and escalation thresholds, so urgent issues do not wait for the next routine report.
  • Assign an owner to every item, not just the project manager, and put recurring items in calendars as soon as the plan is agreed.
  • Review the plan at each phase or whenever stakeholders change; a plan written once and never updated soon stops being followed.

What is a project communication plan?

A project communication plan is a document that lists each regular and planned communication on a project, with its audience, purpose, content, method, frequency and owner, plus how people give feedback and how urgent issues are escalated. It turns good intentions about keeping people informed into a schedule everyone can see and follow.

The project manager usually drafts it during planning, once stakeholders have been identified, and agrees it with the sponsor. On a small project it can be a single table. On a large change programme it is a short document with a matrix, a calendar, escalation rules and key messages.

The plan is one output of project communication management, which also covers delivering communication and checking that it works. This guide is about writing the document itself.

Communication plan vs related documents

Several documents deal with communication, and the names are often used interchangeably. The table shows how they differ.

DocumentWhat it coversRelationship to the communication plan
Communication plan (communication matrix)Who receives what, how, when and from whomThe working schedule this guide explains
Communications management planPMI term for the wider plan: approach, rules, technology, constraints and the matrixA component of the project management plan; the communication plan is usually its core
Stakeholder engagement planHow you will involve and influence each stakeholder groupSets the goals that the communication plan delivers
PRINCE2 communication management approachHow communication with stakeholders inside and outside the project will workThe PRINCE2 equivalent, part of the project initiation documentation
RACI matrixWho is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for each deliverableIts C and I columns are a starting list of audiences

On most projects you do not need all of these as separate documents. One communication plan with a matrix, a few rules and an escalation section usually covers them.

Why a communication plan matters

Communication problems rarely come from a lack of effort. They come from assumptions: the sponsor assumed they would hear about a slipping milestone, the team assumed the sponsor already knew, and the people affected by the change assumed nobody would tell them anything. A written plan replaces those assumptions with agreements. It helps you:

  • Set expectations early, so stakeholders know when and how they will hear from you.
  • Make sure nobody is forgotten, especially end users and people affected by the change.
  • Share the work of communicating, because named owners deliver items, not just the project manager.
  • Get decisions on time, by timing updates just before the decisions they support.
  • Escalate fast, because thresholds and routes are agreed before anything goes wrong.

Signs you need one now: the same questions arrive repeatedly, stakeholders hear news from the grapevine before the project, or meetings keep being called to share information that could have been a report.

How to write a communication plan in 8 steps

This process takes an hour or two on a small project and a short workshop on a large one.

  1. Set communication objectives linked to the project's goals.
  2. Identify and group your audiences from the stakeholder register and RACI matrix.
  3. Define information needs and key messages for each group.
  4. Choose methods, frequency and timing for each communication item.
  5. Assign an owner to every item.
  6. Agree feedback routes and escalation rules.
  7. Review the draft with the sponsor and key stakeholders, then publish it.
  8. Schedule the items and review the plan at each phase or when stakeholders change.

Step 1: Set communication objectives

Start from the project charter and ask what communication must achieve for the project to succeed. Good objectives are specific and observable, for example: "the steering committee receives decision papers at least five working days before each meeting" or "every affected employee knows their move date four weeks in advance". Two to four objectives are enough.

Step 2: Identify and group your audiences

List everyone affected by or able to influence the project, using your stakeholder register. Then group people with similar needs, so the plan has 5-10 rows rather than 40. A power/interest grid from stakeholder management gives a sensible starting approach for each group:

Group on the gridTypical membersCommunication approach
High power, high interestSponsor, steering committee, key client contactsManage closely: regular two-way meetings, early warning, decision papers
High power, low interestSenior executives, finance, legalKeep satisfied: short summaries, exception reports, involve at key decisions
Low power, high interestEnd users, affected staff, team membersKeep informed: regular updates, FAQs, briefings, clear feedback route
Low power, low interestWider organisation, peripheral suppliersMonitor: occasional general updates, information available on request

Check the Consulted and Informed columns of your RACI matrix for anyone missing. People most often forgotten are line managers of affected staff, support teams who will inherit the result, and external regulators or auditors.

Step 3: Define information needs and key messages

For each group, write down what they need to know, what they need to do and what you need from them. Ask them where you can. Useful questions: which decisions do you make and when; how much detail do you want; how do you prefer to receive updates; and what happens if you hear about a problem late? Then draft the two or three key messages each group must understand (see the section on key messages below).

Step 4: Choose methods, frequency and timing

Match the method to the purpose. Use interactive methods (meetings, calls, workshops) for decisions and sensitive news, push methods (reports, emails) for information people must receive, and pull methods (intranet pages, dashboards) for reference material. Our communication management guide explains these methods in detail.

Set frequency from the audience's decision cycle, not from habit. A monthly steering committee needs its report a few days before the meeting, not on the first of the month. Event-driven items, such as briefings before each milestone, are often more useful than fixed intervals.

Step 5: Assign an owner to every item

Name a person or role for each row. The project manager should own steering reports and sponsor meetings, but workstream leads, change leads, internal communications teams and supplier managers are often better placed to deliver other items. An item without an owner will not happen.

Step 6: Agree feedback routes and escalation rules

For every item, decide how recipients respond: questions in the meeting, a shared mailbox, a comments thread or a survey. Then set escalation thresholds and response times, as described in the escalation section below.

Steps 7 and 8: Agree, publish, schedule and review

Walk the sponsor and key stakeholders through the draft; they will often spot a missing audience or an unrealistic frequency. Publish the plan where the team can find it, send calendar invitations for every recurring item the same week, and set review dates at each phase gate.

Escalation rules and thresholds

Routine reports are too slow for urgent problems. The escalation section of your plan states, in advance, which issues skip the normal rhythm, who they go to and how quickly. Agree thresholds with the sponsor and align them with your project governance. The thresholds below are illustrative.

LevelExample triggerEscalate toMethodWithin
1: TeamTask blocked for more than 2 daysProject managerTeam chat or stand-upSame day
2: ProjectMilestone forecast to slip by up to 5 working days, or a new high riskSponsorCall, then written note24 hours
3: GovernanceMilestone slip over 5 days, budget variance over the agreed tolerance, or a safety or legal issueSteering committee via sponsorException report and extraordinary meeting if needed2 working days

Record every escalated item in the issue log with its owner and the decision taken, so the routine reports stay consistent with what was escalated.

Writing key messages

Key messages are the few points each audience must understand and remember. Writing them down keeps everyone who communicates on the project saying the same thing. A simple message map works well:

  1. Write one core message in a single sentence, in the audience's language.
  2. Add three supporting points that explain or justify it.
  3. Add evidence or detail for each point: a date, fact or example.
  4. Note what you want the audience to do next.

Illustrative example for staff in the office relocation:

ElementContent
Core messageYour team is moving together to the new office in May, and your working arrangements stay the same.
Supporting point 1Teams keep their current desk-to-person ratio and sit together.
Supporting point 2The new office has more meeting rooms and quiet space.
Supporting point 3IT equipment is moved and tested for you before your first day.
ActionRead the move page and pack your crate by the Thursday before your move.

Check key messages with the sponsor before use, and update them when facts change. Out-of-date messages repeated by line managers do more damage than no message at all.

Tailoring the plan to your project

The structure of a communication plan stays the same, but the emphasis changes with the project.

Project typeWhat to emphasise
Small internal projectOne-page matrix, a weekly update and a clear escalation line. Avoid over-engineering
Organisational change (relocation, restructure, new system)Affected staff, line manager briefings, key messages, calendars around each go-live, feedback routes. See change management vs change control
Agile product developmentStakeholders outside the team, sprint reviews, roadmap visibility, release notes
Client or agency projectSingle point of contact on each side, approval turnaround times, change request route, what counts as formal instruction
Remote or global teamTime zones, written decisions, recorded meetings, plain language. See remote project management
Regulated or safety-critical projectFormal records, mandatory notifications, approval sign-offs and document control

Putting the plan into action

A plan only works if it becomes part of the project's rhythm. In the first week after approval:

  • Present the plan at the kick-off meeting so everyone knows what to expect and where to find things.
  • Send calendar invitations for every recurring meeting and report deadline.
  • Create the templates the plan names, such as the project status report, and store them in one place.
  • Tell each owner which items they own and when the first one is due.

Revisit the plan at each phase gate, when a stakeholder joins or leaves, after a major change in scope or risk, and whenever you see warning signs such as repeated questions or poor meeting attendance. Plan communication for the end of the project as well: handover, project closure and a lessons learned session.

Tools for building and running a communication plan

A spreadsheet is enough to write the plan. Running it is easier in the tools your team already uses, so updates, comments and decisions sit next to the work:

  • Wrike, our top-rated project management tool, keeps comments and approvals on tasks, offers shareable dashboards on paid plans and includes AI features that summarise long comment threads and help draft status updates.
  • Asana includes status updates, which suit regular sponsor and steering reports.
  • monday.com combines forms, boards and dashboards, useful for collecting questions and showing status at a glance.
  • Microsoft Planner connects each plan to a Microsoft 365 group, with shared files and an optional Teams channel, which suits organisations already on Microsoft 365.

Whichever you choose, keep one source of truth for status and decisions, and link to it from every message.

Common communication plan mistakes

  • Writing it once and never updating it. Stakeholders, risks and phases change; the plan must too.
  • Listing the project manager as owner of everything. Share the work and the plan becomes realistic.
  • Forgetting end users and affected staff. They are often the largest audience and the one that decides whether the change sticks.
  • Too many items. If people receive five updates a week, they read none. Merge or drop items without a clear purpose.
  • Relying on email for important or sensitive messages. Bad news and decisions need a conversation.
  • No feedback route. Communication that only flows one way misses early warnings.
  • Vague escalation. Without thresholds and response times, urgent issues wait for the next monthly report.
  • Timing updates to the calendar, not to decisions. A report that arrives after the meeting is wasted effort.
Tip

Keep the plan to one page or one sheet where you can. If it needs scrolling, it probably contains items nobody will deliver.

Frequently asked questions

What is a communication plan in project management?

A document that defines who receives project information, what they receive, why, how, how often and who provides it, together with feedback routes and escalation rules.

What are the key elements of a communication plan?

Objectives, audiences, information needs, key messages, communication items, method, frequency, owner, feedback route, escalation path, storage conventions and a review schedule.

Who creates the communication plan?

The project manager usually drafts it, with input from the sponsor, team leads, change or internal communications specialists and key stakeholders, and the sponsor agrees it.

When should the communication plan be created?

During planning, once stakeholders have been identified, and before regular reporting starts. Update it throughout the project.

What is a communication matrix?

A table that lists each communication item with its audience, purpose, content, method, frequency, owner and feedback route. It is the core of most communication plans.

How long should a communication plan be?

As short as it can be. For most projects a one-page matrix plus a few rules on storage and escalation is enough; large change programmes add a calendar and key messages.

Is a communication plan the same as a stakeholder engagement plan?

No, but they overlap. The engagement plan sets out how to involve and influence stakeholders; the communication plan sets out the information flows that support it.

What is the difference between a communication plan and a communications management plan?

In PMI terms the communications management plan is the wider document, covering approach, rules and technology, and it is part of the project management plan. The communication plan or matrix is its working schedule. On most projects one document does both jobs.

Do Agile projects need a communication plan?

Agile events such as the daily Scrum and sprint review are built-in communication, so the plan is lighter. It is still useful for sponsors, users and dependent teams outside the core team.

How often should a communication plan be reviewed?

At each phase gate or major milestone, and whenever stakeholders, risks or priorities change significantly.

What should a communication plan include for escalation?

Clear thresholds, who to escalate to at each level, the method and expected response times, so urgent issues do not wait for the next routine report.

What should you do if stakeholders do not follow the plan?

Ask why. Usually the frequency, format or channel does not suit them. Agree a change with them, update the plan and, if it affects decisions, involve the sponsor.

What tools can I use to create a communication plan?

A spreadsheet or our free communication plan template is enough to write it. Project management tools such as Wrike, Asana, monday.com or Microsoft Planner help you run it.
