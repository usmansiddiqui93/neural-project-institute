Resource Allocation in Project Management: Steps and Examples

Resource allocation is the process of assigning the right people, equipment and materials to specific project tasks for specific periods. Good allocation makes sure each task has the skills and time it needs without overloading anyone.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 30 September 2026
  • 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Resource allocation assigns named resources to tasks for specific periods.
  • Compare demand with real availability, not with a full working week.
  • Staff critical-path and scarce-skill tasks first.
  • Resolve over-allocation before publishing and review the plan weekly.

What resource allocation covers

Allocation sits between identifying project resources and tracking their use. It answers three questions for every task: who or what will do it, how much of their time it needs, and when.

  • People: named team members, specialists and contractors.
  • Equipment: machinery, test devices, vehicles, environments.
  • Materials and facilities: components, rooms, labs.
  • Budget: the money that pays for each of the above; see project budget.

How to allocate resources step by step

  1. Break the work down into tasks using a work breakdown structure.
  2. Estimate the effort and skills each task needs; see effort estimation.
  3. List available resources with their skills, cost rates and availability (a resource calendar).
  4. Match resources to tasks, starting with tasks on the critical path and those needing scarce skills.
  5. Check the load on each person per week and look for over-allocation.
  6. Resolve conflicts by moving non-critical work, using float, or applying resource leveling.
  7. Publish the allocation, get agreement from resource managers and review it at least weekly.

Worked example: one week of allocation

A small team has two developers and one tester. Ana works full time on the project (40 hours). Ben is shared with another project and gives 20 hours. Chloe the tester gives 40 hours. The week's planned work is below.

TaskSkillEffort (hours)First allocation
Build APIDeveloper50Ana 40 h, Ben 10 h
Build user interfaceDeveloper30Ben 20 h, 10 h unassigned
Write and run testsTester30Chloe 30 h

Developer demand is 80 hours against 60 hours of developer capacity, so 20 hours are short. Tester demand is 30 of 40 hours, leaving 10 spare. The project manager has four realistic options:

  • Delay part of the user interface work to next week if it has float.
  • Ask Ben's other project for 10 extra hours this week (and accept that 10 hours are still short).
  • Move simple UI tasks to Chloe if she has the skills, using her spare 10 hours.
  • Bring in a contractor for 20 hours, which adds cost but protects the date.

Choosing to delay 10 hours of non-critical UI work and move 10 hours of simple UI checks to Chloe brings every person to 100% or below without changing the end date.

Allocation methods compared

MethodHow it worksBest for
Skills-firstAssign the person with the best-matching skillsSpecialist or high-risk tasks
Availability-firstAssign whoever is free soonestRoutine tasks with many capable people
Critical-path-firstStaff critical tasks before othersDate-driven projects
Cost-firstUse the cheapest capable resourceFixed-budget projects
Pull (Agile)Team members pull the next priority itemSelf-organising teams; see agile capacity planning

Common mistakes

  • Allocating people at 100% of their working week and ignoring meetings, admin and support work.
  • Planning with generic roles ("a developer") instead of named people, which hides real clashes.
  • Forgetting holidays, part-time patterns and commitments to other projects.
  • Allocating the same expert to several critical tasks at once.
  • Never updating the plan once work starts.
Tip

Plan most people at around 70-80% of their hours for project work. The remainder absorbs meetings, email and the unexpected, which keeps your plan honest.

Frequently asked questions

What is resource allocation in project management?

It is assigning specific people, equipment and materials to project tasks for defined periods so the work can be done on time and within budget.

Why is resource allocation important?

Without it, some people are overloaded while others sit idle, tasks wait for missing skills and the schedule and budget slip.

What is the difference between resource allocation and resource leveling?

Allocation assigns resources to tasks; resource leveling adjusts the schedule afterwards to remove any over-allocation that results.

What is over-allocation in resource allocation?

A resource is over-allocated when the work assigned to it in a period exceeds its available hours, for example 50 hours of tasks in a 40-hour week.

How do you allocate resources effectively?

Estimate effort, list available skills and hours, staff critical and scarce-skill tasks first, check each person's load and resolve conflicts before publishing the plan.

What is a resource calendar in resource allocation?

A calendar showing when each resource is available, including working hours, holidays, shifts and commitments elsewhere.

How do you handle resource allocation for shared resources across projects?

Agree allocations with resource or line managers, record them in a shared view and use capacity planning to see total demand across projects.

Should resource allocation put people at 100%?

Rarely. Most people spend part of every week on meetings, email and support, so 70-80% project allocation is a more realistic planning figure.

What is resource allocation in Agile?

Agile teams usually stay stable and allocate work rather than people: the team pulls prioritised items up to its capacity each sprint or iteration.

Which tools help with resource allocation?

Most modern project management tools include workload or resource views; spreadsheets suit very small teams.

Who is responsible for resource allocation?

The project manager plans it, but in matrix organisations functional or resource managers must agree to release the people concerned.
