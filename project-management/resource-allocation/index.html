Resource Allocation in Project Management: Steps & Example

Resource allocation in project management is the process of assigning the right people, equipment and materials to specific tasks for specific periods, based on what each task needs and what each resource really has available. Done well, it gives every task the skills and time it needs without overloading anyone. This guide walks through the steps, the maths of availability and utilisation, and a full worked example.

Key takeaways

  • Resource allocation decides who or what does each task, how much of their time it takes and when.
  • Allocate against real availability: subtract leave, other commitments and non-project time first.
  • Staff critical-path tasks and scarce skills first, then fill in the rest.
  • Check each person's load per week; anything over 100% of available time is over-allocation to resolve before you baseline.
  • Shared people need agreements with their line or resource managers, not just a name in the plan.
  • Review allocations weekly and re-plan when scope, priorities or people change.

What is resource allocation?

Resource allocation is the assignment of available resources, mainly people but also equipment, materials and facilities, to the tasks in a project schedule for defined periods. For each task it answers three questions: who or what will do the work, how much of their time it needs, and when. The aim is to deliver the plan without overloading or idling anyone.

Allocation sits in the middle of resource management. Before it, you identify the project resources you need and estimate the effort for each task. After it, you resolve clashes with resource leveling, approve the project baseline and then track actual use against the plan.

PMI treats this as a core part of project management. The PMBOK Guide's sixth edition placed it in the Project Resource Management knowledge area, with processes including Estimate Activity Resources, Acquire Resources and Control Resources. The eighth edition, published in November 2025, lists resources as one of its seven performance domains. Our PMBOK Guide overview explains how the editions differ.

Why resource allocation matters

Most project plans fail on people, not on logic. A schedule can be perfectly sequenced and still be impossible if the same specialist is needed in three places at once. Careful allocation gives you:

  • A realistic schedule. Dates reflect when people can actually do the work, not just when the task logic allows it.
  • Fewer bottlenecks. Clashes for scarce skills show up in planning, when they are cheap to fix.
  • Controlled cost. Knowing who works on what, at what rate, lets you build an accurate project budget.
  • Sustainable workloads. People are not routinely asked for 50-hour weeks, which protects quality and reduces burnout.
  • Better use of skills. Experts spend their time on work that needs them, and others get development opportunities.
  • Earlier warning. When something changes, you can see straight away who and what is affected.

Without it, the usual symptoms are familiar: some people overloaded while others wait, tasks stalled for a missing skill, overtime that nobody planned and a schedule that slips week by week.

Types of resources you allocate

People are usually the hardest resource to allocate, but most projects also need to schedule other resources. Each type has its own constraints.

Resource typeExamplesWhat to plan for
People (human resources)Team members, specialists, contractors, client staffSkills, availability, part-time patterns, leave, other projects
EquipmentMachinery, test devices, vehicles, servers, environmentsBooking slots, maintenance windows, set-up and transport time
MaterialsComponents, concrete, licences, consumablesLead times, delivery dates, storage, minimum order quantities
FacilitiesMeeting rooms, labs, workshops, sitesCapacity, booking rules, access and security
BudgetFunding for labour, purchases and contingencyWhen money is released, approval limits, cash flow

For the rest of this guide we focus on people, because that is where most allocation decisions are made. The same steps apply to equipment and facilities, with a booking calendar in place of a person's availability.

Resource allocation vs related terms

Several resource terms are used loosely and often interchangeably. The table separates them.

TermWhat it meansTypical horizon
Resource planningWorking out what resources a project or portfolio will need, and how they will be obtainedMonths to years
Capacity planningComparing total demand with the total supply of people or skills across teamsQuarters to a year
Resource allocationAssigning named resources to specific tasks for specific periodsWeeks to months
Project schedulingSetting task dates from logic, durations and resourcesWhole project
Resource levelingMoving task dates to remove over-allocation; may delay the finishWhole project
Resource smoothingMoving tasks only within float to even out demand; finish date fixedWhole project
Resource utilisationA measure of how much of a person's available time is allocated or usedWeekly or monthly

In short: planning and capacity planning ask do we have enough?, allocation asks who does what, when?, and leveling asks how do we fix the clashes?

Who is responsible for resource allocation?

The project manager plans and maintains the allocation for their project. But in most organisations they do not own the people, so allocation is a negotiation as much as a calculation.

RoleResponsibility
Project managerDefines resource needs, builds the allocation, resolves clashes within the project, tracks use
Resource or functional managerOwns the people, agrees who is released to the project and for how much time
PMO or portfolio managerSets priorities between projects and sees demand across the portfolio
SponsorApproves extra budget, contractors or changes in priority when the project cannot be staffed
Team membersGive honest estimates and flag clashes with other work early

In a matrix organisation, where people report to a line manager but work on projects, the allocation is only real once the line manager has agreed it. Record who agreed what, and when; a RACI matrix helps make those responsibilities explicit. Where many projects share the same people, a project management office usually runs a regular resource meeting to settle priorities.

How to allocate resources step by step

The process below works for most projects. On small projects you may do it in one sitting; on larger ones it is repeated each stage.

  1. Confirm the scope and deliverables. You cannot allocate resources to work that is not defined. Start from the approved scope and the work breakdown structure.
  2. Estimate effort and skills per task. Record the hours or days each task needs and the skill required. Effort is not the same as duration; see effort estimation.
  3. Build the resource pool. List the people who could do the work, with their skills, cost rates and the share of their time available to your project.
  4. Calculate real availability. Subtract leave, public holidays, other project commitments and routine non-project time from each person's contracted hours (see the next section).
  5. Allocate critical and scarce work first. Staff tasks on the critical path and tasks that need rare skills before anything else. A skills matrix shows who can do what.
  6. Fill in the remaining tasks. Assign the rest by skill, availability and cost, using development opportunities where risk allows.
  7. Check the load per person per period. Add up allocated hours for each person, week by week, and compare them with availability. Anything over 100% is over-allocation.
  8. Resolve conflicts. Use float to move non-critical work, reassign tasks, split work, add temporary capacity or, if necessary, move dates through resource leveling.
  9. Agree, baseline and monitor. Confirm allocations with resource managers, publish them, include them in the baseline and review actual against planned at least weekly.

What a resource allocation plan contains

The output of these steps is a resource allocation plan. It does not need to be a long document; a well-structured spreadsheet or tool view is enough if it contains:

  • Each task or work package with its effort estimate and required skill.
  • The named person or resource assigned, and the hours or percentage allocated per week.
  • Each person's availability and resulting utilisation per period.
  • Cost rates and the total cost per resource, feeding the budget.
  • Assumptions, such as part-time patterns or a contractor still to be hired, recorded in the RAID log.
  • Who agreed each allocation, and when it was last reviewed.

How to calculate availability and utilisation

Two simple calculations sit at the heart of resource allocation: how much time a person really has, and how much of it you have allocated.

Available hours

Available hours = contracted hours x share of time given to the project - leave and holidays - routine non-project time

Routine non-project time covers team meetings, email, admin, training and support work that happens every week regardless of the project. Many planners handle it with a focus factor: the percentage of the remaining time that can realistically be spent on planned project tasks. Planning at about 70 to 80% is common practice and our own recommendation; it is a planning assumption, not a standard, so adjust it to what your timesheets show.

PersonContracted hours/weekShare given to projectFocus factorPlanning capacity/week
Ana (developer)40100%80%32 hours
Ben (developer)4050%80%16 hours
Chloe (tester)40100%80%32 hours
Priya (designer)4050%80%16 hours

Leave comes off on top. If Chloe is on leave for a whole week, her planning capacity that week is zero, not 32 hours. Converting capacity into full-time equivalents (FTE) is useful when you compare demand across teams.

Utilisation

Utilisation = allocated hours / available hours x 100

If Ana is allocated 40 hours in a week where her planning capacity is 32, her utilisation is 40 / 32 x 100 = 125%: she is over-allocated by 8 hours. Below 100% means spare capacity; exactly 100% means fully loaded against your planning capacity, which already includes the focus-factor buffer.

Tip

Track utilisation against planning capacity, not contracted hours. A person at 100% of planning capacity still has time for meetings and email. A person at 100% of contracted hours does not, and will slip.

Worked example: allocating a four-week website project

This fictional example uses the four people above to deliver a small website relaunch over four weeks. The tasks and their effort estimates are:

  • Spare capacity: Ana has 16 hours free in week 1, Chloe 16 hours in week 2, and Priya 8 hours in week 2 and 16 in weeks 3 and 4.

Every person is now at or below 100% of planning capacity in every week, the go-live date is unchanged and the only extra cost is 8 contractor hours. At an example rate of £50 an hour, that is £400, which the project manager takes to the sponsor for approval from contingency.

Two things are worth noting. First, Ben runs at 100% for three weeks with no slack, so he is the person to watch; any overrun on his tasks will hit the dates. Second, the low utilisation in week 4 for Ana and Ben is deliberate: it is the buffer for bug fixing, which is hard to estimate. If the dates had not been fixed, delaying part of the testing into a fifth week would have been the cheaper option.

Resource allocation techniques

Priority rules for who gets assigned

When several people could do a task, a consistent rule makes decisions faster and easier to explain.

RuleHow it worksBest for
Skills-firstAssign the person with the best-matching skillsSpecialist, high-risk or customer-facing tasks
Availability-firstAssign whoever is free soonestRoutine tasks with many capable people
Critical-path-firstStaff critical tasks before any othersDate-driven projects
Cost-firstUse the lowest-cost capable resourceFixed-budget projects
DevelopmentPair a less experienced person with an expertBuilding capability and reducing single points of failure
PullTeam members take the next priority item when they are freeAgile and Kanban teams

Scheduling techniques that shape allocation

  • Critical path method (CPM): identifies which tasks drive the finish date so you can staff them first. See critical path method.
  • Critical chain: schedules around the most constrained resources and protects the plan with shared buffers rather than padding each task; see critical chain project management.
  • Resource leveling: moves tasks to remove over-allocation, even if the finish date moves.
  • Resource smoothing: moves tasks only within float, keeping the finish date fixed.
  • Fast-tracking and crashing: overlap tasks or add resources to recover time; both affect allocation and cost. See fast-tracking vs crashing.

In practice most project managers combine them: allocate critical work first, smooth within float, and level only when there is no other way.

Allocating resources across multiple projects

Allocation gets harder when people work on several projects at once. A resource allocation matrix, a grid of people against projects showing the percentage of time committed to each, gives everyone the same picture.

PersonWebsite relaunchMobile appSupport and business as usualTotal
Ben50%30%20%100%
Priya50%40%10%100%
Omar0%70%40%110%

Omar is over-committed before any task is scheduled, so no amount of project-level planning will fix his weeks; the resource managers must change the split. Some practical rules for shared people:

  • Agree priorities at portfolio level. When two projects need the same person, the PMO or portfolio board decides, not whoever asks loudest.
  • Limit the number of projects per person. Every switch between projects costs focus time; fewer, larger allocations usually work better than many small ones.
  • Protect scarce specialists. Track people whose skills are needed on many projects and plan their time first.
  • Book time in blocks. Two full days a week on a project is usually more productive than 40% of every day.
  • Review demand monthly. Use capacity planning to see future demand before it becomes a clash.

Our guide to managing multiple projects covers prioritisation and scheduling across a portfolio in more detail.

Resource allocation in Agile teams

Agile teams usually turn the problem around. Instead of allocating people to tasks, organisations keep stable, cross-functional teams and allocate work to teams. The team then decides who does what during the sprint or iteration.

  • Capacity is planned per team per sprint, based on availability and past velocity.
  • In sprint planning, the team pulls the highest-priority items it believes it can complete.
  • Individuals pick up tasks as they free up, which avoids detailed person-by-person schedules.
  • Allocation decisions still happen above the team: which teams work on which products, and whether specialists are shared.

People-level allocation still matters when specialists are shared across teams or when people split time between product work and support.

Common resource allocation mistakes

MistakeWhat happensFix
Allocating people at 100% of contracted hoursNo time for meetings, email or the unexpected; tasks slipPlan against a focus factor of around 70-80%
Planning with generic roles onlyReal clashes between named people stay hiddenUse placeholders early, then replace them with names
Ignoring leave and part-time patternsTasks land on people who are not thereKeep a resource calendar for every person
Putting one expert on several critical tasks at onceOne delay hits the whole scheduleSpread critical work or train a backup
Treating effort as durationA 40-hour task is planned as one week for someone available 16 hoursConvert effort to duration using each person's availability
No agreement from line managersPeople are pulled back to other workGet allocations confirmed and recorded
Allocating once and never updatingThe plan drifts from reality within weeksReview actual against planned weekly
Ignoring non-people resourcesWork waits for equipment, environments or roomsBook key equipment and facilities alongside people

The most common root cause is optimism: assuming people have more time than they do. Using recent timesheet data to set your focus factor is the simplest correction.

Resource allocation tools

A spreadsheet works well for a single small team: one row per task, one column per week, and a total per person with conditional formatting to highlight anything over capacity. Pair it with a skills matrix template to see who can do what.

Once several projects share people, dedicated software saves a lot of time. Look for:

  • Workload or capacity views that show each person's load per week, with over-allocation highlighted.
  • Allocation by hours or percentage, not just by assignee.
  • Leave, holidays and part-time calendars per person.
  • Cross-project views for resource managers and the PMO.
  • Placeholders or job roles for people not yet hired or named.
  • Time tracking, so you can compare planned and actual hours.

Wrike, ranked first in our project management software rankings, offers workload charts that show who is over- or under-allocated, with effort allocation, job roles and capacity planning on its higher tiers; it suits PMOs and services teams managing many projects. Asana, monday.com and ClickUp include workload views on their paid plans, and Microsoft Project and Smartsheet suit teams that prefer scheduling-led or spreadsheet-style planning.

Frequently asked questions

What is resource allocation in project management?

It is assigning specific people, equipment and materials to project tasks for defined periods, based on what each task needs and what each resource has available, so the work can be delivered on time and within budget.

What are the steps in resource allocation?

Confirm scope, estimate effort and skills per task, list available resources, calculate real availability, staff critical and scarce-skill tasks first, fill in the rest, check each person's load, resolve conflicts, then agree, baseline and monitor.

What is an example of resource allocation?

A project manager assigns a developer 32 hours a week to build page templates, a part-time developer 16 hours a week to CMS work and a tester to testing in weeks 3 and 4, then adjusts when the tester's leave creates a gap.

Why is resource allocation important?

It makes the schedule realistic, exposes clashes early, keeps costs under control and stops people being overloaded while others wait for work.

Who is responsible for resource allocation?

The project manager plans it, but resource or functional managers must agree to release their people, and a PMO often sets priorities when projects compete for the same staff.

What is the difference between resource allocation and resource leveling?

Allocation assigns resources to tasks. Resource leveling then adjusts task dates to remove any over-allocation that results, which can move the finish date.

What is the difference between resource allocation and capacity planning?

Capacity planning compares overall demand with supply across teams over months. Allocation assigns named people to specific tasks within that capacity.

What is over-allocation?

A resource is over-allocated when the work assigned in a period exceeds its available time, for example 40 hours of tasks against 32 hours of planning capacity, a utilisation of 125%.

How do you calculate resource utilisation?

Divide allocated (or actual) hours by available hours and multiply by 100. For example, 28 allocated hours against 32 available is 87.5% utilisation.

Should you allocate people at 100%?

Not of their contracted hours. Allocating against a planning capacity of around 70-80% of working time leaves room for meetings, email and support work; that figure is a common rule of thumb, so check it against your own timesheets.

What is a resource allocation matrix?

A grid of people against projects or tasks showing how much of each person's time is committed to each. It quickly shows who is over-committed across projects.

How do you allocate resources across multiple projects?

Agree priorities at portfolio level, keep a shared view of each person's commitments, limit the number of projects per person, protect scarce specialists and review demand regularly.

How does resource allocation work in Agile?

Agile organisations usually keep stable teams and allocate work to teams rather than people to tasks. Each team plans its sprint capacity and pulls the highest-priority work.

Which tools help with resource allocation?

Spreadsheets suit small teams. Tools with workload and capacity views, such as Wrike, Asana, monday.com, ClickUp, Smartsheet and Microsoft Project, help when people are shared across projects; see our project management tools rankings.
