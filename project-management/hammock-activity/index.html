Hammock Activity: Definition, Example and How to Use It

A hammock activity is a schedule activity whose start and finish are tied to other activities, so its duration is calculated from them rather than estimated. It stretches or shrinks as the work it spans moves, which makes it ideal for time-based support such as supervision, equipment hire or project management. This guide shows how the duration is worked out, a costed example, how hammocks differ from summary tasks and level-of-effort activities, and how to build one in common tools.

Key takeaways

  • A hammock activity spans a group of activities and takes its dates from them: start from the first, finish from the last.
  • Its duration is calculated, never estimated: latest linked finish minus earliest linked start.
  • Use hammocks for time-dependent support, costs and reporting spans, such as supervision, hire and overheads.
  • A hammock should follow the network, not drive it, and should not appear on the critical path.
  • Primavera P6 has a Level of Effort activity type for this; Microsoft Project needs a workaround.
  • Spread resources evenly across the hammock so costs are not front-loaded.

What is a hammock activity?

A hammock activity (or hammock task) is a summary-type schedule activity that spans a group of other activities. It has no duration of its own. Instead, it starts when the first activity it is linked to starts and finishes when the last one finishes, so its duration is calculated automatically and changes whenever those activities move.

The name comes from the image of a hammock slung between two trees. Move either tree and the hammock stretches or shortens, but it never holds the trees in place. In the same way, a hammock activity hangs between two points in the schedule and follows them.

You may also see it called a summary duration task or frame activity. Older editions of the PMBOK Guide grouped hammock activities with summary activities in their glossary, and many scheduling tools implement the idea as a level-of-effort (LOE) activity. The terms overlap, and the differences are explained in the comparison further down.

Hammocks belong to the detailed side of project scheduling. You build them once the activity network is in place, because they depend on its dates.

How a hammock activity works

A hammock activity has four defining features:

  • It is linked, not estimated. Its start is tied to the start of one activity (typically with a start-to-start link) and its finish to the finish of another (typically finish-to-finish). See task dependencies for the link types.
  • It can span any activities. Unlike a summary task, it is not limited to children in one branch of the work breakdown structure. It can begin in one phase and end in another.
  • It follows the network. When the activities in between slip or speed up, the hammock lengthens or shortens. It should never push other work.
  • It often carries resources and costs. Anything that is paid for by time, such as a supervisor, a crane or a site cabin, can be assigned to the hammock so its cost updates with the schedule.

The duration formula

Using the day 0 convention from the forward and backward pass:

  • Hammock start = the early start of the first linked activity.
  • Hammock finish = the early finish of the last linked activity.
  • Hammock duration = hammock finish − hammock start, in working days on the hammock's calendar.

If the hammock spans several activities that could start or finish first, use the earliest start and the latest finish among them. Some tools let you link several predecessors and successors to a hammock and do this for you.

What happens when the schedule changes

Because the hammock reads its dates from other activities, it reacts to every change between them:

Change in the scheduleEffect on the hammock
An activity it spans slips but stays within its floatNo change, because the first start and last finish do not move
A critical activity it spans slipsThe hammock lengthens by the same amount
Critical work is crashed or fast trackedThe hammock shortens
The first linked activity starts lateThe hammock starts late; its length may also change
Work after the hammock's end activity changesNo change, because it is outside the span

This is exactly what you want for time-based costs, and it is why hammocks are popular on construction, engineering and government schedules.

Hammock activity example: an office fit-out

A contractor is fitting out an office floor. The six construction activities, with durations in working days, form this network:

IDActivityDurationPredecessorEarly startEarly finishTotal float
AStrip out4-040
BFirst-fix electrics5A491
CPartitioning6A4100
DSecond-fix electrics3B, C10130
EDecorating4D13170
FSnagging and handover2E17190

The critical path is A-C-D-E-F at 19 working days. B has 1 day of float. (If you want to check the numbers, our critical path method guide explains the calculation.)

Step 1: add the hammocks

The contractor has two costs that depend on time rather than on any single activity:

  • H1 Site supervision, at $450 a day, needed from the start of strip-out until handover. It is linked start-to-start from A and finish-to-finish from F.
  • H2 Skip and welfare unit hire, at $60 a day, needed from the start of strip-out until second-fix electrics are complete, when the noisy, dirty work ends. It is linked start-to-start from A and finish-to-finish from D.

Step 2: calculate the durations and costs

  • H1 starts at A's early start (0) and finishes at F's early finish (19), so it lasts 19 days and costs 19 × $450 = $8,550.
  • H2 starts at 0 and finishes at D's early finish (13), so it lasts 13 days and costs 13 × $60 = $780.

Step 3: see what happens when the schedule moves

ScenarioProject finishH1 duration and costH2 duration and costTotal hammock cost
PlanDay 1919 days, $8,55013 days, $780$9,330
B slips 1 day (within float)Day 1919 days, $8,55013 days, $780$9,330
C slips 3 days (critical)Day 2222 days, $9,90016 days, $960$10,860
E crashed by 1 dayDay 1818 days, $8,10013 days, $780$8,880

What the example shows

  • Float protects the hammock. B's one-day slip is absorbed by its float, so neither hammock changes.
  • Critical slips cost money twice. A three-day slip in partitioning delays the finish and adds $1,530 of support costs, which would be easy to miss if supervision and hire were entered as fixed-duration tasks.
  • Each hammock only sees its own span. Crashing the decorating shortens H1 by a day but leaves H2 untouched, because H2 ends at D.
  • The cost of time is visible. When you evaluate schedule compression, the hammock costs saved by finishing earlier are part of the business case. Here, crashing E is worth up to $450 a day before it costs more than it saves.
Tip

Never make a hammock a predecessor of other real work. If H1 drove F and F drove H1, the tool would either report circular logic or produce dates that make no sense. Hammocks should hang from the network, not hold it up.

Hammock activity vs summary task vs level of effort

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they are not quite the same thing.

Hammock activitySummary taskLevel-of-effort (LOE) activityMilestone
What it isAn activity whose dates come from linked activitiesA parent row that rolls up its child tasksOngoing support work with no specific outputA point in time
How dates are setBy dependency links to chosen activitiesBy the outline (WBS) hierarchyBy the work it supports, often via hammock linksBy its predecessors or a constraint
Can span different WBS branchesYesNo, only its own childrenYesNot applicable
DurationCalculatedCalculated from childrenCalculated, or sometimes fixedZero
Holds resources and costsYes, commonlyUsually avoidedYes, that is its main purposeNo
Typical exampleCrane hire from erection to dismantlingPhase 2: Fit-outProject management, QA, site securityPractical completion

In practice: level of effort describes the *kind of work* (support that lasts as long as the work it supports), while a hammock describes the *scheduling technique* (taking dates from other activities). Hammocks are the usual way to schedule LOE work, which is why tools such as Primavera P6 implement hammocks as a Level of Effort activity type.

A summary task follows the WBS outline. Oracle's P6 documentation describes a separate WBS Summary activity type whose dates run from the earliest start to the latest finish of activities sharing a WBS code, which is the summary-task idea rather than a hammock. If your support cost spans several branches of the WBS, a hammock is the better fit. Our guide to project tasks and activities covers the other activity types, and project milestones covers zero-duration events.

When to use a hammock activity

Use a hammock whenever something lasts exactly as long as a stretch of the schedule and you want it to update itself.

UseExampleIndustry
Supervision and managementSite manager from mobilisation to handoverConstruction
Equipment or facility hireTower crane from erection to dismantlingConstruction, engineering
Quality and safety coverQA inspector during the build and test phasesEngineering, manufacturing
Project management effortPM and PMO support from kick-off to closureAny
Environment and licence costsTest environment from build start to user acceptance sign-offIT and software
Reporting spans"Phase 2 delivery" spanning activities from several work packagesProgrammes and government projects
Event supportSecurity and venue staff from load-in to load-outEvents

Hammocks are also useful in large programme schedules, such as an integrated master plan and its schedule, where managers want summary bars that cut across the WBS without restructuring it.

When not to use one

  • When the work produces a deliverable. Real work with an output should be an ordinary activity with its own estimate and links.
  • To represent buffer or float. A hammock that spans a gap is not contingency. Plan buffers explicitly.
  • To force dates. If you want an activity to start on a set date, use a constraint, sparingly, not a hammock.
  • When a summary task already does the job. If the support only covers one branch of the WBS, a summary row may be simpler.
  • On very small projects. If nothing costs money by time, a hammock adds complexity without benefit.

How to create a hammock activity step by step

The method is the same in any tool; only the menus differ.

  1. Build and check the network first. Every real activity needs its durations and predecessors and successors before you add hammocks.
  2. Decide what the hammock represents and which activity marks its start and which marks its finish. Write this in the activity name or notes, for example "Site supervision (hammock: A start to F finish)".
  3. Create the activity with no fixed duration, or as a Level of Effort type if your tool has one.
  4. Link the start to the first activity, usually start-to-start with no lag.
  5. Link the finish to the last activity, usually finish-to-finish with no lag.
  6. Assign resources or costs for the whole period, spread evenly (or on a curve) across the duration.
  7. Test it. Change a critical duration upstream and check that the hammock and its cost move. Change a non-critical duration within its float and check that they do not.

Keep hammocks together, for example under a "Support and overheads" heading, so they are easy to find and exclude from critical path reports.

Hammock activities in Primavera P6

Oracle's Primavera P6 offers five activity types: Task Dependent, Resource Dependent, Level of Effort, Start or Finish Milestone and WBS Summary. According to Oracle's P6 help, a Level of Effort activity has a duration determined by its dependent activities and is typically used for ongoing work such as project management, clerical work or change management. This is P6's hammock.

  1. Add the activity and set its Activity Type to Level of Effort on the General tab.
  2. Add relationships to the first and last activities it should span. A common convention, described by Primavera trainers, is a start-to-start relationship with the first activity and a finish-to-finish relationship with the last.
  3. Schedule the project and check that the LOE runs from the start of the first activity to the finish of the last.
  4. Assign resources or expenses. If you add a resource, apply a resource curve so the units spread across the duration rather than bunching at the start.

Follow your organisation's linking convention and check that the LOE is not driving any real work. If you only need a roll-up for one WBS element, the WBS Summary type may be the better choice.

Hammock tasks in Microsoft Project

Microsoft Project does not have a hammock task type, but a long-standing workaround, described in Microsoft's TechNet wiki, uses linked cells:

  1. Create the task that will act as the hammock.
  2. Select the Start cell of the task that should drive the hammock's start and copy it.
  3. Select the hammock task's Start cell and choose Task > Paste > Paste Special > Paste Link.
  4. Copy the Finish cell of the task that should drive the hammock's finish.
  5. Select the hammock task's Finish cell and paste link it in the same way.

The hammock's dates now update when the source tasks move. Be aware of the caveats the wiki lists:

  • The links use older DDE technology and can be fragile. Only the hammock shows a small indicator; the source tasks show nothing.
  • To remove a hammock, break the links first rather than just deleting the task, to avoid leaving orphaned link fragments.
  • Name the task clearly, for example "Provide management support (hammock)", and record the source tasks in its notes.

Because of these limits, many Microsoft Project users keep hammocks to a handful of important support costs, or use a summary task where the support covers one branch of the outline.

Hammocks in spreadsheets and other tools

If your tool has no hammock or level-of-effort type, you can still get the same result:

  • Spreadsheet formula. Set the hammock's start to the start of the first linked row and its finish to the finish of the last linked row, or use MIN() of the start dates and MAX() of the finish dates across the range it spans. Duration is then finish minus start (or NETWORKDAYS() for working days).
  • Parent or summary rows. Many project management tools calculate a parent row's dates from its children. Group the activities under a parent and assign the cost there, accepting that it only covers that group.
  • Cost columns. Multiply the calculated duration by the daily rate so the support cost updates when dates change.

Our free project schedule template has columns for durations and predecessors, and the project cost estimate template is a good place to hold the daily rates. If you show the result on a Gantt chart, give hammocks a distinct colour so readers do not mistake them for real work.

Hammock activities and the critical path

A hammock spans critical activities, so its total float may look like zero, but it is not itself critical. Nothing depends on it, and shortening it is impossible without shortening the activities it hangs from.

The US Government Accountability Office's Schedule Assessment Guide is clear on the point: it states that LOE activities should not drive the schedule, and warns that if LOE is critical, management has no indication of which activities can slip or which would respond to extra resources. In practice that means:

  • Exclude hammocks and LOE activities from critical path reports and filters.
  • If a hammock appears on the critical path, check for a link where it acts as a predecessor of real work and remove it.
  • Do not count hammock float when you look at float and slack for risk; look at the real activities instead.
  • Review hammocks after major re-planning, because new activities may fall outside the span they were meant to cover.

Hammock activities, costs and earned value

Hammocks matter most to cost control, because many project overheads are paid by time.

  • Budgeting. When you build the project budget, price time-based costs on hammocks so the baseline cost matches the baseline schedule.
  • Forecasting. When the schedule slips, the hammock lengthens and the forecast cost rises automatically, which gives early warning through cost control.
  • Earned value. In earned value management, level-of-effort work is normally measured with the LOE method: value is earned as time passes, so earned value equals planned value and LOE shows no schedule variance. It can still show a cost variance if you spend more per day than planned.

Because LOE earns value with time, a schedule with a large share of its budget on hammocks can make overall performance look better than it is. Keep LOE budgets proportionate and report them separately from discrete work.

Common hammock activity mistakes

  • Typing a fixed duration. A hammock with a hard-coded duration is just an ordinary task and will not update.
  • Making the hammock a predecessor of real work. This can create circular logic or let support activities drive the finish date.
  • Linking to the wrong activities. A hammock tied to a non-critical activity at its end may stop early while the work continues.
  • Front-loaded resources. In some tools, resources on an LOE bunch at the start unless you apply a curve or even spread.
  • Using hammocks to hide gaps or buffers. Contingency should be visible and managed, not buried in a support bar.
  • Too many hammocks. A schedule full of hammocks is harder to read and to audit. Keep them for genuine time-based support and reporting.
  • Fragile links in Microsoft Project. Deleting a task with paste links, without breaking them first, can leave orphaned link fragments.

Hammock activity for the PMP exam

Hammock activities appear occasionally in PMP certification study material as part of schedule management terminology. The points worth remembering are:

  • A hammock is a summary-type activity whose duration is set by the activities it spans.
  • It is linked from the start of one activity to the finish of another.
  • It is commonly used for level-of-effort and support work.
  • It does not drive other activities and is not part of the critical path.

Exam questions are more likely to test the critical path, float and dependency types than hammocks themselves, so make sure those foundations are solid.

Frequently asked questions

What is a hammock activity in project management?

A hammock activity is a summary-type schedule activity that spans a group of activities and takes its dates from them. It starts when the first linked activity starts and finishes when the last linked activity finishes, so its duration changes automatically when they move.

Why is it called a hammock activity?

Because it hangs between two points in the schedule, like a hammock between two trees, and stretches or shrinks as those points move.

How is the duration of a hammock activity calculated?

Duration equals the finish of the last linked activity minus the start of the first linked activity, in working days on the hammock's calendar. If it spans several possible start or end activities, use the earliest start and the latest finish.

What is an example of a hammock activity?

Site supervision on a construction project, linked from the start of the first site activity to the finish of handover. If any critical work slips, the supervision activity, and its cost, lengthens automatically.

Is a hammock activity on the critical path?

No. It spans critical activities but does not drive any work, so it should be excluded from the critical path. The GAO Schedule Assessment Guide states that LOE activities should not drive the schedule.

What is the difference between a hammock activity and a summary task?

A summary task groups its child tasks in the WBS outline. A hammock is linked by dependencies and can span activities from different branches of the WBS, and it commonly carries resources and costs.

Is a hammock activity the same as level of effort?

Not exactly. Level of effort is support work with no specific output, such as project management. A hammock is the scheduling technique of taking dates from other activities. Hammocks are the usual way to schedule LOE work, and Primavera P6 implements hammocks as a Level of Effort activity type.

How do I create a hammock activity?

Create an activity with no fixed duration, link its start to the start of the first activity it should span and its finish to the finish of the last, then assign the time-based resources or costs. Test it by changing an upstream critical duration.

Can a hammock activity have resources?

Yes. Assigning time-based resources and costs, such as supervisors, equipment hire or management time, is one of the main reasons to use a hammock. Spread them evenly or on a curve across its duration.

Does Microsoft Project have hammock tasks?

Not as a built-in type. You can create one by copying the start date of one task and the finish date of another and using Paste Special > Paste Link into the hammock's Start and Finish fields, though these links can be fragile.

How do you create a hammock in Primavera P6?

Set the activity type to Level of Effort and add relationships to the first and last activities it should span, commonly start-to-start with the first and finish-to-finish with the last. Apply a resource curve if it carries resources.

Should a hammock activity drive other activities?

No. A hammock should follow the network and never act as a predecessor of real work, otherwise you risk circular logic and a misleading critical path.

How does a hammock activity help with cost control?

Time-based costs update automatically when the schedule moves, so a slip shows up straight away as a higher forecast cost. See cost control.

How does earned value treat hammock or LOE activities?

LOE work normally earns value as time passes, so earned value equals planned value and it shows no schedule variance, though it can still show a cost variance. See earned value management.
