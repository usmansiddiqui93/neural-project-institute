PMBOK Guide 8th Edition: Principles, Domains and Processes

The PMBOK Guide (A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge) is the Project Management Institute's standard and guide to good practice in project management, and its current edition is the eighth, published in November 2025. It combines six principles, seven performance domains and 40 processes grouped into five focus areas. This guide explains the full structure, what changed from the 6th and 7th editions, and how the book relates to the PMP exam.

Key takeaways

  • The PMBOK Guide 8th edition (November 2025) is the current edition; it replaces the 7th edition published in 2021.
  • It has six principles, seven performance domains (governance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources, risk) and five focus areas containing 40 processes.
  • The 8th edition brings back process guidance that the 7th removed, but presents it as non-prescriptive guidance you tailor, not steps you must follow.
  • New appendices cover project management offices, artificial intelligence and procurement.
  • The PMP exam is built on PMI's July 2026 exam content outline, not on the PMBOK Guide alone; use the book as a reference alongside it.
  • PMBOK is a standard, not a methodology: pair it with a way of working such as predictive, Agile, hybrid or PRINCE2.

What is the PMBOK Guide?

The PMBOK Guide is the Project Management Institute's foundational publication on project management. It sets out the principles, performance domains, processes and vocabulary that PMI regards as good practice for most projects, most of the time. It is a standard and reference, not a step-by-step methodology, so you tailor it to each project.

PMBOK stands for Project Management Body of Knowledge. The body of knowledge itself is the whole collection of practice in the profession; the Guide is PMI's summary of the generally recognised part of it. The book is published by PMI, the Project Management Institute, and it is the best-known reference behind PMI's certifications.

Since the 7th edition, one volume contains two documents:

  • The Standard for Project Management, the formal standard: what a project is, how projects create value, the six principles and project life cycles.
  • The PMBOK Guide itself: the seven performance domains and their processes, tailoring, and reference chapters on inputs, outputs, tools and techniques, plus appendices.

What it is not: it is not a method that tells you which meeting to hold on which day. Methods such as PRINCE2, Scrum or your organisation's own lifecycle supply that detail. Think of the PMBOK Guide as the map of what a well-run project must cover, and the method as the route you choose.

PMI describes the update as refreshing key terms and concepts, consolidating the principles, reintroducing the old process groups as focus areas and bringing technical "how to" guidance back into the performance domains. In short, it keeps the principle-based thinking of the 7th edition and restores much of the practical structure that readers of the 6th edition missed.

Inside The Standard for Project Management

The Standard is the shorter, more formal half of the book. Its four chapters set the foundations that the rest of the Guide builds on.

ChapterWhat it covers
1. IntroductionPurpose of the Standard, key terms, characteristics of a project, how organisational and project governance connect, and how portfolios, programmes, projects and operations relate
2. A System for Value DeliveryHow projects create value, how to assess project success, the project environment (enterprise environmental factors, organisational process assets, structures), product management and project roles
3. Project Management PrinciplesThe project management mindset and the six principles, each with its impact, the principle in action and the performance domains it connects to
4. Project Life CyclesPhases, predictive, adaptive and hybrid development approaches, how to choose one, delivery cadence, and the five focus areas

Two ideas from the Standard run through everything else. Projects exist to deliver value, judged by benefits as well as time and cost. And the development approach is a choice: predictive, as in waterfall project management, adaptive, as in Agile project management, or a hybrid.

The six project management principles

The 7th edition had twelve principles. The 8th consolidates them into six, which describe the mindset behind good project management rather than the mechanics. The titles below are the chapter headings in the Standard; some summaries shorten them (for example "embed quality" or "lead accountably").

PrincipleWhat it meansWhat it looks like on a project
Adopt a holistic viewSee the project as a system of interacting parts inside a wider organisationChecking how a scope change affects cost, risk, people and other projects before approving it
Focus on valueJudge work by the benefits it delivers, not only by outputsRe-testing the business case at each phase gate and stopping work that no longer pays back
Embed quality into processes and deliverablesBuild quality into how work is done, not just inspect it at the endAgreeing acceptance criteria before work starts and reviewing early drafts
Be an accountable leaderTake ownership of outcomes and lead with integrity, whatever your formal roleOwning a missed milestone openly and setting out the recovery plan
Integrate sustainability within all project areasWeigh environmental, social and economic effects in decisionsIncluding energy use, supplier practices or long-term running costs in option appraisal
Build an empowered cultureCreate a team and stakeholder environment where people can collaborate and decideDelegating decisions to the people closest to the work, with clear boundaries

Sustainability is the most visible new emphasis; the rest will feel familiar from the 7th edition. Our guide to project management principles explains the wider set of principles in plain English.

The seven performance domains

A performance domain is a group of related activities that are critical to delivering project outcomes. In the 8th edition the domains are practical subject areas again, much closer to the old knowledge areas than the 7th edition's more abstract domains. Each domain chapter covers key concepts, its processes, tailoring considerations, interactions with other domains and how to check results.

DomainWhat it coversProcessesClosest 6th-edition knowledge areas
GovernanceOversight, decision-making, integrated planning, sourcing strategy, quality assurance, knowledge, change and closure9Integration; parts of quality and procurement
ScopeRequirements, scope definition, the scope structure (such as a WBS), validation and control6Scope
SchedulePlanning, developing and controlling the schedule, whichever approach you use3Schedule
FinanceCost estimates, budget, funding, reserves and financial control4Cost
StakeholdersIdentifying and engaging stakeholders, and planning and managing communications7Stakeholder; communications
ResourcesPeople and physical resources: planning, estimating, acquiring, leading the team and control5Resource
RiskThreats and opportunities: planning, identification, analysis, responses and monitoring6Risk

Notice what has gone as a separate heading: communications now sits inside stakeholders, quality is both a principle and a governance process, and procurement is a governance process plus a full appendix. Integration has become governance, which is broader: it covers how decisions are made and who makes them, not only how plans fit together. The five focus areas and 40 processes

What the focus areas are

The focus areas reuse the names of the old process groups: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. They describe the type of management attention a project needs at a given moment. They are not phases: on a multi-phase project you initiate, plan, execute, monitor and close within every phase, and monitoring and controlling runs throughout. On an adaptive project the same cycle repeats inside each iteration.

Planning holds almost half of the processes, as it did in the 6th edition, which reflects the number of subjects that need thinking through, not the share of time planning should take. Compared with the 6th edition's 49 processes, several have been merged: schedule work now has three processes instead of six, and qualitative and quantitative risk analysis have become one Perform Risk Analysis process. Our guide to project management processes shows the classic 49-process matrix if you need to compare the two.

Tip

Treat the 40 processes as a checklist of decisions, not a list of documents. A two-month internal project might cover Plan Risk Management, Identify Risks and Plan Risk Responses in one hour-long workshop and a risk register template.

Tailoring, inputs and outputs, tools and techniques

Three further chapters turn the domains into something you can use.

  • Tailoring (chapter 3) explains why to tailor, what to tailor (life cycle and development approach, processes, and engagement) and a four-step tailoring process: select the initial development approach, tailor for the organisation, tailor for the project, and implement ongoing improvement. It also covers tailoring each performance domain and diagnostics for spotting where tailoring is needed.
  • Inputs and outputs (chapter 4) describes the documents and artefacts that processes use and produce, such as the charter, plans, registers and logs.
  • Tools and techniques (chapter 5) describes the methods used to carry out the processes, from estimating and analysis techniques to meetings and data representation.

This is a middle path between the 6th edition, which attached inputs, tools and techniques and outputs (ITTOs) to every process, and the 7th, which replaced them with a looser section on models, methods and artefacts.

The PMO appendix reflects a shift PMI describes from PMOs that enforce process to PMOs that act as customer-focused partners delivering value. If you run or are setting up one, read it with our guide to the project management office (PMO). The procurement appendix restores much of the contract material that was a full knowledge area in the 6th edition; our guide to project procurement management covers the same ground step by step.

What changed: 6th vs 7th vs 8th edition

Many study guides, courses and job adverts still refer to older editions, so it helps to know how they line up.

6th edition (2017)7th edition (2021)8th edition (November 2025)
Organised aroundProcessesPrinciples and outcomesPrinciples, domains and non-prescriptive processes
PrinciplesNone as a list126
Main structure10 knowledge areas, 5 process groups8 performance domains7 performance domains, 5 focus areas
Processes49, each with ITTOsRemoved from the guide (moved to Process Groups: A Practice Guide, 2022)40, as guidance
ApproachesMainly predictive, with Agile considerationsPredictive, adaptive and hybridPredictive, adaptive and hybrid
Notable additionsAgile content per knowledge area; Agile Practice Guide published alongsideValue delivery system, tailoring, models, methods and artefactsSustainability principle, governance and finance domains, appendices on PMOs, AI and procurement

From the 7th to the 8th edition

  • Fewer principles: twelve became six, with sustainability added as a principle of its own.
  • Different domains: the 7th edition's domains (stakeholders, team, development approach and life cycle, planning, project work, delivery, measurement, uncertainty) gave way to seven subject-based domains.
  • Processes are back: 40 processes within five focus areas, after the 7th edition removed process detail from the guide.
  • More practical content: domain chapters include processes, tailoring and checks, and the reference chapters restore inputs, outputs, tools and techniques.
  • New topics: PMOs, artificial intelligence and procurement get dedicated appendices.

PMBOK Guide editions history

The body of knowledge grew out of a 1983 PMI report on ethics, standards and accreditation, and PMI dates the body of knowledge itself to 1987. The first edition of the Guide followed in 1996.

YearEditionWhat changed

20002nd editionUpdated to reflect growth in practice
20043rd editionInclusion standard moved from "generally accepted" to "generally recognised as good practice"
20084th editionRefinements to processes and terminology

PMI also publishes companion titles. The most relevant are *Process Groups: A Practice Guide* (2022), which preserved the 49-process model after the 7th edition, and the *Agile Practice Guide*. Our guide to what PMI is lists PMI's other standards.

The PMBOK Guide and the PMP exam

The most common question about the PMBOK Guide is whether you need it to pass the PMP certification. The short answer: it is a key reference, but it is not the syllabus. PMI builds the exam from its Examination Content Outline (ECO), and the current outline took effect in July 2026.

PMBOK Guide 8th editionPMP exam (from July 2026)
What it isA standard and guide to good practiceA test of how you would act in realistic situations
Structure6 principles, 7 domains, 5 focus areas, 40 processes3 domains: People 33%, Process 41%, Business Environment 26%
ApproachesPredictive, adaptive and hybridPredictive, Agile and hybrid scenarios across all domains
Role in studyBackground, vocabulary and conceptsDefines what you are tested on

The 8th edition fits the new exam well: its governance, finance and sustainability material overlaps with the much larger Business Environment domain, and its stakeholder and resources domains support People questions. But the exam rewards judgement, so reading the Guide cover to cover is far less useful than practising scenario questions.

How to study the PMBOK Guide for the PMP

  1. Download the current PMP Examination Content Outline from pmi.org and read it first.
  2. Check eligibility: 35 contact hours or an active CAPM, plus three, four or five years of experience within the last 10 years depending on your education (two with a GAC-accredited degree).
  3. Book training from a PMI Authorized Training Partner if you need live hours: from 1 December 2026 only approved providers count. See our guide to 35 contact hours for the PMP.
  4. Read the Standard's principles and life cycle chapters and the seven domain chapters; skim the reference chapters and appendices.
  5. Map each ECO task to the domains and processes that support it, and note any gaps your course covers.
  6. Spend most of your time on full-length, timed practice exams built on the 2026 outline.

If you are earlier in your career, the CAPM certification tests foundational knowledge and is a common first step.

Worked example: applying PMBOK 8 to a project

This illustrative example shows how the principles, domains and focus areas work together on one project. The organisation and figures are invented.

A regional insurer is replacing its customer relationship management (CRM) system. The project has a budget of £300,000, six months to go-live and about 85 users in sales and customer service. The project manager chooses a hybrid approach: a predictive plan for the contract, data migration and cut-over, and two-week iterations for configuring screens and workflows with users.

Step 1: apply the principles

  • Holistic view: the CRM touches billing and the call-centre telephony, so both teams join the design reviews.
  • Value: success is defined as cutting average call handling time and giving sales a single customer view, not just "system live by 30 June".
  • Quality: acceptance criteria for each workflow are agreed before configuration starts.
  • Sustainability: the option appraisal compares hosting energy use and the supplier's support commitments alongside price.

Step 2: work through the domains

DomainKey questionWhat the project manager did
GovernanceWho decides, and how do changes get approved?Signed project charter, steering group every four weeks, change threshold of £10,000 or two weeks
ScopeWhat exactly is in and out?Requirements workshops, scope statement, WBS for migration and cut-over, product backlog for configuration
ScheduleWhen will it be done?Milestone plan for the predictive work, iteration calendar for configuration
FinanceWhat will it cost and how is it funded?Budget with a 10% contingency reserve held by the sponsor
StakeholdersWho is affected and how will we engage them?Stakeholder map, fortnightly demos, communications plan for go-live
ResourcesWho and what do we need?Two super-users seconded from each department, supplier consultants for eight weeks
RiskWhat could go wrong or better?Risk register; top threat is poor data quality, so a trial migration is scheduled in month two

The result covers every domain without 40 documents. Work management platforms such as Wrike can hold the predictive milestones and the iteration boards in one place, with dependencies and dashboards for the steering group.

How to use the PMBOK Guide step by step

Whether or not you are studying for an exam, the Guide is most useful as a checklist and shared vocabulary. A practical routine:

  1. Choose the development approach using the Standard's life cycle chapter: predictive, adaptive or hybrid.
  2. Check what is mandatory in your organisation: governance gates, PMO standards, contract or regulatory requirements.
  3. Walk the seven domains and write down, for each, the key decisions this project needs.
  4. Pick the processes from the 40 that each decision needs, and decide how formal each one should be.
  5. Record your tailoring in the project management plan so the sponsor and team know what to expect.
  6. Use the principles as a test at each phase gate: are we still delivering value, leading accountably and building quality in?
  7. Review and adjust the process set when risk, size or stakeholders change.

PMBOK, PRINCE2 and Agile

The PMBOK Guide is often compared with methods, but it does a different job. PRINCE2 is a process-based method that tells you how to direct and manage a project in stages; Scrum is an Agile framework for delivering in short iterations; the PMBOK Guide describes what a well-run project must address, whatever method you use. Many organisations use PRINCE2 or an Agile framework as the method and the PMBOK Guide as the wider body of knowledge. See our guide to PMBOK vs PRINCE2 for a detailed comparison.

How to get the PMBOK Guide

PMI members can download a free digital copy of the PMBOK Guide and PMI's other standards as a membership benefit, and PMIstandards+ offers searchable online access. Non-members can buy print or digital copies from PMI's store and major booksellers, and translations are available in several languages. Check that you are buying the eighth edition: listings for the 6th and 7th editions are still common.

Common mistakes with the PMBOK Guide

  • Studying from an old edition only. The 6th edition's 49 processes and the 7th edition's domains are both out of date. Use the 8th edition with materials built on the 2026 exam outline.
  • Treating it as a methodology. It tells you what to cover, not how to run your Monday stand-up. You still need to choose and tailor an approach.
  • Running every process. The 40 processes are options. Using all of them on a small project wastes time and frustrates the team.
  • Confusing focus areas with phases. Initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing repeat within each phase or iteration.
  • Memorising instead of applying. Exams and real projects both reward judgement. Practise using the domains on a real or illustrative project.

Frequently asked questions

What does PMBOK stand for?

Project Management Body of Knowledge. The full title of PMI's book is A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK Guide).

What is the latest edition of the PMBOK Guide?

The eighth edition, published by PMI in November 2025. It replaced the seventh edition from 2021.

What are the six principles in PMBOK 8?

Adopt a holistic view; focus on value; embed quality into processes and deliverables; be an accountable leader; integrate sustainability within all project areas; and build an empowered culture.

What are the seven performance domains in the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

Governance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources and risk.

How many processes are in the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

40, presented as non-prescriptive guidance and grouped into five focus areas: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. Planning holds 19 of them.

Are the process groups back in PMBOK 8?

In effect, yes. The five process groups return as five focus areas with the same names, after the 7th edition removed process detail from the guide.

What happened to the ten knowledge areas?

They were replaced by seven performance domains. Most map directly; integration became governance, communications moved into stakeholders, cost became finance, and quality and procurement are covered by governance processes, a principle and an appendix.

Is PMBOK a methodology?

No. It is a standard and guide to good practice. Methodologies such as PRINCE2 or Scrum describe how to do the work; the PMBOK Guide describes what good practice covers.

Is the PMP exam based on the PMBOK Guide 8th edition?

The exam is based on PMI's Examination Content Outline, updated in July 2026. The PMBOK Guide is one of several references, so study it alongside the outline and scenario-based practice questions.

Is the PMBOK Guide free?

PMI members get a free digital copy as a membership benefit. Non-members usually buy it from PMI or a bookseller.

Does the PMBOK Guide cover Agile?

Yes. The 8th edition covers predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches and includes tailoring guidance for each. PMI also publishes a separate Agile Practice Guide.

What is the difference between PMBOK and PMP?

PMBOK is the body of knowledge and PMI's guide to it; the PMP is a certification awarded by PMI to people who meet the experience and training requirements and pass the exam.

Is the PMBOK Guide used outside the US?

Yes. It is used worldwide, particularly in the Americas, the Middle East and Asia. In the UK it is often used alongside PRINCE2 or the APM Body of Knowledge.
