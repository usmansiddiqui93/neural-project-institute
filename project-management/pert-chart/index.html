PERT Chart: Formula, Steps and Worked Example

A PERT chart is a network diagram that maps a project's activities and dependencies using the Program Evaluation and Review Technique, in which each activity gets three duration estimates (optimistic, most likely and pessimistic). From those you calculate an expected duration, find the critical path and estimate the probability of finishing by a given date. This guide gives the formulas, the steps and a complete worked example.

Key takeaways

  • PERT expected duration = (O + 4M + P) / 6; standard deviation = (P - O) / 6; variance = SD squared.
  • Draw the network, run a forward and backward pass on the expected durations, and the zero-float chain is the critical path.
  • Add variances, not standard deviations, along the critical path, then use z = (deadline - expected finish) / path SD for the probability.
  • Classic PERT ignores near-critical paths, so it tends to be optimistic where paths merge; check important dates with Monte Carlo simulation.
  • Use PERT for new or uncertain work and to set schedule contingency; single-point estimates are fine for routine tasks.

What is a PERT chart?

A PERT chart is a network diagram of a project's activities, the dependencies between them and their estimated durations, built using the Program Evaluation and Review Technique. Each activity has three estimates, which PERT combines into an expected duration and a measure of uncertainty, so the chart shows the likely finish date and how confident you can be in it.

The term is used in two slightly different ways. The PERT chart is the picture: boxes or circles joined by arrows. The PERT estimate or PERT analysis is the arithmetic behind it. Most practical uses combine both. See our guide to the project network diagram for the general layout.

The original PERT used activity on arrow, with numbered events. Today most people draw activity on node because it is easier to read and to build in software. The worked example below shows the same network both ways.

How to read a PERT chart node

On an activity-on-node PERT chart, each box usually carries six numbers around the activity name. The top row shows the early start, expected duration and early finish; the bottom row shows the late start, total float and late finish. Some charts add the standard deviation under the duration. Read the chart from left to right: arrows show which activities must finish first, the boxes with zero float form the critical path (often drawn in red or bold), and the last box's early finish is the expected project duration.

A short history of PERT

PERT was developed in 1958 for the US Navy's Polaris submarine missile programme by the Navy's Special Projects Office, working with Lockheed and the consultancy Booz Allen Hamilton. It was first called Program Evaluation Research Task and was renamed by 1959. The aim was to coordinate thousands of contractors on a programme where nobody could estimate durations precisely.

At about the same time, DuPont developed the critical path method (CPM) for plant maintenance and construction. CPM used a single duration per activity and focused on time-cost trade-offs; PERT focused on uncertainty. The two ideas merged so thoroughly that many people now say "PERT/CPM" and use the terms almost interchangeably.

The PERT formulas

MeasureFormulaWhat it tells you
Expected duration (E)(O + 4M + P) / 6The duration to plan with
Standard deviation (SD)(P - O) / 6How uncertain one activity is
VarianceSD squaredUncertainty in a form you can add up
Path expected durationSum of E along the pathThe expected length of a path
Path SDSquare root of the sum of variances along the pathHow uncertain the whole path is
z-score(Deadline - path expected duration) / path SDLook up z in a normal table to get the probability

Quick example. A design task is estimated at O = 4 days, M = 6 days and P = 14 days. E = (4 + 24 + 14) / 6 = 42 / 6 = 7 days, and SD = (14 - 4) / 6 = 1.67 days. The expected duration is longer than the most likely value because the pessimistic tail is long, which is exactly the effect PERT is designed to capture.

Why the most likely value is weighted by 4

PERT assumes each activity's duration follows a beta distribution, which can be skewed, unlike a normal curve. The (O + 4M + P) / 6 formula is a simple approximation of that distribution's mean, and (P - O) / 6 assumes the range covers roughly six standard deviations. Both are approximations, not laws. A simpler alternative is the triangular average, (O + M + P) / 3, which gives more weight to the extremes; our guides to cost estimation and effort estimation compare the two.

How to get good three-point estimates

PERT is only as good as its inputs. Three-point estimates go wrong in predictable ways, so plan how you collect them.

  • Ask the people who will do the work, ideally two or three of them, and discuss differences rather than averaging them away.
  • Ask for the extremes first. Asking "what is the worst realistic case?" before "what is most likely?" reduces anchoring on a single number.
  • Define the pessimistic case clearly. It should cover things that plausibly go wrong (rework, a key person off sick, a slow supplier), not disasters, which belong in the risk register.
  • Use history where you have it. Actual durations from similar past activities are better than memory; expert judgment fills the gaps.
  • Record assumptions with each estimate so you can revisit them when conditions change.
  • Challenge narrow ranges. If O, M and P are almost identical on new work, the team may be underestimating uncertainty.

How to make a PERT chart step by step

  1. List the activities. Start from your work breakdown structure and break work packages into activities small enough to estimate.
  2. Identify dependencies. For each activity, record which activities must finish before it can start.
  3. Collect three estimates. Ask the people doing the work for O, M and P. Ask for the pessimistic case explicitly; do not just pad the most likely figure.
  4. Calculate E, SD and variance for every activity.
  5. Draw the network. Use boxes and arrows (AON) or events and arrows (AOA), working left to right from start to finish.
  6. Run the forward and backward pass using E as the duration to find early and late dates and float. Our forward and backward pass guide shows the working.
  7. Mark the critical path: the chain of activities with zero total float.
  8. Calculate the probability of meeting the deadline from the critical path's variance.
  9. Check near-critical paths and, for important commitments, run a simulation.
  10. Set contingency and baseline the schedule, then update estimates as work progresses.

Read our guide to Monte Carlo simulation before step 9 if you have not run one before.

Probability of meeting a deadline

Suppose the sponsor wants the portal live within 40 working days. PERT treats the critical path's total duration as roughly normally distributed, so:

  1. Path expected duration = 37 days.
  2. Path variance = 1.78 + 1.00 + 2.78 + 2.78 + 1.78 + 0.11 = 10.22, so path SD = square root of 10.22 = 3.20 days.
  3. z = (40 - 37) / 3.20 = 0.94.
  4. From a normal table, z = 0.94 gives a probability of about 83%.

You can also work backwards from the confidence the sponsor wants: deadline = expected duration + z x path SD. Using z = 0.84 for 80%, 1.28 for 90% and 1.645 for 95% gives about 39.7, 41.1 and 42.3 days.

Tip

Never add standard deviations along a path. Add the variances, then take the square root. Adding SDs here would give 7.33 days instead of 3.20 and make the project look far riskier than PERT predicts.

Merge bias: why PERT can be optimistic

Classic PERT looks only at the critical path. But F cannot start until C, D and E have all finished. If the back end (E) runs late while the front end (D) runs to time, the back-end path becomes critical. Because that path is only one day shorter, this happens often. The effect is called merge bias, and it always pushes the real finish later than PERT predicts.

To show the size of the effect, we ran a Monte Carlo simulation of this illustrative network: 200,000 runs, each drawing every activity's duration from a beta-PERT distribution built from the same O, M and P.

In the simulation, the back end rather than the front end set the start of integration testing in about 38% of runs. The lesson is not that PERT is wrong, but that it is a first approximation. When near-critical paths merge, treat PERT's probabilities as an upper bound, and simulate before you make a firm commitment.

What-if: reducing back-end uncertainty

Suppose the team runs a short technical spike on the riskiest integration in week one and re-estimates the back end (E) at O = 10, M = 14 and P = 18 days. E falls to 14 days and its float rises to 3. Classic PERT shows no change at all, because E is not on the critical path. The simulation tells a different story:

Measure (simulation)Before the spikeAfter the spike
Mean finish37.8 days37.1 days
Chance of finishing within 40 days76%81%
90% confidence date41.9 days41.4 days

Reducing uncertainty on a near-critical path can be worth as much as shortening the critical path itself, and only a method that looks beyond the single critical path will show it. A sensitivity analysis helps you find which activities to work on first.

Turning PERT results into a schedule commitment

The point of PERT is a date you can defend. Using the example:

  • Planning target: 37 days, the expected duration, for the team's working schedule.
  • Commitment to the sponsor: the sponsor asked for 90% confidence. The simulation gives 41.9 days, so the project manager commits to 42 working days.
  • Schedule contingency: the 5-day gap between 37 and 42 is held as a single buffer at the end of the project, owned by the project manager, rather than spread as padding across activities.
  • Risk focus: E and C have the largest variances. Splitting the back-end build or starting infrastructure earlier reduces uncertainty more than squeezing the most likely estimates.

This end-of-project buffer is the same idea that critical chain project management formalises. Record the buffer and its triggers in your contingency plan, and if the committed date is still too late, see schedule compression for fast tracking and crashing options.

Gives a probability for a deadline, not just a single dateAssumes activity durations are independent, which is rarely fully true
Shows dependencies and the critical path clearlyIgnores near-critical paths, so it is optimistic where paths merge

Helps justify schedule contingency to sponsorsEstimates are only as good as the people giving them

When to use a PERT chart

  • Use it for new, one-off or research-heavy work where durations are genuinely uncertain: software launches, R&D, first-of-a-kind engineering, complex events.
  • Use it when a sponsor asks how confident you are in a date, or when you need to size a buffer.
  • Use it selectively on larger projects: three-point estimates for the critical and near-critical activities, single estimates for routine work.
  • Skip it for small, familiar projects where the team has done the work many times; a simple project schedule is enough.

In Agile teams, full PERT networks are rare because work is planned in short iterations. Three-point thinking still helps for release forecasting alongside story point estimation.

PERT charts in project management software

Most mainstream work management tools do not have dedicated PERT fields, but they handle the network, dependencies and critical path, and you can add the three estimates yourself.

  • Wrike (our top-rated tool) supports all four dependency types with lead and lag, the critical path, float and baselines in its Gantt chart. Add custom fields for O, M and P, then use E as the task duration.
  • Smartsheet is spreadsheet-based, so a formula column can calculate E and SD next to each task, with dependencies and the critical path in the Gantt view.
  • ClickUp offers Gantt charts with dependencies, the critical path, slack and baselines on paid plans.
  • Microsoft Planner premium plans cover scheduled projects with dependencies, and Plan 3 adds baselines and the critical path.

For learning or one-off analysis, a spreadsheet is often the quickest option. Our free critical path template handles durations, predecessors, early and late dates and float, and you can add O, M and P columns in front of the duration. For a calendar view, use the project schedule template.

Common PERT mistakes

  • Padding the most likely estimate. M should be the realistic central case; the pessimistic estimate is where the bad news belongs.
  • Adding standard deviations. Add variances along a path, then take the square root.
  • Ignoring near-critical paths. A path a day or two shorter can drive the finish; check it or simulate.
  • Treating the expected duration as a promise. E is roughly a 50% date. Commit to a higher confidence date and hold the difference as contingency.
  • Using very wide ranges without asking why. If P is many times O, the activity is poorly understood. Break it down or investigate before relying on any number.
  • Never updating the chart. Re-estimate remaining work as you learn; the uncertainty should shrink as the project progresses.

Frequently asked questions

What does PERT stand for?

Program Evaluation and Review Technique. It was developed in 1958 for the US Navy's Polaris programme.

What is a PERT chart used for?

To plan projects with uncertain durations: it shows activities and dependencies, finds the critical path and estimates the probability of meeting a deadline.

What is the PERT formula?

Expected duration = (optimistic + 4 x most likely + pessimistic) / 6. For O = 4, M = 6 and P = 14 days, the PERT estimate is 7 days.

How do you calculate PERT standard deviation?

(Pessimistic - optimistic) / 6 for one activity. For a path, add the activities' variances (SD squared) and take the square root.

Why is the most likely value multiplied by 4 in PERT?

The formula approximates the mean of a beta distribution, in which the most likely value carries most of the weight while the optimistic and pessimistic values allow for skew.

How do you calculate the probability of completing a project with PERT?

Calculate z = (deadline - expected duration of the critical path) / path standard deviation, then look up z in a normal distribution table. A z of 1 gives about 84%.

Is a PERT chart the same as a network diagram?

A PERT chart is a type of network diagram. What makes it PERT is the use of three-point estimates and probability analysis.

What is a dummy activity in a PERT chart?

A zero-duration arrow used in activity-on-arrow charts to show a dependency, or to stop two activities sharing the same start and end events. It uses no time or resources.

What is the difference between a PERT chart and a Gantt chart?

A PERT chart shows the logic and uncertainty of the network; a Gantt chart shows tasks as bars on a calendar for scheduling and tracking. Many teams use both.

What is the difference between PERT and CPM?

PERT uses three estimates per activity to model uncertainty and probability; CPM uses one estimate and focuses on the critical path, float and time-cost trade-offs.

Can PERT be used in Agile?

Not usually as a full network, but three-point estimates are useful for release forecasts and for communicating uncertainty to stakeholders.

What is the difference between PERT and three-point estimating?

Three-point estimating is the general idea of using optimistic, most likely and pessimistic values. PERT is one way of combining them, with the most likely value weighted four times; the triangular average, (O + M + P) / 3, is another.

What confidence level should I commit to?

It depends on the cost of being late. Many teams plan internally to the expected duration and commit externally to an 80% or 90% date, holding the difference as schedule contingency.

Who invented PERT?

The US Navy's Special Projects Office developed it in 1958 with Lockheed and Booz Allen Hamilton, to manage the Polaris missile programme.

Is PERT on the PMP exam?

Three-point estimating and network calculations are common PMP study topics. See our PMP certification guide for the current exam content.
