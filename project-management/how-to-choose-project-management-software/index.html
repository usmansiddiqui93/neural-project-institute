How to Choose Project Management Software: 8 Steps

To choose project management software, define the problems you need to solve, turn them into prioritised requirements, shortlist three tools, score them with a weighted matrix, trial them on real work and compare the full cost before you commit. This guide walks through each step with worked examples, a requirements checklist, a trial plan and the questions to ask vendors.

Key takeaways

  • Start from today's problems and must-have requirements, not from vendor demos or feature lists.
  • Count full users, light users and guests separately: licence types change the price more than the headline rate.
  • Score a shortlist of three with a weighted matrix, then trial the top two on a real project for two to four weeks.
  • Compare total cost of ownership, including the plan tier that holds your must-haves, set-up, training and admin time.

What does choosing project management software involve?

Choosing project management software is a structured selection process: you identify what your team needs a tool to do, compare options against those needs, test the best candidates on real work and confirm the total cost before buying. The goal is the best fit for how your team works, not the tool with the longest feature list or the best demo.

It is worth doing properly. Once a team has moved its projects, templates, history and habits into a platform, switching again is slow and disruptive. A tool people find awkward is used half-heartedly, and plans drift back into spreadsheets.

If you are still getting to grips with what these tools do, start with our overview of project management software and the main types of project management tools.

How deep should your selection go?

The process below scales. A five-person team can work through it in a fortnight; a regulated enterprise may need a formal procurement with legal and security review.

How to choose project management software in 8 steps

  1. Define the problems you need the tool to solve.
  2. Turn them into requirements, sorted into must-haves, should-haves and could-haves.
  3. Count your users and the type of access each one needs.
  4. Check integrations, security and IT requirements.
  5. Build a shortlist of three tools that meet every must-have.
  6. Score the shortlist with a weighted matrix.
  7. Run a structured trial of the top two on real work.
  8. Calculate the total cost, make the decision and plan the rollout.

Each step is explained below, with a running example: a 25-person marketing and creative team that currently manages work in spreadsheets, email and a shared drive.

Step 1: Define the problems you need to solve

Begin with what is going wrong today, in plain language. Problems are more useful than features at this stage.

Run a 45-minute session with a few people from each role and ask:

  • Where do deadlines slip, and why?
  • How do new requests reach the team, and what gets lost?
  • How do people know what they should work on next?
  • How does the manager see who is overloaded and who has capacity?
  • How long does it take to produce a status report?
  • Where do reviews and approvals get stuck?

For our example team, the answers point to four core problems: missed launch dates because dependencies are invisible, overloaded designers, approvals buried in email, and requests arriving through every channel at once.

Tip

Write each problem with a measure attached, such as "status reporting takes about four hours a week". You can check the same measures three months after rollout to see whether the tool helped.

A gap analysis template is a simple way to record the current state, the desired state and the gap for each problem.

Step 2: Turn problems into prioritised requirements

Next, translate each problem into requirements the software must meet. Sort them with a simple MoSCoW split: must have, should have, could have (and won't have this time). Keep must-haves short and strict: if a tool fails one, it is out.

Launch dates slip because dependencies are invisibleGantt or timeline view with task dependencies that reschedule automaticallyMust
Designers are overloadedWorkload view showing each person's assigned effort against capacityMust
Approvals are buried in emailProofing and approval workflow on files, with comments on the assetMust
Requests arrive through every channelRequest forms that create tasks automaticallyMust
Status reports take hoursShareable dashboards that update in real timeShould
Work sits in many toolsIntegrations with email, chat and file storageShould
Repetitive adminNo-code automationsCould

Step 3: Count users and licence types

Most project management software is priced per user, so the number and type of users drives cost more than any other factor. List three groups:

  • Full users: people who create and manage work every day, such as project managers, designers and developers.
  • Light users or collaborators: people who update their own tasks or comment but do not build projects.
  • Viewers and guests: executives who only read dashboards, and clients or freelancers outside your organisation.

Vendors handle these groups differently. Some offer cheaper collaborator roles or free viewers; others charge everyone the same seat price; a few, such as Basecamp, charge a flat monthly price rather than per user. Some sell seats in fixed bundles, and some plans have minimum or maximum user counts: Wrike's Team plan, for example, is for 2 to 15 users and its Business plan for 5 to 200 (according to our review data, checked 5 October 2026).

Also estimate growth over the next two years. A plan that fits 12 people today may not fit 20 next year.

Tip

Ask every vendor exactly what a viewer, collaborator or guest can and cannot do. The definitions differ, and they decide how many full licences you really need.

Step 4: Check integrations, security and IT requirements

A project management tool sits in the middle of your other systems, so check early whether it connects to them and whether your IT team can approve it.

Integrations

  • Email and calendar (Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace)
  • Chat (Slack or Microsoft Teams)
  • File storage (OneDrive, SharePoint, Google Drive, Dropbox, Box)
  • Development tools (Jira, GitHub, GitLab) if engineers are involved
  • CRM, finance or time-tracking systems, if projects are billed to clients
  • BI tools, such as Power BI or Tableau, if you report across the business
  • An open API or automation platform connector for anything else

Check whether each integration is native, works through a third-party connector or needs a higher plan. For example, our Wrike review notes that its two-way sync and cross-system integration products are included on the top Apex plan and sold as add-ons on other plans.

Security and administration checklist

  • Single sign-on (SAML SSO) and automatic user provisioning (SCIM), which are often on enterprise tiers only
  • Granular permissions, user roles and the ability to lock sensitive projects
  • Independent security certifications or audit reports (for example SOC 2 or ISO/IEC 27001) and their scope
  • Data residency options and GDPR support, if you hold personal data
  • Audit logs and admin controls
  • Data export in usable formats if you ever leave

Ask for the vendor's trust or security documentation rather than relying on badges on a marketing page, and let IT review it before the trial, not after you have chosen.

Step 6: Score your shortlist with a weighted matrix

A weighted scoring matrix stops the loudest opinion or the slickest demo from deciding. You give each criterion a weight that reflects its importance, score each tool against each criterion, multiply and add up.

  1. List 6–8 criteria drawn from your must-haves and should-haves, plus ease of use and cost.
  2. Give each criterion a weight so that the weights add up to 100.
  3. Score each tool from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on each criterion, ideally after demos and a first look at the trial.
  4. Multiply each score by its weight, add the results and divide by 5 to get a total out of 100.
  5. Use the totals to pick the top two for a full trial, not to make the final decision on their own.

Worked example: the 25-person marketing team

Wrike leads because it scores highest on the three heaviest must-have criteria, even though it scores lower on ease of use. monday.com wins ease of use and cost, and Asana sits between the two. The team takes Wrike and Asana into the trial. If ease of use had carried a weight of 30 instead of 15, the result would be much closer, which is why agreeing the weights before scoring matters.

Tip

Agree the weights with the team and the budget holder before anyone sees a demo. Changing weights after scoring is the most common way selection processes are quietly bent towards a favourite.

Step 7: Run a structured trial

A trial is where most selection decisions are really made. Most of the tools in our rankings offer a free plan or a free trial; for example, Wrike offers a 14-day trial without a credit card, and Smartsheet a 30-day trial (from our review data, checked 5 October 2026). Plan the trial so you test real work, not just features.

A four-week trial plan

WeekWhat to doOutput
Before you startPick one live project and 5–8 testers across roles, including one or two sceptics. Ask the vendor for onboarding help.Trial plan and test scenarios
Week 1Set up the workspace, import or create the project, build the request form and the workload view.A realistic set-up, not a demo account
Week 2Run daily work in the tool: assign tasks, run an approval, handle new requests.Notes on friction and missing features
Week 3Test reporting, integrations, mobile, permissions and guest access; produce a real status report from the tool.Evidence for each must-have
Week 4Collect scores and feedback; confirm pricing and contract terms.Completed trial scorecard

Trial scorecard

Ask each tester to rate the tool on the same short set of questions, from 1 to 5:

  • How easy was it to find what you needed to work on?
  • How easy was it to update your work?
  • Did the tool save you time compared with the current way of working?
  • Did each must-have work for your real tasks?
  • Would you be happy to use it every day?

Combine the testers' average with your matrix scores. If a tool wins on features but testers would not use it every day, take that seriously: adoption is part of fit.

In the example, Wrike's request forms, proofing and workload view handled the team's real campaign well; testers found the interface dense in week one but comfortable by week three once templates were in place. Asana was quicker to learn, but the team needed its Advanced plan for proofing and workload. The team chose Wrike and planned extra onboarding for the first month.

Step 8: Calculate total cost and make the case

The headline price per user is only part of the cost. Total cost of ownership (TCO) includes the plan tier that holds all your must-haves, add-ons, set-up time, training, ongoing administration and any migration help.

Three lessons come out of this kind of calculation:

  • Internal time often costs more than licences. In the example, set-up, training and admin are well over half of the year-one total for every tool.
  • The right tier matters more than the entry price. monday.com's lowest list price is $9 per seat, but the example team priced its Pro plan, and monday.com's fuller resource and portfolio management sits on its quote-based Enterprise plan. Price the tier that includes every must-have.
  • Price differences need context. Wrike costs about $2,800 more than monday.com in year one in this example. The team judged that worth it for stronger workload management and built-in proofing, which were two of its four must-have problems. Another team could reasonably decide the other way.

Also check add-ons (such as extra AI capacity, whiteboards or integration products), monthly versus annual billing, price increases at renewal, and whether you can reduce seats mid-contract.

Making the case to your budget holder

Keep the business case short: the problems from Step 1 with their measures, the weighted scores, the trial feedback, the three-year cost and the rollout plan. Our business case template gives you a ready-made structure.

Free vs paid project management software

Free plans are useful, but they are designed to let you start, not to run a growing team indefinitely.

Free plansPaid plans
Good forIndividuals, very small teams, simple task lists, trying a toolTeams that need planning, reporting and control
Typical limitsFew users, projects, boards or storage; basic views onlyHigher or no limits, depending on tier
Usually missingGantt charts, dashboards, workload, automations, admin controlsEnterprise security and advanced resource or financial features may still need top tiers

Examples from our review data (checked 5 October 2026): Jira's free plan covers up to 10 users, Trello's up to 10 collaborators per workspace, monday.com's up to 2 seats and Basecamp's one project. If your must-haves include Gantt dependencies, workload views or dashboards, you will almost certainly need a paid plan, so trial that tier rather than judging the tool by its free version.

Questions to ask vendors

Use demos and sales calls to fill gaps your trial cannot answer. Ask for answers in writing, especially on pricing and contract terms. For a formal process, a request for quote (RFQ) template keeps vendor responses comparable.

  • Which plan includes each of our must-have features, and which are add-ons?
  • How are full users, collaborators, viewers and guests defined and priced?
  • Are there minimum or maximum user numbers, or seat bundles, on each plan?
  • What limits apply to automations, storage and AI usage on each plan?
  • Which integrations are native, and which need a third-party connector?
  • What security certifications and audit reports can you share, and where is our data stored?
  • What onboarding, training and support are included, and what costs extra?
  • How do price increases at renewal work, and can we reduce seats mid-term?
  • How can we export all our data, including attachments and history, if we leave?

Rolling out your new project management tool

Selection is only half the job. A careful rollout is what turns a good choice into a tool people actually use.

  1. Appoint an owner and champions. One person owns the set-up and standards; a champion in each team answers day-to-day questions.
  2. Design a simple structure. Agree spaces or workspaces, project templates, statuses and a handful of custom fields before importing anything.
  3. Migrate selectively. Move active projects and templates; archive finished work in the old system or as exports rather than migrating everything.
  4. Pilot with one team. Run two to four weeks with a single team, fix what does not work, then roll out in waves.
  5. Train by role. Short, role-specific sessions work better than one long general demo. A training plan template helps you schedule them.
  6. Switch off the old way. Set a date after which status reports, requests and approvals happen only in the new tool.
  7. Review after 90 days. Re-measure the problems from Step 1 and adjust templates, automations and permissions.

Common mistakes and red flags

Common mistakes

  • Choosing on demos. A demo shows the vendor's best case; a trial with your real work shows yours.
  • Buying for the most advanced user. A tool configured for the most demanding person can overwhelm everyone else; use templates and permissions to keep views simple.
  • Comparing entry prices instead of the right tier. Must-have features often sit one or two tiers above the headline price.
  • No owner after go-live. Without someone maintaining templates and standards, every team sets the tool up differently.

Red flags during selection

  • Testers keep returning to spreadsheets or chat during the trial.
  • The vendor cannot say clearly which plan includes a must-have feature.
  • Exporting your data is difficult or incomplete.
  • Pricing depends on bundles or limits you only discover late in the process.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose the right project management software?

Define the problems you need to solve, turn them into must-have and nice-to-have requirements, shortlist three tools, score them with a weighted matrix, trial the top two on real work, then compare total cost and plan the rollout.

What are the most important features to look for?

It depends on your work, but most teams need task management, several views (list, board, timeline or Gantt), collaboration on tasks, dashboards, integrations and sensible permissions. Resource management, proofing and time tracking matter for specific teams.

What is the best project management software?

There is no single best tool for everyone. In our independent rankings, Wrike scores highest overall (9.4/10) as the strongest all-in-one platform, followed by monday.com and Asana. The best choice for you is the one that meets your must-haves and passes a real trial.

How much does project management software cost?

Paid plans from the tools in our rankings typically range from about $5 to $30 per user per month on annual billing, with enterprise plans priced on quote. Basecamp is an exception with flat monthly pricing. Always add set-up, training and admin time.

Is free project management software good enough?

For individuals, small teams and simple task lists, often yes. Free plans usually limit users, projects or storage and leave out Gantt charts, dashboards, workload views and admin controls that growing teams need.

How long should a project management software trial last?

Long enough to run real work through the tool, typically two to four weeks. Many vendors offer 14- or 30-day trials; ask for an extension if your project needs longer.

What is a weighted scoring matrix?

A table that lists your selection criteria, gives each one a weight reflecting its importance, scores each tool against each criterion and multiplies the scores by the weights to produce a comparable total.

Which project management software suits Agile teams?

Jira is the specialist choice for software teams running Scrum or Kanban. ClickUp and Wrike suit teams that mix Agile work with other kinds of projects.

Which project management software suits small businesses?

Small businesses often start with an easy tool and a free or low-cost plan, such as Trello, Asana, monday.com or ClickUp, or Basecamp for flat pricing. If you expect to need resource planning and reporting as you grow, include Wrike in the trial.

Should IT be involved in choosing project management software?

Yes, from the start. IT should check single sign-on, security certifications, data storage, integrations and data export before the trial, so a late objection does not overturn your choice.

How do I get my team to adopt new project management software?

Involve users in the trial, start with simple templates, train by role, appoint champions in each team and set a date after which work is managed only in the new tool.
