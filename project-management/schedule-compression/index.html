Schedule Compression: Techniques, Steps and Worked Example

Schedule compression is a set of techniques for shortening a project schedule without reducing its scope. The two main techniques are crashing, which adds resources to critical activities at extra cost, and fast tracking, which overlaps activities at extra risk. This guide explains both, shows how to choose which activities to compress, and works through a full example with costs, paths and trade-offs.

Key takeaways

  • Schedule compression shortens the timeline while keeping scope the same.
  • Crashing buys time with money; fast tracking buys time with risk.
  • Only compress critical activities, cheapest first, one step at a time, and recheck the critical path after each step.
  • Compression can lower total cost up to a point, because indirect costs fall as the project gets shorter.
  • Stop when you reach the target or when the next day saved costs more than it is worth.

What is schedule compression?

Schedule compression is the shortening of a project schedule to meet a deadline or recover lost time without reducing the agreed scope. It works by shortening activities on the critical path, either by adding resources (crashing) or by overlapping activities that would normally run in sequence (fast tracking). Both trade time for extra cost or risk.

Compression only works on the critical path, the longest chain of dependent activities. Shortening anything else just adds float to that activity and leaves the finish date where it was. And because the critical path can move, compression is an iterative process: shorten, recalculate, repeat.

The PMBOK Guide's 6th edition listed schedule compression as a tool and technique of the Develop Schedule process, and crashing and fast tracking remain standard topics in PMP certification preparation. You will use it in two situations: during planning, when the first schedule misses a required date, and during delivery, when the project has slipped and needs to recover.

When to compress a schedule

  • An imposed date: a regulatory deadline, an event, a contract milestone or a market launch window.
  • Negative float: the calculated finish is later than a required date, so critical activities show negative total float.
  • Schedule slippage: a negative schedule variance on critical work, or an earned schedule forecast beyond the deadline.
  • Earlier benefits: finishing early brings revenue, savings or compliance forward.
  • Penalties or high running costs: late-delivery damages, site costs or team costs that accrue every day.
  • Resource windows: a specialist, facility or shutdown period that is only available on certain dates.

During delivery, earned value data often provides the trigger. In the illustrative CRM project in our schedule variance guide, an SV of -$25,000 at month five translated into an earned schedule forecast of about 11 months against a 10-month plan. If go-live were tied to a fixed date, that forecast would be the starting point for a compression analysis: about one month to recover, concentrated in the build and testing work on the critical path.

Compression is often the wrong answer when requirements are still changing (overlap leads to rework), when the team is already working overtime, or when the cost of compressing is greater than the cost of finishing late. In those cases, negotiating the date or the scope through change control is usually better.

Schedule compression techniques

Crashing

Crashing shortens critical activities by adding resources: more people, overtime, extra shifts, faster equipment or premium delivery from suppliers. It increases direct cost, but the time saved is usually more predictable than with fast tracking.

Crashing has limits. Each activity has a crash point beyond which no amount of money makes it shorter, and returns diminish well before that. New people need onboarding and add communication overhead. Fred Brooks put it memorably in The Mythical Man-Month (1975): "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later". Crash work that can genuinely be divided, such as installation, data entry or parallel testing, rather than work that depends on a few people's knowledge.

Fast tracking

Fast tracking overlaps activities that would normally run one after another, for example starting build before design is fully signed off. In scheduling terms you change a finish-to-start dependency into one with a lead, or into a start-to-start link. See leads and lags.

Fast tracking has little direct cost, but it adds risk: if the earlier activity changes, work already done on the later one may need redoing. It only works on discretionary (soft) dependencies, not on mandatory ones such as "concrete must cure before loading". Our guide to task dependencies explains the difference.

Other ways to shorten a schedule

Crashing and fast tracking are the textbook techniques, but recovery plans often start with cheaper options:

  • Remove waiting time: shorten lags, speed up approvals and procurement, pre-order long-lead items.
  • Rethink the logic: challenge dependencies that are habits rather than necessities.
  • Move people from float to critical work: reassign resources from non-critical activities, within their skills.
  • Remove blockers: missing decisions, access, data or environments.
  • Improve productivity: fewer interruptions and handoffs, better tools, focused teams.

CrashingMore resources on critical activitiesHigher costSecond installation crew; weekend shifts
Fast trackingOverlapping sequential activitiesRework riskStart build on approved design modules
Remove waitingShorter lags and approval cyclesNeeds stakeholder cooperationWeekly sign-off meeting instead of email chains

What is not schedule compression

Three other responses to a deadline problem are often confused with compression:

OptionWhat it doesWhy it is different
Reducing scopeDelivers less, or delivers some items laterCompression keeps scope the same. Cutting scope changes the deliverables and needs sponsor approval; see project scope
Resource levellingResolves over-allocation by delaying activitiesIt usually lengthens the schedule; see resource levelling
Re-baseliningApproves a new finish dateAccepts the delay rather than recovering it

All three are legitimate options, and a recovery plan often combines them with compression. Just label them correctly, so the sponsor knows whether they are buying time with money, risk or scope.

Crash cost slope and the time-cost trade-off

To crash efficiently you need to know what each day saved costs. The crash cost slope gives that figure for each activity:

Crash cost slope = (crash cost - normal cost) / (normal duration - crash duration)

For example, an activity with a normal duration of 15 days costing $30,000 that can be crashed to 12 days for $32,400 has a slope of 2,400 / 3 = $800 per day. The crash duration (12 days) is its crash point.

Project costs fall into two groups, and they move in opposite directions as you compress:

  • Direct costs belong to activities: labour, materials, equipment. Crashing increases them.
  • Indirect costs accrue with time: project management, site or office costs, team overheads, financing, late penalties. Shortening the project reduces them.

As long as the crash cost slope is less than the indirect cost saved per day, compression lowers total cost. The duration where total cost is lowest is the least-cost (or optimum) duration. Beyond it, each day saved costs more than it saves, until you reach the crash limit, where no more time can be saved at any price.

Tip

Many projects never calculate indirect costs per day, so compression always looks like pure extra cost. Ask finance or the PMO for the daily running cost of the team and any penalties. It often changes the decision.

How to compress a schedule step by step

  1. Confirm the target date and how many days must be saved, and whether the date is truly fixed.
  2. Update the schedule with current progress and run the forward and backward pass to find the critical path and near-critical paths.
  3. List options for each critical activity: can it be crashed (by how much, at what cost), can it overlap its predecessor, and can its lags or waiting time shrink?
  4. Calculate the crash cost slope for each crashable activity, and assess the rework risk of each fast-tracking option.
  5. Start with the cheapest, lowest-risk option on the critical path. Prefer activities shared by several near-critical paths, because one change shortens them all.
  6. Shorten one day (or one period) at a time and recalculate. When another path becomes critical, you must shorten both paths together.
  7. Stop at the target date, at the least-cost duration if there is no fixed target, or when the next step costs more than the benefit.
  8. Record new risks in the risk register, update the budget, and check that resources are really available.
  9. Approve the revised schedule and costs through change control and update the project baseline.

Worked example: compressing a website relaunch

This illustrative example is larger than the six-activity network in our fast tracking vs crashing guide, so you can see what happens when more than one path becomes critical. A company is relaunching its website. The schedule is 58 working days, but the sponsor needs the site live in 52 days for a product launch. Normal direct cost is $180,000, and indirect costs (project team, agency retainer and environments) run at $1,000 per day.

ActivityPredecessorsNormal daysCrash daysMax days savedCrash cost slope

E Integrations buildB18153$1,000/day

G TestingD, E, F1082$2,000/day
H LaunchG330Cannot crash

There are three paths through the network:

  • A-B-D-G-H: 10 + 15 + 20 + 10 + 3 = 58 days (critical).
  • A-B-E-G-H: 10 + 15 + 18 + 10 + 3 = 56 days (near-critical, 2 days of float).
  • A-C-F-G-H: 10 + 12 + 6 + 10 + 3 = 41 days.

C is the cheapest activity to crash, but it is not on the critical path, so crashing it buys nothing.

Crashing B at $800 per day saves $1,000 per day of indirect cost, so the first three days actually reduce total cost. From there, every activity costs more than $1,000 per day, so total cost rises. Meeting the 52-day target by crashing alone costs only $300 more than the original plan, which is a much easier conversation with the sponsor than "$6,300 extra".

Fast tracking the same project

Instead of paying for time, the team could overlap both builds with the end of design, starting each build on design modules already approved. With a 6-day lead on B to D and a 4-day lead on B to E, the two longest paths both become 52 days, at no direct cost. The price is risk: front-end development would start six days before design sign-off, and any late design change means rework. Both paths would also have zero float, so any slip moves the launch date.

Comparing the options

A third option combines the two: crash design by three days with a second designer ($2,400), then overlap front-end build with design by 3 days and integrations build by 1 day. Both paths come out at 52 days (10 + 12 - 3 + 20 + 10 + 3 for the front end, and 10 + 12 - 1 + 18 + 10 + 3 for integrations).

In this example the combined option is the most balanced recommendation: it meets the date, saves money overall and keeps overlap short enough to manage. Fast tracking alone looks cheapest on paper, but a single significant design change could wipe out the saving. Whatever the choice, the new risks go into the risk register and the revised schedule goes through change control.

How to present compression options to a sponsor

Compression is a trade-off between time, cost, risk and sometimes quality, so the decision belongs to the sponsor, not the project manager alone. Present it as a choice between clear options rather than a single request for money.

  1. State the problem in time terms: the current forecast finish, the required date and the gap in days.
  2. Explain what drives the date: the critical path and any near-critical paths.
  3. Show two or three options, each with the new finish date, extra direct cost, indirect savings, net cost and main risks, as in the comparison table above.
  4. Include the do-nothing option: the cost and consequences of finishing late, such as penalties, lost revenue or a missed launch.
  5. Make a recommendation and explain why.
  6. Say what you need: budget, people, decisions or faster approvals, and by when.

Framing compression against the project constraints helps sponsors see that time cannot be bought without moving cost, risk or scope. A sponsor who chooses an option knowing its risks is far more likely to support you when one of those risks occurs.

Risks of schedule compression and how to manage them

RiskWhy it happensHow to manage it
ReworkLater work starts on unfinished inputsOverlap only stable components; agree change rules; review early

Burnout and quality lossLong periods of overtimeTime-limit overtime; protect testing and reviews

Coordination overloadMore parallel work and handoffsShort daily coordination; clear interfaces and owners

The common thread is that a compressed schedule is more fragile. With less float and more parallel work, small delays travel further. Some teams protect the compressed plan with an explicit buffer before the deadline, an idea borrowed from critical chain project management.

Schedule compression in Agile and hybrid projects

Agile teams rarely compress in the traditional sense. Sprints are fixed time-boxes, so when a release date is at risk the usual response is to reprioritise the product backlog and deliver the most valuable items by the date, which is a scope decision rather than compression.

Agile does have its own versions of the techniques. Swarming, where the team works together on the highest-priority item, is a way of shortening the critical work without adding people. Adding people to a team mid-release behaves like crashing and carries the same onboarding cost.

In hybrid projects, classic compression still applies to the predictive parts, such as procurement, infrastructure, data migration and training, while Agile teams manage scope within their time-boxes.

Common schedule compression mistakes

  • Compressing non-critical activities because they are cheap or easy.
  • Forgetting to recalculate after each step, and missing a new critical path.
  • Fast tracking mandatory dependencies.
  • Adding people to work that cannot be divided.
  • Ignoring indirect costs, so every option looks more expensive than it is.
  • Treating scope cuts as compression without sponsor approval.
  • Leaving the extra cost and risk out of the budget and risk register.

Schedule compression checklist before approval

  • The target date and the number of days to save are confirmed and agreed.
  • The critical path and near-critical paths have been recalculated with current progress.
  • Only critical activities are being compressed.
  • Crash cost slopes are based on real quotes or rates, not guesses.
  • Extra people or equipment are confirmed as available on the dates needed.
  • Every overlap is on a discretionary dependency, with a plan for handling changes.
  • Indirect cost savings and any penalties avoided are included in the comparison.
  • New risks are in the risk register with owners; our risk register template has the fields you need.
  • The revised budget, schedule and baseline are approved through change control.
  • Near-critical paths will be monitored more often until the deadline.

Frequently asked questions

What are the two main schedule compression techniques?

Crashing, which adds resources to critical activities at extra cost, and fast tracking, which overlaps activities normally done in sequence at extra risk.

What is the difference between crashing and fast tracking?

Crashing shortens activities by adding people, overtime or equipment, so it costs more. Fast tracking runs activities in parallel, so it costs little directly but increases the risk of rework.

What is an example of schedule compression?

Adding a second designer to finish design three days sooner (crashing), or starting development on approved design modules before the full design is signed off (fast tracking).

How do you calculate crash cost per day?

Use the crash cost slope: (crash cost - normal cost) / (normal duration - crash duration). An activity crashed from 15 to 12 days for an extra $2,400 costs $800 per day saved.

What is the crash point of an activity?

The shortest duration an activity can reach however many resources are added. Beyond it, extra money buys no more time.

Is fast tracking always cheaper than crashing?

Not always. Fast tracking has little direct cost, but if overlapping work has to be redone, the rework can cost more than crashing would have. Its true cost depends on how stable the earlier work is.

Why do you only compress critical path activities?

Only critical activities determine the project finish date. Shortening a non-critical activity adds float to it but does not bring the finish forward.

Is reducing scope a schedule compression technique?

Strictly, no. Compression keeps scope the same. Reducing scope is a separate option that changes the deliverables and needs sponsor approval.

What is the difference between schedule compression and resource levelling?

Compression shortens the schedule. Resource levelling resolves over-allocation by delaying activities, so it usually lengthens the schedule.

Can schedule compression reduce total project cost?

Yes, up to a point. If the cost of crashing a day is less than the indirect cost saved per day, total cost falls. The lowest point is the least-cost duration.

How far can you compress a schedule?

Until every critical path is at its crash limit, or until risk or cost outweighs the benefit. In practice, stop at the target date or the least-cost duration.

Does schedule compression increase risk?

Yes. Both techniques reduce float and create more critical paths, and fast tracking adds rework risk. Record the new risks in the risk register.

Can Agile teams compress a schedule?

Agile teams usually hold the date and adjust scope through the product backlog. Swarming and adding team members are the closest Agile equivalents to compression.
