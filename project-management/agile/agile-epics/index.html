Agile Epics: What They Are, How to Write Them, Examples

Agile epics are large backlog items with a single goal that are too big to finish in one Sprint, so teams split them into smaller user stories and deliver them over several Sprints. Epics connect day-to-day stories to the outcomes on your roadmap and give stakeholders one place to see progress on something that matters. This guide shows how to write a good epic with a template, how to size, forecast, track and close one, and how SAFe uses the term, with worked examples you can adapt.

Key takeaways

  • An epic is a large body of work with one goal and a finish line. It spans several Sprints and is split into user stories before the team builds it.
  • Write epics around outcomes: a clear title, who benefits and why, one to three success measures, scope boundaries and high-level acceptance criteria.
  • Most team-level epics take several Sprints and fit within a quarter. If an epic never ends, it is really a theme.
  • Forecast an epic with a velocity range and the share of capacity it actually gets, not the team's full velocity.
  • An epic is done when its goal is met, not when every story ever linked to it is finished. Move leftover stories out and close it.
  • In SAFe, an epic is much larger: a portfolio-level investment with a hypothesis, an MVP and a Lean business case.

What is an epic in Agile?

An epic is a large backlog item that describes one goal and is too big to deliver in a single Sprint. It is broken down into smaller user stories that the team completes over several Sprints, sometimes across several teams. An epic has a clear outcome and a finish line, and closes once that outcome is achieved.

Epics sit between the strategic level (themes, initiatives or objectives) and the user stories the team pulls into Sprints. An epic says what you want to achieve, such as 'self-service returns'; its stories describe the individual slices that get you there, such as 'as a customer, I want to print a returns label so that I can send an item back without calling'.

Good epics share four characteristics:

  • One goal. Every story in the epic contributes to the same outcome.
  • Too big for one Sprint. If it fits in a Sprint, it is a story.
  • Split before it is built. The team never works on the epic directly, only on its stories.
  • A finish line. You can tell when it is done. Ongoing areas of work, such as 'site performance', are themes.

Epics are a widely used convention, not part of Scrum. The 2020 Scrum Guide only describes Product Backlog items, and teams decide for themselves how to group them. See our guide to epics vs stories vs tasks for a side-by-side comparison of the three levels and how tools such as Jira name them.

Why teams use epics

Epics give a long backlog a structure that people outside the team can follow.

  • They connect strategy to delivery. Items on the Agile roadmap become epics, and epics become stories, so everyone can see why a story matters.
  • They make release planning possible. A Product Owner can order and size a dozen epics long before every story is written; see Agile release planning.
  • They give stakeholders one place to look. Progress on 'self-service returns' is easier to follow than progress on 14 separate stories.
  • They help control scope. Boundaries and acceptance criteria at epic level make it obvious when a new request belongs somewhere else.

Types of epics

Most epics deliver something to users, but not all. Labelling the type helps the Product Owner balance the backlog and explain non-feature work to stakeholders.

TypeWhat it deliversExample (illustrative)
Business (feature) epicNew or improved value for customers or usersSelf-service returns for online orders
Enabler or technical epicArchitecture, infrastructure or tooling that future features depend onMove order data to a new event-driven platform
Compliance epicChanges required by law, regulation or policyBring the checkout up to WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility
Exploration epicLearning that informs a decision, usually through experiments or prototypesTest whether customers will pay for same-day delivery
Improvement or debt epicReduced technical debt, faster delivery or lower running costReplace the legacy payment library; see technical debt

Size also varies by context. A team epic is owned by one Product Owner and delivered in a few Sprints. A portfolio epic in the Scaled Agile Framework is a much larger investment that may run across several teams for many months; it is covered later in this guide.

Anatomy of a good epic

An epic needs enough information for the team to split it and for stakeholders to know what success looks like. Use these fields as a template.

FieldWhat to write
TitleA short outcome-focused name (two to five words)
Epic statementWho benefits, what changes for them and why it matters
Success measuresOne to three measurable outcomes and a time frame
In scopeWhat the epic will deliver
Out of scopeWhat it will not deliver, to prevent scope creep
Acceptance criteriaHigh-level conditions that must be true for the epic to be done
Dependencies and risksOther teams, systems, suppliers or decisions it relies on
Rough sizeT-shirt size or initial story-point range
LinksThe roadmap item or objective it supports, and its child stories

Writing the epic statement

Three formats are common. Pick one and use it consistently.

  • Outcome statement: 'Customers can return items online without contacting support.' Short and easy for stakeholders.
  • Story-style: 'As an online customer, I want to start a return myself so that I get my refund without waiting on the phone.' Familiar to teams that already write user stories.
  • Hypothesis: 'We believe that self-service returns for online customers will reduce return-related contacts. We will know we are right when contacts fall below 1,000 a month.' Useful when the benefit is uncertain and you want to test it.

Epic acceptance criteria and Definition of Done

Epic acceptance criteria describe the whole outcome, not individual screens: 'customers can start, track and complete a return for any eligible UK order', or 'support staff can see every return started online'. Each story still has its own acceptance criteria and must meet the team's Definition of Done. The epic is done when its acceptance criteria are met and its success measure can be tracked.

Tip

The one-sentence test: if you cannot explain why an epic matters to a user or the business in one sentence, it is probably a bundle of unrelated work. Split it into separate epics with their own goals.

How to write an epic, step by step

Use this process when a roadmap item is ready to move into the backlog, typically one or two quarters before work starts.

  1. Start from the outcome. Take the roadmap item or objective and ask what should be different for users or the business when this work is finished.
  2. Name it by outcome. Write a short title that describes the result, not the technical work. 'Self-service returns', not 'Returns API'.
  3. Write the epic statement. Use an outcome, story-style or hypothesis format to say who benefits and why.
  4. Define success. Choose one to three measures with a baseline and a target, such as 'return contacts fall from about 2,400 to under 1,000 a month within three months of launch'.
  5. Set boundaries. List what is in and out of scope, and anything still undecided.
  6. Add high-level acceptance criteria. Three to six conditions that describe the finished outcome.
  7. Note dependencies and risks. Identify other teams, suppliers, data or approvals you need, and plan early stories or spikes to reduce the biggest risk.
  8. Size it roughly. Ask the team for a T-shirt size or a story-point range. If it looks larger than a quarter, split it into two epics with separate goals.
  9. Split the first slice into stories. Write the stories for a thin end-to-end slice first and leave later parts rough until you get closer. Our user story template includes an epic sheet.
  10. Order it in the backlog. Place the epic in the Product Backlog by value, risk and dependencies, and review it in backlog refinement. Our product backlog template keeps the order, value and estimates in one place.

The Product Owner usually writes the epic, but the best epics are shaped with Developers, designers and stakeholders, who spot risks and simpler options early.

Worked example: a self-service returns epic

The following example is illustrative. An online retailer handles returns by email and phone, and its support team receives about 2,400 return-related contacts a month. The Product Owner writes an epic to change that.

Epic statementAs an online customer, I want to start and track a return myself so that I get my refund or exchange without contacting support.

Before story 3, the team runs a time-boxed spike to test the carrier's label service, because that is the biggest unknown.

Forecasting the epic

The team's velocity ranges from 30 to 36 points per Sprint, but it also handles bugs and another epic. The Product Owner plans to give this epic about 40% of capacity, so it gets roughly 12 to 15 points a Sprint.

CasePoints per Sprint on this epicCalculationSprints needed
Fast1542 ÷ 15 = 2.83
Slow1242 ÷ 12 = 3.54

The forecast is three to four two-week Sprints. Using the team's full velocity (42 ÷ 36) would have suggested about one and a half Sprints, a common and avoidable forecasting error. See Agile estimation and story points for how to size stories consistently.

Tracking and closing

Start--42

In Sprint 2 the team discovered that international orders need customs paperwork and added a 5-point story. The core returns flow went live at the end of Sprint 3. In Sprint 4 the Product Owner reviewed the acceptance criteria, which cover UK orders only, and moved the international story out of the epic into its own backlog item, because international orders are a small share of returns and can be prioritised separately. The epic was closed at the end of Sprint 4, within the forecast range. The success measure stays on the roadmap and is reviewed three months after launch.

Non-software example: new starter onboarding

Illustrative. An HR team wants new employees to be productive faster. Epic: New starter ready in week one. Statement: as a new starter, I want everything I need on my first day so that I can start useful work within my first week. Success measure: average time to first independent task falls from about 15 working days to 7. Stories include:

  • As a new starter, I want my laptop and accounts working on day one so that I do not lose my first days waiting.
  • As a hiring manager, I want a pre-start checklist so that nothing is forgotten.
  • As a new starter, I want a named buddy so that I have someone to ask.
  • As HR, I want contracts signed electronically before the start date so that onboarding is not delayed by paperwork.
  • As a new starter, I want a first-week plan so that I know what is expected of me.

Each story can be delivered and tested with the next intake of new starters, which gives the team feedback long before the whole epic is finished. Our guide Is Agile only for software? has more examples outside IT.

How big should an epic be?

There is no standard size. Vendors' guidance ranges from 'anything that takes weeks or longer' to one to four months. For a single team, a practical rule is that an epic takes several Sprints and fits within a quarter, so it can be planned on the roadmap and finished before priorities shift.

SignalWhat it suggestsWhat to do
Fits in one SprintIt is a story, not an epicTreat it as a single backlog item
Two to six Sprints, one clear goalA healthy team epicKeep it; split stories progressively
More than a quarterProbably several goals bundled togetherSplit into two or more epics with separate outcomes
No end in sightIt is a theme or a product areaTurn it into a theme and create finite epics under it
Dozens of stories after refinementToo broad, or stories are too smallCheck the goal; merge tiny stories or split the epic

The number of stories varies with how finely a team splits work. Many team epics end up with somewhere between about five and fifteen stories; far more than that is a prompt to check whether the epic has one goal.

The epic lifecycle

Tracking epics through clear states stops half-defined ideas from reaching Sprint Planning and stops finished epics from staying open for months. A typical team-level lifecycle looks like this:

StateWhat happensReady to move on when
IdeaCaptured from the roadmap, stakeholders or the teamSomeone owns it and it supports a current objective
ShapingStatement, success measures, boundaries and rough size writtenThe team understands the goal and the biggest risks
ReadyFirst stories written and refined; epic ordered in the backlogEnough stories are ready for one or two Sprints
In progressStories delivered Sprint by Sprint; progress trackedEpic acceptance criteria are met
DoneOutcome delivered; leftover stories moved or removedThe success measure is being tracked
AbandonedStopped because evidence or priorities changed; a healthy outcome if early releases show a smaller benefitDecision and reason recorded

How to break an epic into user stories

Split epics into vertical slices: stories that each deliver something a user can see or use, cutting through every layer needed to make it work. Avoid splitting by technical layer ('database', 'API', 'screen'), because none of those delivers value on its own.

  1. Map the user journey. Lay out the steps a user takes, left to right, and list options under each step.
  2. Choose a thin end-to-end slice first. In the returns example, that was 'see returnable items, choose them, get a label'. It proves the whole flow early.
  3. Add rules, variations and exceptions as later stories. Exchanges, international orders and error messages come after the core path works.
  4. Refine progressively. Detail the next one or two Sprints' stories and leave the rest as rough placeholders.

Tracking epic progress

Track an epic on two levels: delivery (how much of the planned work is done) and outcome (whether the goal is being achieved).

  • Epic burndown or burnup. Shows remaining or completed points Sprint by Sprint, including scope added along the way. A burnup makes scope growth easier to see.
  • Forecast range. Remaining points divided by the slow and fast rate the epic actually receives, updated each Sprint.
  • Outcome measure. The success measure from the epic, tracked once the first slice is live.

Warning signs: scope growing faster than work is completed, the epic getting far less capacity than planned, and stories finished but not released. Our guide to the Agile velocity chart explains how to read the team's pace.

When to close an epic

Close an epic when its goal is achieved and its acceptance criteria are met, even if some linked stories remain. Leftover stories are a sign of learning, not failure.

  • Move stories that still matter but are not needed for this goal into their own backlog items or a new epic.
  • Remove stories that no longer add value.
  • Record what was delivered, what was moved and why, in a sentence or two on the epic.
  • Keep tracking the success measure on the roadmap or in your OKRs; our OKR template can hold it.

If the same area keeps needing work, create a theme and add new, finite epics to it.

Epics in SAFe: portfolio epics

In the Scaled Agile Framework, an epic is far larger than a team epic. SAFe defines it as a container for a significant solution development initiative, the kind of investment that a portfolio decides to fund. SAFe distinguishes business epics, which deliver value directly, from enabler epics, which build the architectural runway that future business needs depend on.

  • Epic hypothesis statement: describes the epic, the expected business outcomes and the leading indicators that will show whether it is working.
  • Minimum viable product (MVP): an early, minimal version used to prove or disprove the hypothesis with real users.
  • Lean business case: the result of analysing the epic, which portfolio leaders review to make a go or no-go decision.
  • Epic Owner: the person responsible for coordinating the epic's analysis and helping split it into features or capabilities for the teams.
  • Portfolio Kanban: a board where epics move through states from initial idea to approval, implementation and completion.

SAFe states that epics are not simply another word for projects: rather than funding projects, it funds value streams and uses the epic to manage a large investment. Features from an approved epic are then planned in PI Planning. If you are not using SAFe, you can still borrow the hypothesis and MVP ideas for risky epics. Our guide to Agile portfolio management covers how organisations decide which large epics to fund.

ScaleOne team, several SprintsSeveral teams or trains, often many months

Epics in project management tools

Most project management tools support epics directly or through parent items, so check how the tool connects epics to roadmaps and reports before you set it up.

  • Jira has epics at the top of its default work item hierarchy, with stories, tasks and bugs below and sub-tasks beneath them. Administrators can add levels above epics, and epic progress appears on boards, timelines and reports.
  • Wrike (our #1, 9.4) lets teams model epics as parent items or projects with subtasks, and connect them to Gantt charts, dashboards and portfolio reporting, which suits organisations that need epic progress visible to leadership.
  • monday.com (9.1), Asana (9.0) and ClickUp (8.9) can represent epics as parent items, projects or tasks with subtasks, with roadmap, timeline and dashboard views; monday.com also offers a separate product, monday dev, for sprint-based software teams.

Whichever tool you use, agree a simple convention: one epic per goal, a status that matches the lifecycle above, and a link from each epic to the roadmap item it supports. Compare tools side by side on our project management tools comparison.

Frequently asked questions

What is an epic in Agile?

A large backlog item with one goal that is too big for a single Sprint. It is broken down into smaller user stories that the team delivers over several Sprints, and it closes when the goal is achieved.

What is an example of an epic?

'Self-service returns' for an online retailer is a typical epic: customers can start, track and complete a return without contacting support. Its stories include viewing returnable items, printing a prepaid label and tracking the refund.

How do you write a good epic?

Give it an outcome-focused title, a one-sentence statement of who benefits and why, one to three success measures, in-scope and out-of-scope lists, high-level acceptance criteria, dependencies and a rough size.

How long should an epic be?

There is no fixed rule. For a single team, an epic usually takes several Sprints and fits within a quarter. If it is expected to run much longer, split it into epics with separate goals.

How many user stories should an epic have?

It depends on how finely the team splits work. Many team epics end up with roughly five to fifteen stories. Far more than that suggests the epic has more than one goal.

Who writes epics?

Usually the Product Owner or product manager, with input from the Developers, designers and stakeholders. In SAFe, an Epic Owner coordinates portfolio epics.

Are epics part of Scrum?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention epics; it describes Product Backlog items. Epics are a common practice that many Scrum Teams use to organise the backlog.

Do epics have story points?

Teams often give epics a rough T-shirt size or a story-point range for roadmap planning, then replace it with the sum of the story estimates once the epic is split.

Should an epic have acceptance criteria?

Yes. High-level acceptance criteria describe when the outcome as a whole is achieved. Individual stories still have their own acceptance criteria and must meet the Definition of Done.

What is the difference between an epic and a user story?

An epic is a large goal that spans several Sprints; a user story is a small slice of value that fits in one Sprint. Epics are split into stories. See our guide to epics vs stories vs tasks.

What is the difference between an epic and a feature?

It depends on the framework. In SAFe, epics are larger and are split into features, which are then split into stories. Many teams outside SAFe use the words loosely or interchangeably.

What is the difference between an epic, a theme and an initiative?

A theme or initiative is a broad objective or focus area that can contain several epics and may have no end date. An epic is a specific body of work with a finish line.
