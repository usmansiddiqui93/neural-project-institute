Spotify Model: Squads, Tribes, Chapters and Guilds Explained

Squads, tribes, chapters and guilds are the four units of the so-called Spotify model: squads are small cross-functional delivery teams, tribes group related squads, chapters bring together people with the same skill for line management, and guilds are voluntary communities across the whole organisation. The terms come from a 2012 paper by Henrik Kniberg and Anders Ivarsson that described how Spotify's engineering organisation worked at the time, not a framework to install. This guide explains each unit, who decides what, how the matrix behaves in practice, what critics found when others copied it, and how to adapt the ideas with a worked example.

Key takeaways

  • A squad builds and runs one part of the product; a tribe is a group of squads with a related mission; a chapter is a same-skill group inside a tribe whose chapter lead is the line manager; a guild is an open, organisation-wide community of interest.
  • The result is a matrix weighted towards delivery: the product owner shapes what a squad builds, while the chapter lead looks after how well people build it and how they grow.
  • The 2012 paper called Spotify's approach a journey in progress. Its authors and later Spotify staff have said it was a snapshot of one company, not a framework to copy.
  • Common failures come from renaming teams without changing decision rights, from autonomy without alignment, and from chapter leads who carry too many reports while also working in a squad.
  • Start from your own problems and missions, then borrow the parts that address them; SAFe, LeSS or Scrum@Scale may fit better when many teams share one product.

What is the Spotify model?

The Spotify model is a way of organising many Agile teams around small, autonomous, cross-functional squads, grouped into tribes by mission, with chapters for same-skill line management and guilds for voluntary knowledge sharing. It aims to give teams autonomy while keeping technical standards and people development consistent across the organisation.

The name was never official. Henrik Kniberg and Anders Ivarsson published *Scaling Agile @ Spotify with Tribes, Squads, Chapters & Guilds* in October 2012. At the time of writing, Spotify's technology staff had grown from 30 to 250 people in three years and worked in more than 30 teams across three cities. Kniberg's two animated *Spotify Engineering Culture* videos, published on Spotify's engineering blog in March and September 2014, spread the ideas much further.

The paper addresses one problem: how to keep the speed and ownership of a small start-up team as the number of teams grows, without each team drifting off in its own technical direction or losing contact with people who share its craft. The four units are its answer. The authors also called the approach "a journey in progress, not a journey completed", which matters when you decide how much of it to copy.

If you are new to team design, start with what an Agile team is and the wider choice of Agile team structures; this guide assumes you already know what a cross-functional team does.

A quick way to remember the difference: squads and tribes answer what the organisation builds; chapters and guilds answer how well it builds it. The first two are the delivery structure, the second two cut across it.

Two pairs are easy to confuse. Chapter vs guild: a chapter is a formal line-management group inside one tribe; a guild is a voluntary community open to anyone in any tribe. Squad vs tribe: a squad is one team with one mission; a tribe is a group of squads with related missions.

Squads: the delivery team

A squad is the basic unit of delivery. Kniberg and Ivarsson compare it to a Scrum team and describe it as a "mini-startup": its members sit together, the squad has the skills to design, build, test and release its part of the product, and it chooses its own way of working, whether that is Scrum, Kanban or a mix. According to the paper, squads also spend roughly 10% of their time on hack days for experiments.

Roles in a squad

  • Product owner: orders the squad's backlog and decides what to build next, working with other product owners on the tribe's roadmap. The product owner does not tell the squad how to do the work.
  • Agile coach: helps the squad improve its way of working, for example by running retrospectives and sprint planning and offering one-to-one coaching. In Scrum terms this resembles the Scrum Master accountability, although a coach often supports more than one squad.
  • Squad members: developers, testers, designers, data specialists and whoever else the mission needs. Each also belongs to a chapter.

There is no formally appointed squad leader. Leadership comes from the product owner on priorities, from the members on technical decisions and from the coach on process, much like the self-managing team in the 2020 Scrum Guide, which says team members "internally decide who does what, when, and how".

Squad missions

Each squad owns a long-term mission rather than a project: "make it easy for travellers to find the right hotel" or "keep payment fraud below the agreed threshold" outlasts any single feature. A good mission is customer-facing or platform-facing, is stable for at least a year, and lets the squad release most changes without waiting for another squad.

Squad size is not fixed in the paper. If the squad runs Scrum, the Scrum team guidance of "typically 10 or fewer people" applies, which sits comfortably with the 6–12 range Atlassian gives.

Tribes: groups of related squads

A tribe is a collection of squads working in a related area, such as a music player, backend infrastructure or payments. Each tribe acts as an "incubator" for its squads, and the paper sizes tribes at under about 100 people, citing Dunbar's number: beyond that size people stop knowing one another and, in the authors' view, bureaucracy and politics creep in.

The tribe lead is responsible for giving the squads the best possible environment: clear context, a sensible split of missions, space to work together and help when squads need something from outside the tribe. In many adoptions the tribe lead also manages the product owners, though this varies.

How tribes handle dependencies

Squads in a tribe still depend on one another. Spotify used two lightweight mechanisms, according to the 2012 description. First, squads regularly report which other squads they depend on and how badly that blocks them, so leaders can see which dependencies to remove by changing missions or code ownership. Second, a Scrum of Scrums is run only when a piece of work needs it, such as a daily sync for a multi-squad release, rather than as a standing meeting for everyone.

Tip

Treat a recurring cross-squad dependency as a design problem, not a meeting problem. If two squads must coordinate every week, move the shared component into one squad or redraw the missions.

Chapters and the chapter lead

A chapter is the group of people with similar skills working in the same tribe: all the backend developers, all the testers, all the designers. The chapter lead is their line manager, responsible for one-to-ones, career development, salary and hiring, and for consistent practices within that skill. The chapter lead also works in a squad, so they stay close to the real work.

This creates a two-way matrix. A tester's day-to-day work is directed by the squad and its product owner; their development, pay and professional standards sit with the chapter lead. Kniberg and Ivarsson call the matrix "weighted towards delivery": the squad is the primary grouping and the chapter is a support structure. The paper links this to the "professor and entrepreneur" model recommended by Mary and Tom Poppendieck, in which the product owner pushes for fast delivery and the chapter lead pushes for quality, and the tension between them is expected to be healthy.

Chapters typically meet regularly to agree coding or testing standards, review tooling, share problems and plan recruitment. A useful test of a chapter is whether a squad member could explain where to go for help with a skill question that their squad cannot answer.

Guilds: organisation-wide communities

A guild is an informal, voluntary community of people who share an interest, such as web development, test automation, accessibility or machine learning. Unlike a chapter, a guild cuts across every tribe and anyone can join, including people outside the skill: a product owner might join the accessibility guild. Each guild has a guild coordinator who organises meetings, mailing lists or unconferences, and members drift in and out as their interests change.

Guilds are what the wider literature calls communities of practice. They survive when they solve a problem members feel, such as "our five web squads keep building the same date picker", and they wither when attendance is mandatory or when nobody acts on what they discuss. Give a guild a small budget of time, a coordinator and a visible output, such as a shared library or a short standards page, rather than a standing weekly meeting.

Other roles: system owners, the chief architect, trios and alliances

Two architecture roles appear in the 2012 paper, and later descriptions added two leadership groupings. The latter come from secondary sources such as Atlassian's guide to the model, not from the 2012 paper.

System owner2012 paperOne person, or ideally a developer and operations pair, who is the point of contact for a system's technical integrity and architecture. Not a gatekeeper: any squad may change the system.
Chief architect2012 paperCoordinates high-level architecture across systems and reviews new designs. The advice is not binding; the squad building the system makes the final call.

Worked example: reorganising a 61-person product organisation

The following scenario is illustrative. A hypothetical online travel-booking company has 61 people in product and technology, currently arranged in functional departments (backend, frontend, QA, design, data) with projects staffed from each. Releases wait on hand-offs between departments, and nobody owns the customer journey end to end. Leadership wants long-lived teams, but people also need clear line management and a home for their craft.

Step 1: missions and squads

The leadership team lists the long-term outcomes customers and the business need, then groups them into two tribes by mission rather than by headcount.

The squads total 29 people in Traveller Experience and 27 in Supply and Payments, so 56 people work in squads. Add two tribe leads and three agile coaches (each supporting two or three squads) and the total is 56 + 2 + 3 = 61. Both tribes are far below the paper's ceiling of about 100, which is fine: the size limit is a maximum, and the split follows two distinct missions, not a target headcount.

Step 2: chapters and line management

Counting each skill per tribe gives the candidate chapters. Traveller Experience has 8 backend developers, 10 frontend and mobile developers, 4 testers and 3 designers. Supply and Payments has 13 backend developers, 7 data specialists and 3 testers. Two groups need a design decision:

  • Testing (4 + 3): two chapters of three and four would be thin, so the company forms one quality chapter of 7 across both tribes. This is a deliberate deviation from the paper, where chapters sit inside a tribe; it is recorded as such.
  • Backend in Supply and Payments (13): one chapter lead with 12 reports who also works in a squad is heavy (see the calculation below), so it is split into an integration backend chapter (partner integrations and pricing, 4 + 3 = 7 people) and a payments and platform backend chapter (4 + 2 = 6 people).

That gives seven chapters: backend (8), frontend and mobile (10) and design (3) in Traveller Experience; integration backend (7), payments and platform backend (6) and data (7) in Supply and Payments; and quality (7) across both. The eight product owners report to their tribe lead. Every squad member now has exactly one line manager, and every chapter lead is also a working member of a squad.

Step 3: check the chapter lead's load

A chapter lead's management time comes out of their squad's capacity, so the company estimates it before confirming chapter sizes.

Formula: weekly management hours H = (R × m ÷ 60 ÷ f) + s + d

  • R = direct reports (chapter members minus the lead), in people.
  • m = length of each one-to-one, in minutes (assumed 45).
  • f = weeks between one-to-ones (assumed 2, i.e. fortnightly).
  • s = weekly chapter meeting, in hours (assumed 1).
  • d = other people-management work such as hiring, development plans and pay reviews, in hours per week (assumed 3).

For the original 13-person backend chapter: R = 12, so one-to-ones take 12 × 45 ÷ 60 ÷ 2 = 4.5 hours a week. H = 4.5 + 1 + 3 = 8.5 hours a week, which is 22.7% of a 37.5-hour week and leaves the lead 29 hours (77.3%) for squad work. After the split, the 7-person chapter lead has R = 6 and H = 2.25 + 1 + 3 = 6.25 hours (16.7%), and the 6-person chapter lead has R = 5 and H = 1.875 + 1 + 3 = 5.875 hours, about 5.9 hours (15.7%).

Interpretation: compared with one lead carrying 12 reports (8.5 hours), each lead after the split spends 2.25 or about 2.6 hours a week less on management, at the cost of an extra manager. The squad that hosts a chapter lead should plan for roughly a sixth less capacity from that person and say so in its capacity planning. Limitations: the inputs are assumptions; real load spikes during hiring rounds and performance reviews, and a new manager needs more time than an experienced one. Re-check after the first review cycle.

Step 4: guilds and the first 90 days

No guilds are imposed. The company announces that anyone can start one and offers a coordinator two hours a week. Within the first quarter, three form around problems people already feel: web performance, test automation and data quality. In the first 90 days the tribe leads run the dependency report every fortnight, the chapters agree a short list of shared standards, and each squad holds retrospectives on the new structure itself, not only on its delivery.

How to adapt the Spotify model step by step

The steps below start from problems and missions and only reach the labels at the end. Our guide to Agile transformation covers the funding, governance and HR changes that a larger restructure also needs.

  1. Name the problems. Write down the specific symptoms the new structure must fix, such as slow hand-offs, unclear ownership or inconsistent engineering practice. If the list is short, a smaller change may do.
  2. Define long-term missions. List the customer journeys, products and platforms the organisation must support for the next year or more, and turn each into a mission one squad can own.
  3. Map skills. Use a skills matrix template to rate everyone's skills (0–4) and check that each proposed squad has what it needs and no single expert is spread across several squads.
  4. Group squads into tribes. Put squads with related missions and frequent dependencies together. Keep each tribe within the paper's Dunbar-based limit and give it a tribe lead.
  5. Design chapters and line management. Count each skill per tribe, decide where small groups merge or large ones split, and estimate each chapter lead's management load.
  6. Write the decision rights. Agree who decides priorities, technical standards, architecture, hiring and cross-tribe trade-offs, and publish it as a RACI.
  7. Define alignment mechanisms. Decide how company goals reach squads, for example quarterly objectives in an OKR template, and how dependencies are surfaced and resolved.
  8. Pilot with one tribe. Run the structure for at least a quarter with one tribe before rolling it out. According to the 2012 paper, Spotify introduced its structure gradually over the preceding year rather than in one step.
  9. Let guilds emerge. Offer time and a coordinator role; do not assign guild membership.
  10. Inspect and adapt the structure itself. Review dependency reports, delivery lead time, engagement and attrition each quarter and change missions or chapters when the evidence says so.

Benefits of the Spotify model

  • Ownership: a long-lived squad with a stable mission accumulates knowledge of its users and code, so decisions get faster and better informed over time.
  • Fewer hand-offs: cross-functional squads can release without waiting for a separate testing or operations department.
  • A home for each craft: chapters give specialists a manager who understands their work, which a product owner rarely can.
  • Knowledge flows across silos: guilds spread tools and practices between tribes without a central standards body.
  • Flexible process: squads can pick the method that suits their work rather than following one prescribed framework.

These benefits depend on conditions the paper took for granted, chiefly high trust, a modern release pipeline and missions that let squads release independently. Without them the same labels produce none of the benefits.

Criticism and limitations

Some of the sharpest criticism of the model comes from people who worked at or with Spotify.

  • It was a snapshot, not a framework. In a June 2015 blog post Kniberg wrote that the material "wasn't actually intended to be a generic framework" and described himself as "just the messenger" for ideas many Spotify people developed. In 2016 InfoQ reported a talk by Spotify chapter lead Marcin Floryan arguing that the model was a simplified description of Spotify and not something to copy.
  • Spotify may not have worked that way either. Jeremiah Lee, who interviewed for a product role at Spotify in 2017 and later worked there, wrote in an April 2020 essay that the model was "only ever aspirational" and never fully implemented. He quoted Joakim Sundén, an agile coach at Spotify from 2011 to 2017: "Even at the time we wrote it, we weren't doing it." Lee also wrote that the material was planned as a multi-part series and that "the parts on alignment and accountability were never completed."
  • Matrix friction. Lee described engineers line-managed by chapter leads across all the squads in a tribe while product managers reported to the tribe lead, so a squad disagreement could mean negotiating with one engineering manager per specialism and then escalating to an engineering director. The chapter lead's dual role can leave nobody clearly accountable for a squad's engineering outcomes.
  • Autonomy without alignment. Squads optimise for their own mission unless company priorities and cross-team processes are explicit. The 2012 paper says little about how priorities are set across tribes.
  • Inconsistent practices. Letting each squad choose its process makes it harder for people to move between squads and for leaders to compare progress.

None of this makes the ideas worthless. Kniberg also wrote that he had not seen a company end up worse off after adapting the ideas, and that some had seen large improvements. The sound conclusion is narrower: copy the problems Spotify was solving and test your own answers, rather than copying the org chart. The bank ING is a widely cited example of an organisation that adopted squads and tribes after studying other companies and then designed its own model; see the ING example in our Agile transformation guide.

Spotify model vs SAFe, LeSS and Scrum@Scale

The Spotify model mostly describes structure and line management. SAFe, LeSS and Scrum@Scale mostly describe how several teams plan and deliver together. That difference explains why some organisations use chapters and guilds alongside a delivery framework.

Is your organisation ready? A checklist

Answer each question honestly before relabelling anything. Three or more "no" answers suggest fixing those conditions first.

  • Can most teams release to customers without waiting for another team or a central department?
  • Are there at least two or three long-term missions per proposed tribe that will still matter in a year?
  • Are leaders willing to move decisions about how work is done to the squads?
  • Will HR support line management by skill, with chapter leads who also do delivery work?
  • Is there a clear way for company priorities to reach every squad, such as quarterly objectives?
  • Is there enough coaching capacity for the number of squads? (In the worked example above, each coach supports two or three squads.)
  • Will you measure the change (lead time, dependency blocks, engagement) and adapt the structure?

Common mistakes when copying the Spotify model

  • Starting with the labels. Calling departments tribes and teams squads changes nothing if budgets, approvals and line management stay as they were, and renaming first tells people the change is cosmetic. Start with missions and decision rights.
  • Squads that cannot release alone. If squads still depend on a central release or testing team, they are not autonomous whatever they are called. Fix the pipeline and ownership first.
  • Overloaded chapter leads. A lead with a dozen reports and a full squad role ends up doing neither well. Estimate the load and split chapters when needed.
  • No alignment mechanism. Autonomy needs shared goals and visible priorities. Without them squads optimise locally.
  • Mandatory guilds. Compulsory attendance turns a community into another meeting. Keep guilds voluntary and outcome-focused.
  • Ignoring the project and programme layer. Cross-tribe initiatives still need someone to coordinate them. Decide who does this; Agile portfolio management and the Agile PMO offer options.
  • Treating 2012 as current. The paper describes Spotify as it was in 2012. Do not present it as how Spotify works today.

What happens to managers and project managers

The model has no project manager role. Line management moves to chapter leads, priorities to product owners and process improvement to agile coaches, so existing managers usually take one of those roles or move into tribe or portfolio leadership. Project managers often become product owners, tribe-level delivery leads or coordinators of cross-tribe initiatives. The servant leadership style that coaches and tribe leads need is a different skill set from task assignment, and our guide on how to manage Agile teams covers the shift in practice.

Tools that support squads and tribes

No tool makes an organisation work like Spotify, but squads need their own board and tribe leads need a roll-up view of work and dependencies across squads. Jira (8.6 out of 10 in our review, best for software and Agile teams) covers squad backlogs, Scrum and Kanban boards and releases; Wrike, our top-rated tool at 9.4, suits organisations that need cross-department visibility and reporting across many teams. Configure either around missions and squads, not the old departments, or the tool will quietly preserve the structure you are trying to leave.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Spotify model in Agile?

A way of organising Agile teams into squads (cross-functional delivery teams), tribes (groups of related squads), chapters (same-skill groups with a line manager) and guilds (voluntary communities), described in a 2012 paper by Henrik Kniberg and Anders Ivarsson about Spotify's engineering organisation.

What is the difference between a squad and a tribe?

A squad is one small, cross-functional team with its own mission. A tribe is a group of squads working in a related area, led by a tribe lead and kept under about 100 people in the original paper.

What is the difference between a chapter and a guild?

A chapter is a formal group of people with the same skill inside one tribe, led by a chapter lead who is their line manager. A guild is an informal, voluntary community of interest that anyone across the organisation can join.

Who does a squad member report to?

In the original description, to their chapter lead, who handles line management, career development and pay. Day-to-day priorities come from the squad's product owner.

How big should a squad be?

The 2012 paper does not set a number. Atlassian describes squads of 6 to 12 people, and squads using Scrum should follow the Scrum Guide's typically 10 or fewer.

How big is a tribe?

The paper keeps tribes under about 100 people, citing Dunbar's number. Atlassian gives a range of 40 to 150.

Is a squad the same as a Scrum team?

Similar but not identical. A squad is cross-functional and self-managing like a Scrum team, but it may use Kanban or another method, has an agile coach rather than necessarily a Scrum Master, and its members are line-managed through chapters.

Is the Spotify model a framework?

No. Kniberg wrote in 2015 that it was not intended as a generic framework, and Spotify staff have described it as a simplified snapshot of one company. Treat it as a case study to learn from.

Does Spotify still use the Spotify model?

Spotify's organisation has changed since 2012, and former employees such as Jeremiah Lee have argued that it was never fully implemented as described. There is no current official description to point to, so do not treat the 2012 paper as how Spotify works today.

What is a trio in the Spotify model?

A later addition described by Atlassian and others: a tribe lead, product lead and design lead who keep a tribe's business, product and design work aligned. Several trios working on a shared goal form an alliance. Neither appears in the 2012 paper.
