The Agile Product Manager: Role, Skills and Examples

An Agile product manager decides what a team should build and why, then uses short delivery cycles and continuous customer feedback to check that the decision was right. They connect business strategy, customers and the delivery team, and they are judged on outcomes such as adoption and retention rather than on the number of features shipped. This guide explains the role, how it differs from the Scrum Product Owner and the project manager, and the techniques you will use every week, with worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • An Agile product manager owns the why and the what: product vision, strategy, roadmap and the outcomes the team is trying to achieve.
  • The Scrum Guide does not define a product manager. It defines the Product Owner accountability, and in many organisations one person holds both.
  • In larger organisations a product manager usually works outward (market, strategy, roadmap) while one or more Product Owners work inward with delivery teams on the backlog.
  • Good Agile product managers run discovery and delivery in parallel, so ideas are tested cheaply before the team commits real effort to them.
  • Prioritisation techniques such as RICE, cost of delay, WSJF, MoSCoW and Kano support decisions; they do not replace judgement.
  • The usual route in is through an adjacent role (Product Owner, business analyst, designer, engineer or project manager) plus deliberate practice in customer research and data.

What is an Agile product manager?

An Agile product manager is the person responsible for a product's value: understanding customers and the market, setting the product vision and strategy, deciding which problems to solve next, and measuring whether the solutions worked. They work with a cross-functional team in short iterations, using evidence from each release to adjust the plan rather than following a fixed specification.

The word "Agile" changes how the job is done more than what it is. A traditional product manager might write a large requirements document, hand it over and wait months for a release. An Agile product manager breaks the strategy into small, testable bets, stays close to the team every day and expects to change direction when the data says so. That approach follows the Agile Manifesto preference for customer collaboration and responding to change.

It is worth being precise about terminology. The 2020 Scrum Guide defines three accountabilities: Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. It does not mention a product manager. "Agile product manager" is therefore an organisational job title, not a framework role, and what it covers varies between companies. The sections below describe the most common pattern.

What does an Agile product manager do?

Most of the work falls into six areas. The balance between them shifts with the size of the organisation and the maturity of the product, but a capable Agile product manager is active in all six.

1. Set the product vision and strategy

The vision describes the future the product is trying to create and for whom. The strategy explains how you will get there: target customers, the problems you will solve, how you will win against alternatives and how the product supports business goals. In Scrum terms, this strategy is broken into a Product Goal, the long-term objective the Scrum Team plans against.

2. Understand customers and the market

You cannot prioritise well without evidence. Agile product managers run or commission customer interviews, usability tests, surveys, support-ticket analysis and competitor reviews. A competitor analysis template helps keep market research structured and shareable.

3. Own the roadmap and the outcomes

The product manager usually owns the Agile roadmap: a living view of the problems the team will tackle and the outcomes it expects, ordered by priority rather than locked to dates. Each roadmap item should link to a measurable result, often expressed as an objective and key results using an OKR template.

4. Shape and prioritise the backlog

Roadmap themes are broken into epics and then into user stories that sit on the Product Backlog. Whether the product manager orders the backlog directly or delegates that to a Product Owner, they set the priorities it reflects.

5. Work with the delivery team

Agile product managers attend backlog refinement to explain the problem behind each story, answer questions during the Sprint, help shape the Sprint Goal at Sprint Planning and use the Sprint Review to gather feedback from stakeholders. They do not assign tasks or tell Developers how to build things.

6. Measure results and communicate

After each release the product manager checks the data: did the change move the metric it was meant to move? They then decide whether to iterate, scale up or stop, and explain those decisions to leadership, sales, support and customers.

Agile product manager vs Product Owner vs project manager

These three roles are often confused, and job adverts mix them freely. The clearest way to separate them is by the question each one answers.

  • Product manager: Are we building the right thing for the market and the business?
  • Product Owner: Is the team's backlog ordered to deliver the most value, Sprint by Sprint?
  • Project manager: Will this piece of work be delivered within its agreed constraints, and are dependencies, budget and risk under control?

The Scrum Guide describes the Product Owner as accountable for maximising the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team, including developing and communicating the Product Goal and ordering the Product Backlog. It says the Product Owner is one person, not a committee, and may delegate work to others while remaining accountable.

When one person does both product roles

In start-ups and small product teams, the product manager is usually also the Product Owner. That works well when there is one team and one product: there is a single voice on priorities and no hand-off between strategy and backlog. The risk is overload. A product manager who spends all day in refinement stops talking to customers, and the strategy goes stale. If you hold both roles, protect at least a few hours a week for discovery.

How the split works in larger organisations

With several teams on one product, a common pattern is one product manager setting strategy and the roadmap, with a Product Owner embedded in each team turning that direction into a backlog. The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) formalises this: it describes a Product Management function that owns the backlog for an Agile Release Train and works with team-level Product Owners, with priorities agreed at PI Planning. Other scaling approaches, such as LeSS, prefer one Product Owner across several teams.

Discovery and delivery: the dual-track loop

Agile product managers work in two loops at once. Discovery tests ideas cheaply, through interviews, prototypes and small experiments, to learn which ones are worth building. Delivery builds, tests and releases the ideas that survive discovery. Running both continuously, often called dual-track Agile, means the delivery team always has validated work ready and the product manager is always learning about the next problem.

The two tracks are not separate teams. Designers and at least one Developer usually take part in discovery, so that technical constraints and usability are considered early and nobody receives a surprise hand-off.

Step-by-step: from outcome to learning

  1. Pick an outcome, not a feature. Start from a measurable goal on the roadmap, such as "increase the share of new users who complete a first booking".
  2. Find out why the metric is where it is. Look at funnel data, session recordings and support tickets, then interview five to eight customers who dropped out.
  3. List the opportunities. Write down each customer problem you heard, in their words. Group duplicates.
  4. Generate several solutions per opportunity. Involve the designer and a Developer. Avoid jumping to the first idea.
  5. State the riskiest assumption. For each promising solution, ask what must be true for it to work, and which assumption you are least sure of.
  6. Test the assumption as cheaply as possible. Use a clickable prototype, a fake-door button, a concierge test or a one-week experiment.
  7. Move proven ideas into delivery. Write user stories with clear acceptance criteria and order them on the backlog.
  8. Release in small slices and measure. Compare the metric with the baseline after release.
  9. Decide: iterate, scale or stop. Share the result at the Sprint Review and feed it into the next round of discovery.

Worked example: reducing drop-off in a booking app

Imagine you are the product manager for a fitness-class booking app. The figures below are illustrative. Analytics show that about 40% of new users who reach the payment screen leave without booking. Your quarterly outcome is to raise first-booking completion.

Prioritisation techniques with worked examples

Every Agile product manager has more good ideas than capacity. Scoring techniques make the trade-offs visible and give stakeholders a consistent way to challenge your choices. Use them to structure the conversation, then apply judgement.

The examples below use the same four backlog items from the booking app so you can compare the methods.

RICE scoring

RICE, popularised by the software company Intercom, scores each item as Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Reach is how many users the change affects in a period; Impact is a scale (Intercom uses 3 for massive, 2 for high, 1 for medium, 0.5 for low and 0.25 for minimal); Confidence is a percentage; Effort is in person-months.

ItemReach (users per quarter)ImpactConfidenceEffort (person-months)RICE score
Booking reminders by text message3,000180%12,400
Guest checkout4,000280%32,133
Saved payment cards2,5001100%21,250
Dark mode6,0000.2550%1750

Reminders edge out guest checkout because they are cheap to build, even though guest checkout has the higher impact. That is a useful prompt for discussion: if guest checkout is the item most closely tied to this quarter's outcome, you might still choose it first. RICE is good at exposing that kind of trade-off.

Cost of delay and WSJF

Cost of delay asks what it costs, each week, not to have something. It is especially useful when timing matters, such as a regulatory deadline or a seasonal peak. Dividing cost of delay by duration (sometimes called CD3) tells you which order minimises total loss.

Suppose feature A would earn £10,000 a week and takes four weeks to build, while feature B would earn £4,000 a week and takes one week. The figures are illustrative.

SequenceWeeks A is delayedWeeks B is delayedTotal cost of delay
A first, then B4 (£40,000)5 (£20,000)£60,000
B first, then A5 (£50,000)1 (£4,000)£54,000

Doing the smaller item first saves £6,000, even though A is worth more per week. CD3 gives the same answer: A scores 10,000 ÷ 4 = 2,500 and B scores 4,000 ÷ 1 = 4,000.

When money is hard to estimate, SAFe uses Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF), which estimates cost of delay as the sum of three relative scores (user or business value, time criticality, and risk reduction or opportunity enablement) and divides by job size.

MoSCoW for a release

MoSCoW sorts items into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time. It works best for agreeing the scope of a fixed-date release with stakeholders. A practical rule is to keep Must haves to a clear minority of the planned effort so the team has room to absorb surprises.

CategoryBooking app release example
Must haveGuest checkout, payment confirmation email
Should haveBooking reminders by text message
Could haveSaved payment cards
Won't have this timeDark mode

Kano model

The Kano model, developed by Noriaki Kano, classifies features by how they affect satisfaction. Basic needs cause dissatisfaction when missing but little delight when present (a working payment page). Performance features increase satisfaction the better they are (faster booking). Delighters are unexpected and create disproportionate satisfaction (a free first class). Use it to make sure the roadmap fixes basics before chasing delighters.

TechniqueBest forWatch out for
RICEComparing many unrelated ideasFalse precision; confidence scores can be optimistic

MoSCoWAgreeing release scope with stakeholdersEverything becoming a Must have

Building an outcome-based Agile roadmap

An Agile roadmap is a communication tool, not a contract. The most robust format for product managers is now, next, later: what the team is working on now, what it expects to tackle next and what is further out. Items further out are less detailed and more likely to change. Each item states the problem and the outcome rather than a specific feature.

Now (this quarter)Next (next quarter)Later
ThemeFirst-booking conversionRetention after first classStudio partner growth
OutcomeRaise first-booking completionIncrease second bookings within 30 daysGrow the number of active partner studios
Candidate solutionsGuest checkout, remindersClass recommendations, streaksSelf-service studio onboarding
ConfidenceHigh: tested in discoveryMedium: early interviewsLow: hypothesis

Share the roadmap openly, review it at least monthly and explain changes when you make them. If leadership needs dates, give ranges and tie them to the release plan rather than adding dates to the roadmap itself.

Metrics an Agile product manager uses

Agile product managers are measured on outcomes. Output measures, such as features shipped or story points completed, show activity but not value. Pair a small set of outcome metrics with a few flow measures so you can see both whether the product is working and whether the team can deliver.

How to become an Agile product manager

There is no single entry route. Most product managers move across from a neighbouring role, bringing one strong skill and building the rest on the job.

  1. Start from your strength. Engineers bring technical depth, designers bring customer insight, analysts bring data skills and project managers bring delivery and stakeholder skills. Identify the gaps you need to close.
  2. Learn the Agile foundations. Read the Scrum Guide and the Agile Manifesto, and understand how a Scrum Team and a Kanban system work. Our guide to Scrum team roles is a good starting point.
  3. Get close to customers. Volunteer to sit in on interviews, read support tickets and analyse feedback in your current job.
  4. Take on Product Owner responsibilities. Offer to own a small backlog, write user stories or run a Sprint Review. Practical experience counts for more than titles.
  5. Build a portfolio of outcomes. Record problems you tackled, what you tried and the measurable result.
  6. Consider a certification for the Scrum side. Product Owner credentials such as Scrum.org's Professional Scrum Product Owner (PSPO) or Scrum Alliance's Certified Scrum Product Owner (CSPO) teach backlog and value management. In SAFe organisations, the SAFe Product Owner/Product Manager (POPM) course is common. These show knowledge of a framework; they do not replace product experience.
  7. Apply for associate or Product Owner roles and move up as you build a record of results.

Templates and tools for Agile product managers

You do not need specialist software to start. A shared roadmap, an ordered backlog and a place to record research will cover most needs. These free templates are a good set:

  • Product roadmap template for themes, initiatives and quarters.
  • Product backlog template for ordered stories and estimates.
  • User story template for consistent stories and acceptance criteria.
  • Go-to-market plan template for launches.
  • OKR template for outcome goals.

As the team grows, a work management tool helps link roadmap items to delivery. Software teams often use Jira, which is built around backlogs, Sprints and boards. Where product work needs to be coordinated with marketing, operations or several departments, Wrike is a strong option because it combines Agile boards with portfolio views and cross-team reporting.

Frequently asked questions

What does an Agile product manager do?

Understands customers and the market, defines the product vision and strategy, owns the roadmap, sets outcome measures, prioritises work with the delivery team and measures results after each release.

Does an Agile team need an Agile product manager?

It needs someone who owns value and priorities. In Scrum that is the Product Owner. Whether that person is also called a product manager depends on the organisation; with several teams on one product, a separate product manager is common.

What is the difference between an Agile product manager and a Product Owner?

A product manager is an organisational job focused on market, strategy and the roadmap. The Product Owner is a Scrum accountability for maximising the value of the team's work through the Product Backlog. One person often holds both in smaller organisations.

Is an Agile product manager the same as a project manager?

No. The product manager decides what to build and why and is measured on outcomes. A project manager coordinates delivery, budget, risk and dependencies so that agreed work is delivered. See Agile project manager.

Does Scrum have a product manager role?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide defines the Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. Product manager is a job title organisations use alongside or instead of the Product Owner.

What is product discovery for an Agile product manager?

Testing ideas cheaply through interviews, prototypes and experiments to learn what is worth building before committing delivery effort.

What is RICE prioritisation for an Agile product manager?

A scoring method: Reach multiplied by Impact multiplied by Confidence, divided by Effort. Higher scores suggest higher priority, but the scores should start a discussion rather than end it.

How does an Agile product manager use cost of delay?

By estimating what it costs each week not to have a feature, then dividing by its duration to decide the order that loses the least value overall. WSJF is a relative version used in SAFe.

How does an Agile product manager use the Kano model?

To classify features as basic needs, performance features or delighters based on customer research, so the roadmap fixes basics before investing in delighters.

Does the Agile product manager own the roadmap?

Usually yes. They create and communicate the Agile roadmap, with input from leadership, customers and the team.

Does an Agile product manager write user stories?

Often, or they write them with the team or a Product Owner. What matters is that each story is clear, linked to customer value and has agreed acceptance criteria.

How is an Agile product manager measured?

By outcomes: customer and business results such as activation, retention, revenue or satisfaction, rather than the number of features shipped.

How do you become an Agile product manager?

Move across from a neighbouring role, learn Agile and Scrum, build customer research and data skills, take on Product Owner responsibilities and record the outcomes you achieve.

Which certifications help an Agile product manager?

Product Owner certifications such as PSPO (Scrum.org) and CSPO (Scrum Alliance), or SAFe POPM in SAFe organisations, cover the Scrum and scaling side. Product management itself has no single standard credential, so experience matters most.
