Agile Capacity Planning: Formula, Steps and Example

Agile capacity planning is the practice of working out how much work a team can realistically take on in a sprint, release or quarter, based on who is available and how much the team has delivered before. Done well, it stops overcommitment, protects a sustainable pace and makes forecasts believable. This guide gives you the formulas, a fully worked five-person example, and the adjustments for holidays, part-timers, new joiners and unplanned work.

Key takeaways

  • Capacity is the time a team has available; velocity is how much it has historically finished. Use velocity as the baseline and capacity as the adjustment.
  • Sprint capacity = available person-days × realistic focus hours, minus Scrum events and an allowance for unplanned work.
  • Scale average velocity by this sprint's capacity compared with a normal sprint, and plan to a range rather than a single number.
  • Historical velocity already includes past interruptions, so do not subtract an unplanned-work buffer from it a second time.
  • Check capacity by skill as well as in total: one overloaded specialist can sink a sprint that looks fine on paper.
  • The same logic scales up: releases are forecast as ranges, and portfolios are planned in team-sprints.

What is Agile capacity planning?

Agile capacity planning is the process of estimating how much work an Agile team can complete in a coming time-box, usually a sprint, by combining each person's availability with the team's past delivery rate. Its output is a realistic forecast, in hours or story points, that the team uses to decide how much work to commit to.

It is not a separate ceremony. Capacity planning happens inside events you already run: at the start of Sprint Planning, during Agile release planning, in PI Planning if you use SAFe, and in quarterly portfolio reviews.

The Scrum Guide (2020, still the current official edition) does not prescribe a capacity formula, but it does say that the more the Developers know about their past performance, their upcoming capacity and their Definition of Done, the more confident they will be in their Sprint forecasts. Those three inputs are exactly what this guide works with.

Capacity planning matters for three reasons:

  • Sustainable pace. One of the twelve principles behind the Agile Manifesto asks teams to maintain a constant pace indefinitely. Planning to real capacity is how you do that.
  • Predictability. Stakeholders can plan launches, marketing and training around forecasts that hold up sprint after sprint.
  • Trust. A team that regularly meets its Sprint Goal earns the freedom to manage its own work. A team that regularly misses it attracts micromanagement.

Agile capacity planning is a team-level version of a broader discipline. For organisation-wide resource planning, see our guide to capacity planning.

Capacity vs velocity and other key terms

Most capacity mistakes come from mixing up a handful of terms. The table sets them side by side.

TermWhat it measuresUnitExample
CapacityThe time the team has available for sprint work in a given periodHours or person-days42 person-days this sprint
VelocityThe amount of work the team has actually finished per sprint, on averageStory points (or items) per sprint31 points per sprint
Load (demand)The work the team is being asked to doHours or points45 points of candidate backlog items
Focus factorThe share of available time that goes on planned sprint workPercentage or ratio0.7 (70%)
UtilisationHours booked against hours availablePercentage85% of hours booked
ThroughputItems finished per period, used mainly in KanbanItems per week9 items per week

The working rule is simple: velocity tells you what a normal sprint produces; capacity tells you how this sprint differs from normal. Velocity without capacity ignores the fact that two people are on holiday. Capacity without velocity tells you how many hours you have but not how much work fits into them.

Who plans capacity and when

Capacity planning happens at several levels, with different people and units at each one.

LevelWhenWho is involvedTypical unitOutput
SprintStart of every Sprint Planning (prepare availability the day before)Developers, facilitated by the Scrum Master; Product Owner brings prioritiesHours, person-days or story pointsSprint Backlog that fits the team
ReleaseRelease planning, then refreshed every sprintProduct Owner with the teamStory points per sprint, as a rangeForecast of scope by date, or date for scope
Quarter or PIQuarterly planning or PI PlanningTeams, Product Management, Release Train Engineer in SAFePoints per iterationCommitted objectives per team
PortfolioQuarterly portfolio reviewPortfolio leads, product leaders, PMOTeam-sprints or teams per quarterWhich initiatives are funded now and which wait

In Scrum, the Developers decide how much work they can take on. The Scrum Master helps them use good data and keeps the conversation honest; the Product Owner explains which items matter most but does not set the amount. A manager calculating capacity on the team's behalf and handing it down misses the point.

The sprint capacity formula

There is no official formula, but four versions are in common use. They are the same idea seen from different angles, and the worked example below shows that they give consistent answers.

MethodFormulaBest for
HoursΣ (available days × focus hours per day) − Scrum events − unplanned-work allowanceTeams that plan tasks in hours; checking specialist workloads
Person-daysΣ (sprint days − absences) × allocation to the teamQuick availability check; the input to every other method
Velocity scalingAverage velocity × (available person-days ÷ normal person-days)Teams that estimate in story points
Focus factorAvailable person-days × (past velocity ÷ past person-days)The same as velocity scaling, expressed per person-day

What is a realistic focus factor?

The focus factor is the share of someone's working time that actually goes on planned sprint work, once email, support, meetings outside the team, context switching and breaks are taken out. Practitioner guidance commonly puts it between 0.6 and 0.8 (60–80%), with newer teams and teams with heavy support duties at the lower end. That range is a starting point, not a standard: the most reliable focus factor is the one you measure from your own last few sprints.

Tip

A quick way to find your real focus factor: for two sprints, log roughly how many hours each person spent on Sprint Backlog items. Divide by their available hours. Most teams are surprised by how low the number is.

How to calculate sprint capacity step by step

Use this sequence before or at the start of Sprint Planning. Steps 1 to 5 give you capacity in hours; steps 6 and 7 turn it into a story-point forecast; step 8 checks it is achievable in practice.

  1. List everyone on the team and the number of working days in the sprint (10 for a two-week sprint, minus any public holidays).
  2. Subtract absences: annual leave, training, conferences, planned sick leave, on-call days and other known commitments.
  3. Apply each person's allocation. Someone split 50/50 with another team has half the days.
  4. Multiply by realistic focus hours per day. Few people manage more than five or six hours a day of focused sprint work.
  5. Subtract Scrum events and set aside an allowance for unplanned work, based on what actually arrived in recent sprints.
  6. Compare available person-days with a normal sprint (one with no unusual absences) to get a capacity ratio.
  7. Scale your average velocity by that ratio and express the result as a range, using your lowest and highest recent sprints.
  8. Sanity-check by skill and by item. Make sure no single person or skill is overloaded, and that the items selected can genuinely be finished to the Definition of Done.

Keep the arithmetic in a shared sheet so the team can see it. Our free sprint planning template has a Capacity tab with columns for team member, available days, focus factor, capacity in days and notes, and totals it for you.

Worked example: a five-person team

Here is the whole method applied to one team. It is a two-week sprint with 10 working days and no public holidays.

Team memberRoleAbsence this sprintAllocation to teamAvailable daysNormal days
PriyaDeveloperNone100%1010
TomDeveloper2 days annual leave100%810
SaraDeveloperNone50% (shared with another product)55
LuisTesterNone100%1010
MeiDesigner1 day training100%910
Team total4245

Method 1: capacity in hours

  • Focus hours: the team uses 6 focus hours per day, so 42 days × 6 = 252 hours.
  • Scrum events: Sprint Planning (3 hours), Sprint Review (1.5), Sprint Retrospective (1.5) and backlog refinement (2) come to 8 hours per full-time person, or 4 for Sara. That is 4 × 8 + 4 = 36 hours.
  • Unplanned work: over the last three sprints, about 10% of hours went on production support and urgent requests, so the team reserves 10% of the remaining 216 hours, about 22 hours.
  • Plannable capacity: 252 − 36 − 22 ≈ 194 hours.

Method 2: scaling velocity

  • The last three sprints delivered 33, 29 and 31 points. Average velocity = 93 ÷ 3 = 31 points.
  • Those sprints all had normal availability of 45 person-days. This sprint has 42, so the capacity ratio is 42 ÷ 45 = 0.93.
  • Forecast = 31 × 0.93 ≈ 29 points. Applying the same ratio to the lowest and highest sprints (29 and 33) gives a range of about 27 to 31 points.

Method 3: focus factor per person-day

Past delivery was 31 points from 45 person-days, or about 0.69 points per person-day. This sprint: 42 × 0.69 ≈ 29 points. It is the same answer as Method 2 because it is the same calculation written differently, which is a useful check that your inputs are consistent.

Cross-check by skill

Luis is the only tester. His 10 days × 6 hours = 60 hours, minus 8 hours of events and a 10% allowance, leaves about 47 hours. When the team breaks down the candidate items, they find 58 hours of testing. Rather than overload Luis, two developers agree to pair on test automation for one story, and the team drops a low-priority item that needed heavy manual testing. Total capacity was fine; skill capacity was not.

What the team commits to

The Product Owner's top items are sized 8, 5, 5, 3, 5 and 3 points. The first five total 26 points and are enough to meet the Sprint Goal; the sixth brings the total to 29, at the top of the comfortable range. The team's task breakdown comes to about 180 hours against 194 available, so they take all six, with the Sprint Goal resting on the first five. The next refined item stays at the top of the Product Backlog in case capacity frees up.

Tip

Notice that the velocity method did not subtract the 10% unplanned-work allowance. Past velocity already reflects the interruptions that happened in past sprints. Subtracting a buffer from velocity as well would count the same interruptions twice.

Should you plan in hours or story points?

Both work. Many teams use story points to decide how much to take on, and hours or days only to check availability and specialist load.

Story points and velocityHours
What it capturesRelative size, complexity and uncertaintyEffort per task
StrengthsQuick, accounts for uncertainty, self-corrects through velocityIntuitive, easy to check against calendars, good for spotting overloaded specialists
WeaknessesAbstract for stakeholders; easy to misuse as a productivity scoreEncourages false precision and time-sheet thinking; estimates are often optimistic
Good fitStable teams with a few sprints of historyNew teams, mixed-skill teams, client work billed by the hour

Our advice: if you have at least three sprints of stable velocity, plan in points and use hours as a sanity check. If the team is new or its membership has just changed, start with hours and switch once velocity settles.

Planning for unplanned work

Few teams get a sprint free of interruptions. Production incidents, urgent customer requests and questions from other teams all eat into capacity. Ignoring them does not make them go away; it just means the Sprint Goal pays for them.

  1. Measure it. Tag unplanned items on the board for three or four sprints and record the hours or points they took.
  2. Reserve the average. If unplanned work takes 10–15% of capacity, reserve that share in the hours method.
  3. Consider a rotation. One person per sprint handles interruptions, protecting the focus of everyone else. Rotate the role so nobody is stuck with it.
  4. Make it visible. Show unplanned work as its own swimlane so the team and stakeholders can see what it costs.
  5. Reduce it at source. Use the Sprint Retrospective to tackle the causes, such as fragile code or unclear support routes. Paying down technical debt is often the best long-term fix.

If unplanned work is more than about half of what the team does, and it cannot wait until the next sprint, fixed sprints may be the wrong container. A flow-based approach with explicit WIP limits often works better; see Kanban vs Scrum.

Special cases that change capacity

Part-time members and shared specialists

Count people at their real allocation, and be honest about it. Someone nominally 50% on your team who spends most of the week firefighting elsewhere may give you 30%. Shared specialists such as UX designers, data engineers or security reviewers should be planned explicitly, because their availability often limits what the team can finish. Expressing allocations as full-time equivalents (FTE) keeps the arithmetic clear.

New team members

A new joiner usually reduces capacity for the first sprint or two, because experienced colleagues spend time helping them. Plan the newcomer at a low focus factor at first, for example 0.3 to 0.5, and lower the mentor's slightly too. Fred Brooks observed in The Mythical Man-Month that adding people to a late software project tends to make it later; the same effect applies at sprint scale.

Holidays and peak leave periods

Collect planned leave a sprint ahead, and check public holidays in every country where team members work. Around holiday seasons, consider a shorter Sprint Goal rather than a shorter sprint, because changing sprint length disrupts your velocity history.

Skill bottlenecks

Total capacity hides skill constraints, as the worked example shows. A simple skills matrix shows where one person is the only one who can do a type of work. Over time, cross-training and pairing widen the team's skills and make capacity more flexible. Swarming, where several people work on one item together, is another way to stop work queuing behind one person.

Team changes and velocity resets

When the team's membership changes significantly, old velocity becomes a poor guide. Use the hours method for two or three sprints, then rebuild a velocity baseline from the new team's actual results.

Capacity planning beyond the sprint

Release and quarter forecasts

For a release, multiply the number of sprints by a velocity range, adjusted for known capacity changes. Continuing the example: the team has six sprints in the quarter, and one falls in a holiday period with about 70% of normal capacity. That gives 5.7 sprint-equivalents.

ScenarioVelocityCalculationForecast for the quarter
Pessimistic2929 × 5.7≈ 165 points
Expected3131 × 5.7≈ 177 points
Optimistic3333 × 5.7≈ 188 points

The release backlog holds 150 points of Must-have items and 40 points of Should-have items. The Product Owner can tell stakeholders with confidence that all Must-haves will land this quarter, plus somewhere between 15 and 38 points of Should-haves. That is far more useful than a single date that is either padded or wrong. Update the forecast every sprint as real results come in. Our release plan template helps you lay this out.

SAFe and PI Planning

In the Scaled Agile Framework, each team calculates capacity for every iteration of the Program Increment during PI Planning. For teams with no velocity history, SAFe describes a starting method using normalised story points: give the team eight points for every full-time member, adjust for part-timers, and subtract one point for each vacation day or holiday. For our example team that is 8 + 8 + 4 + 8 + 8 = 36, minus 3 days of absence, or 33 points. SAFe presents this as a way to start, not a permanent rule; once the team has real velocity, it uses that instead.

SAFe also uses capacity allocation: agreeing in advance what share of capacity goes on new features versus enablers, maintenance and technical debt. The split is a policy decision for each Agile Release Train, not a fixed ratio.

Portfolio capacity

At portfolio level, the useful unit is usually the team-sprint: one stable team for one sprint. Leaders compare roadmap demand with the number of team-sprints available and decide what not to start.

ItemTeam-sprints
Supply: 4 teams × 6 sprints24
Less 20% reserved for maintenance and technical debt−5 (rounded)
Available for new initiatives19
Demand: Initiative A (8), B (6), C (5), D (4)23
DecisionFund A, B and C now (19); start D next quarter

Matching work to stable teams, rather than moving people between projects, keeps velocity meaningful and avoids the cost of constant team changes.

Kanban and flow-based teams

Kanban teams do not plan sprints, but they still manage capacity, mainly through WIP limits. Instead of velocity they use throughput, the number of items finished per week. To answer "how many items by date X?" or "when will these 30 items be done?", many teams run a Monte Carlo simulation on their historical throughput, which gives a forecast with a stated probability rather than a single number.

As you add teams, most Agile tools offer capacity or workload views, often on higher-priced plans. Azure DevOps, for example, has a capacity page for each sprint; Jira, Wrike, monday.com, ClickUp and Smartsheet offer workload or resource views that show who is over-allocated. Common capacity planning mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Planning at 100% of available hoursNo room for meetings, interruptions or problems; the Sprint Goal is missedPlan with a realistic focus factor and an unplanned-work allowance
Ignoring leave and public holidaysVelocity is applied to a sprint that has fewer people in itCollect availability before every Sprint Planning
Double-counting interruptionsSubtracting a buffer from historical velocity under-commits the teamUse the buffer in the hours method only
Treating velocity as a targetTeams inflate estimates; forecasts stop meaning anythingUse velocity only for forecasting, never as a performance score
Comparing velocity between teamsStory points are relative to each team, so comparisons are meaninglessCompare predictability and outcomes, not points
Assuming new people are fully productiveCapacity drops while they learn, and mentors slow down tooPlan newcomers and mentors at a lower focus factor
Checking total capacity onlyOne overloaded specialist blocks several itemsCheck capacity by skill during planning
Managers setting capacity for the teamCommitment without ownership; trust erodesDevelopers decide; managers provide data and remove obstacles
Single-number forecastsStakeholders hear a promise, not a forecastGive a range and update it every sprint

If capacity planning keeps failing, look at item size. Large, poorly understood items are the most common reason forecasts slip. Good backlog refinement that splits work into small, well-understood items makes every capacity calculation more accurate.

Capacity planning checklist

Run through this before your next Sprint Planning:

  • Everyone's leave, training, on-call and public holidays for the sprint are recorded.
  • Part-time and shared members are counted at their real allocation.
  • The focus factor reflects recent sprints, not an ideal week.
  • Average velocity and the range from your last three to five sprints are to hand.
  • Unplanned work from recent sprints is measured, and an allowance is agreed.
  • Candidate items are refined, sized and small enough to finish in the sprint.
  • Specialist skills are checked so nobody is overloaded.
  • The Sprint Goal can be met even if the last item selected is not finished.
  • The forecast is shown as a range, and the method is visible to the whole team.

Frequently asked questions

What is capacity planning in Agile?

Agile capacity planning is working out how much work a team can realistically take on in a sprint, release or quarter, by combining each person's availability with the team's past delivery rate. It helps the team commit to an achievable amount and give stakeholders believable forecasts.

How do you calculate sprint capacity?

List each person's available days after leave and other commitments, apply their allocation to the team, multiply by realistic focus hours per day, then subtract Scrum events and an allowance for unplanned work. To get story points, scale your average velocity by available person-days divided by a normal sprint's person-days.

What is the difference between capacity and velocity?

Capacity is the time the team has available in a period. Velocity is the work it has historically finished per sprint. Velocity is the baseline for what a normal sprint produces; capacity shows how this sprint differs from normal.

What is a focus factor?

The share of a person's working time that actually goes on planned sprint work after email, support, other meetings and context switching. A focus factor of 0.7 means 70% of available time goes on sprint work.

What is a good focus factor for a Scrum team?

Practitioner guidance commonly suggests 0.6 to 0.8, with new teams and teams carrying support work at the lower end. The best value is the one you measure from your own recent sprints.

Should capacity planning use hours or story points?

Either works. Many teams use story points and velocity to decide how much to take on, and hours or days only to check availability and specialist workloads. New teams with no velocity history often start in hours.

How do you adjust velocity for holidays?

Multiply average velocity by the ratio of available person-days to a normal sprint's person-days. If a team normally has 45 person-days, has 42 this sprint and averages 31 points, plan for about 31 × 42 ÷ 45 ≈ 29 points.

Should teams plan at 100% capacity?

No. Leave room for Scrum events, support, unplanned work and the unexpected. Planning at 100% of hours makes missing the Sprint Goal very likely and undermines a sustainable pace.

How do you handle unplanned work in capacity planning?

Measure how much arrives over a few sprints, reserve that share of hours, or rotate one person to handle interruptions. Do not also subtract it from historical velocity, which already includes past interruptions.

Who is responsible for capacity planning in Scrum?

The Developers decide how much work they can take on. The Scrum Master facilitates and helps them use good data, and the Product Owner explains priorities. Managers can provide information but should not set the team's capacity for it.

How is capacity calculated in SAFe?

Teams plan capacity for each iteration during PI Planning. For teams without velocity history, SAFe describes a starting method of eight normalised story points per full-time team member, adjusted for part-timers, minus one point per vacation day or holiday. Real velocity replaces this once available.

Does Kanban use capacity planning?

Yes, but differently. Kanban teams limit work in progress rather than planning sprints, and forecast using throughput, often with Monte Carlo simulation, rather than velocity.

How do new team members affect capacity?

They usually reduce it at first, because they are learning and colleagues spend time helping them. Plan the newcomer and their mentor at a lower focus factor for the first sprint or two.

What is portfolio capacity planning?

Matching roadmap demand with the number of stable teams and sprints available, usually counted in team-sprints, then deciding which initiatives to start now and which to defer. See our capacity planning guide for the wider discipline.

Can you change capacity during a sprint?

Capacity changes when someone falls ill or urgent work arrives. The Developers review progress at the Daily Scrum and, if needed, renegotiate scope with the Product Owner while protecting the Sprint Goal. The sprint length itself does not change.
