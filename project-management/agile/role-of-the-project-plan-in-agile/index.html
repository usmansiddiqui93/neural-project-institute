Role of the Project Plan in Agile: Levels and Example

The role of the project plan in Agile is to set direction, forecast delivery and make trade-offs visible, not to fix every task in advance. Agile projects do plan, but they replace one large baseline with several smaller plans at different levels of detail, from the product vision down to today's work, and update them as the team learns. This guide explains each planning level, what an Agile project plan contains, who owns it, how to build one step by step, and how it copes with fixed budgets, deadlines and governance boards.

Key takeaways

  • Agile projects still have a plan. It is layered (vision, roadmap, release, Sprint, daily) and gets more detailed the closer the work is.
  • The plan's job is to align people on the goal, forecast what is likely by when, show the constraints and make every change and trade-off visible.
  • In most Agile plans time and cost are held steady and scope flexes. The plan says which outcomes are committed and which are only forecast.
  • A useful Agile project plan is short: goal, success measures, scope boundaries, team and cadence, release forecast, milestones, budget, risks and reporting.
  • Forecast with a velocity range, not a single number, and re-forecast at the end of every Sprint using what the team has actually finished.
  • Governance boards can be satisfied with working increments, burnup charts, a decision log and stage reviews aligned with releases.

What role does the project plan play in Agile?

In Agile, the project plan is a living, layered forecast rather than a fixed baseline. It states the goal, the constraints and the likely path to delivery, then is refined every iteration using real progress. Its role is to align stakeholders, guide prioritisation and make trade-offs visible, while detailed task planning happens just in time.

A common myth says Agile teams do not plan. The Agile Manifesto values 'responding to change over following a plan', but it also says there is value in the items on the right. The point is not to stop planning; it is to stop treating the first plan as the truth. Agile teams usually plan more often than predictive teams, just in smaller pieces.

In practice, an Agile project plan does five jobs:

  • Aligns people on the outcome. Everyone can see the goal, the success measures and what is out of scope.
  • Forecasts delivery. It shows what is likely to be ready by when, as a range rather than a promise.
  • States the constraints. Budget, fixed dates, regulatory milestones and dependencies are written down so priorities respect them.
  • Supports governance. Sponsors and boards get a clear basis for funding, reviewing and stopping work.
  • Makes trade-offs explicit. When something changes, the plan shows what moves as a result, so decisions are made deliberately.

The document itself matters less than the planning conversations behind it. A good Agile plan fits on a few pages or a single workspace in your tool, and the team can explain it without reading it out.

Agile plan vs traditional project plan

Both approaches plan; they differ in what they fix, how much detail they commit to up front and how they handle change. The table compares a typical predictive project management plan with a typical Agile project plan.

AspectTraditional (predictive) planAgile plan
What is fixedScope, then time and cost are estimated from itUsually time and cost; scope is prioritised to fit
Detail up frontFull work breakdown and schedule before executionDetail only for the next one or two Sprints
Planning horizonThe whole projectLayered: years, quarters, months, weeks, days
How change is handledFormal change control against a baselineExpected; reprioritised in the backlog every Sprint
Measure of progressPercentage of planned tasks complete, earned valueWorking increments, outcomes and a burnup of completed scope
Forecast formA single date and costA range, updated with real velocity or throughput
Typical artefactsGantt chart, WBS, baseline schedule, plan documentVision, roadmap, release plan, Sprint Backlog, burnup

Neither is 'no plan'. Predictive plans suit work where requirements are stable and changes are expensive; Agile plans suit work where you learn by delivering. Many organisations combine both. See our comparison of Agile vs Waterfall for when each approach fits.

The five levels of Agile planning

Agile planning is often drawn as an onion, a model popularised by Mike Cohn in Agile Estimating and Planning. The outer layers cover long horizons with little detail; the inner layers cover short horizons in fine detail. Authors label the layers slightly differently (some add strategy and portfolio layers), but most teams work with these five:

LevelHorizonQuestion it answersUsually owned byTypical artefactRevisited
VisionOne to several yearsWhy are we doing this and for whom?Sponsor, product manager or Product OwnerVision statement, Product GoalRarely; when strategy changes
RoadmapQuartersWhich outcomes come first?Product Owner or product managerAgile roadmap (Now-Next-Later or by quarter)Monthly or quarterly
ReleaseOne to six monthsWhat is likely to be ready by when?Product Owner with the teamRelease plan and burnupEvery Sprint
Sprint (iteration)One to four weeksWhat will we deliver next, and how?The Scrum TeamSprint Goal and Sprint BacklogEvery Sprint Planning
DailyTodayWhat will we do next to reach the Sprint Goal?The DevelopersPlan agreed at the Daily Scrum, task boardEvery day

Vision and roadmap

The vision explains the problem and who benefits. In Scrum, the Product Goal describes a future state of the product that the team plans against. The Agile roadmap turns that goal into an ordered sequence of outcomes, usually expressed as themes or epics, with near-term items more specific than later ones.

Release planning

A release plan forecasts which backlog items are likely to be delivered by a date, or when a set of items is likely to be ready. It combines an ordered backlog, rough estimates and the team's velocity range. This is the level most sponsors mean when they ask for 'the plan'. Our guide to Agile release planning covers the forecasting maths in detail.

Sprint and daily planning

At the start of each Sprint, Sprint Planning sets a Sprint Goal, selects items from the Product Backlog and decides how to deliver them. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes the Sprint Backlog as 'a plan by and for the Developers'. The Daily Scrum then adjusts that plan every day and produces an actionable plan for the next day of work.

Tip

Detail should increase as the horizon shortens. If your roadmap lists individual tasks for next year, or your Sprint Backlog contains vague goals with no concrete work, the levels have been mixed up.

What an Agile project plan contains

When a sponsor, PMO or client asks for an Agile project plan, they usually need one short document or workspace that ties the levels together. These are the components most organisations expect.

ComponentWhat to writeExample (illustrative)
Goal and problemThe outcome in one or two sentences and why it mattersLet policyholders track claims online so that status calls fall
Success measuresTwo or three measurable outcomes and how they will be trackedStatus calls down 40% within six months of launch
Scope boundariesWhat is in, what is out, what is undecidedIn: claim status and document upload. Out: new claims intake
StakeholdersWho is involved, how and how oftenClaims director at every Sprint Review; legal at release gates
Team and rolesWho is on the team and who makes which decisionsProduct Owner, Scrum Master, five Developers
CadenceSprint length and the meeting scheduleTwo-week Sprints; Review and Retrospective every other Thursday
Initial backlogEpics in priority order with rough sizesSix epics, about 340 story points in total
Release forecastLikely scope by date, as a rangeMVP between Sprint 7 and Sprint 9
Milestones and dependenciesFixed dates and external inputsData protection review before the pilot
BudgetFunding, cost per Sprint and runway£38,000 per Sprint, 12 Sprints funded
Risks and assumptionsTop risks, owners and responsesLegacy claims system integration; spike in Sprint 1
Reporting and governanceWhat is reported, to whom and whenBurnup and forecast to the steering group monthly

Keep each component brief. Detailed work belongs in the backlog, risks in a living risk register or RAID log, and dates in the release plan. The project plan links to those artefacts instead of copying them.

Who owns the plan in an Agile project

Ownership is shared, because each planning level has a different owner. Confusion usually comes from expecting one person to write and defend every level.

RolePlanning responsibility
Sponsor or business ownerSets the vision and funding, agrees success measures, approves the budget and stage decisions
Product OwnerOwns the Product Goal, roadmap and Product Backlog order; makes scope trade-offs; leads release forecasting
Project manager (where the role exists)Owns the overall project plan in hybrid or governed settings: budget, milestones, dependencies, risks, reporting and stakeholder communication
Scrum MasterMakes sure planning events happen and are useful; helps the team improve forecasting and remove impediments
DevelopersSize the work, plan each Sprint, own the Sprint Backlog and the daily plan

Scrum itself has no project manager accountability, but many organisations keep the role for budgets, contracts and cross-team coordination. Our guide to the Agile project manager explains how the role works alongside a Product Owner and Scrum Master.

How to create an Agile project plan, step by step

Follow these steps for a new Agile project. Expect the first version to take days, not weeks; you will refine it every Sprint.

  1. Agree the outcome. Write the problem, who benefits and two or three success measures. If people cannot agree on these, more planning detail will not help.
  2. Set the boundaries and constraints. Record what is in and out of scope, the budget, any fixed dates, regulatory or contractual milestones and known dependencies.
  3. Form the team and choose the cadence. Confirm who is on the team, the Sprint length (two weeks is common) and the schedule of Sprint Planning, Reviews and Retrospectives.
  4. Build an initial backlog. Break the outcome into epics and the first few epics into user stories. Order them by value, risk and dependency. Use MoSCoW or a ranked list to mark what is essential.
  5. Size the work roughly. Ask the team to estimate epics with T-shirt sizes or story points. Precision is not the goal; a consistent relative size is.
  6. Estimate a velocity range. If the team has history, use its slowest, average and fastest recent Sprints. If not, forecast capacity for the first Sprints and replace the guess with real data after two or three Sprints.
  7. Forecast releases. Divide the remaining work by the velocity range to show earliest and latest likely Sprints for each release. Mark what is committed (usually the minimum viable scope) and what is only forecast.
  8. Calculate cost per Sprint and runway. Multiply team cost by Sprint length, add other recurring costs, and divide the budget by the result. This tells you how many Sprints are funded.
  9. List risks, assumptions and milestones. Plan early work to reduce the biggest risks first, for example a technical spike in Sprint 1.
  10. Agree reporting and review points. Decide what stakeholders will see (Sprint Reviews, a burnup, a monthly forecast) and when the plan is formally reviewed.
  11. Plan the first Sprint in detail. Hold Sprint Planning for Sprint 1 only. Later Sprints are planned when you reach them.

For the forecasting steps, our release plan template and Sprint planning template do the arithmetic for you.

Worked example: a plan for a claims portal project

The following example is illustrative. A mid-sized insurer wants policyholders to track the status of their claims online and upload documents, so that fewer customers phone the claims team. The sponsor asks for a plan before approving funding.

Team, cadence and budget

ItemValue
TeamProduct Owner, Scrum Master, five Developers (7 people)
Sprint lengthTwo weeks (10 working days)
Blended day rate£520 per person
Team cost per Sprint7 × £520 × 10 = £36,400
Tools and hosting per Sprint£1,600
Total cost per Sprint£38,000
Approved budget£456,000
Funded runway£456,000 ÷ £38,000 = 12 Sprints (24 weeks)

Backlog and forecast

The team sizes six epics at about 340 story points. The Product Owner marks 210 points as the minimum viable product (must-have), 70 as should-have and 60 as could-have. The team has worked together before, so it uses a velocity range of 24 (slow), 28 (average) and 33 (fast) points per Sprint.

ScopePointsFast (33)Average (28)Slow (24)
MVP (must-have)2107 Sprints8 Sprints9 Sprints
MVP + should-have2809 Sprints10 Sprints12 Sprints
Everything34011 Sprints13 Sprints15 Sprints

Each figure is the points divided by velocity, rounded up to whole Sprints. With 12 Sprints funded, the plan says:

  • Committed: the MVP, released to all policyholders by the end of Sprint 9 at the latest.
  • Forecast: should-have items by Sprint 12, likely but not guaranteed.
  • Not promised: could-have items. They are only reached if the team runs at the fast end of its range.

Releases, milestones and risks

Release or milestoneTargetContents or condition
Technical spikeSprint 1Prove the connection to the legacy claims system (the biggest risk)
Data protection reviewBefore Sprint 5 endsRequired before any customer data is shown in the pilot
Release 1: pilotEnd of Sprint 5Claim status for a pilot group of policyholders
Release 2: MVPEnd of Sprint 7 to 9Status, document upload and notifications for all policyholders
Release 3: enhancementsEnd of Sprint 12Should-have items such as live chat hand-off

Re-planning after Sprint 6

After six Sprints the team has completed 165 points, all from the MVP (an average of 27.5 per Sprint). Legal has added 18 points of compliance stories to the MVP. The Product Owner updates the forecast:

MeasureCalculationResult
Remaining MVP210 + 18 − 16563 points
Sprints to finish MVP63 ÷ 33 to 63 ÷ 242 to 3 Sprints, so Sprint 8 or 9
Remaining must + should63 + 70133 points
Capacity left in budget6 Sprints × 24 to 6 Sprints × 33144 to 198 points
Could-have items133 + 60 = 193 pointsOnly reachable at the fast end

The commitment still holds, the should-have forecast has improved, and the sponsor can see that the extra compliance work was absorbed without new funding. That conversation took ten minutes because the plan already showed what was committed, what was forecast and why.

Fixed dates, budgets and scope: how the plan handles constraints

Most Agile plans hold time and cost steady and let scope flex. That does not mean scope is vague: the plan names the minimum that must be delivered and orders everything else by value. When a constraint is fixed, the plan shows which other variable absorbs change.

SituationWhat the plan fixesWhat flexesHow to plan it
Fixed budgetCost per Sprint and number of SprintsScope beyond the minimumFund a stable team for a set runway; forecast scope with a velocity range
Fixed deadlineRelease dateScope, ordered by valueWork out how many Sprints fit before the date; commit to what fits at the slow velocity
Fixed scopeA defined minimum feature setDate, within a rangeForecast a date range; review it every Sprint and act early if it slips

If a stakeholder insists on fixing scope, date and budget together, the honest response is to show the forecast range and ask which constraint gives way if the slow case happens. Writing that agreement into the plan prevents arguments later. Our guide to project constraints explains the underlying trade-offs.

Satisfying governance without a fixed plan

Many organisations need plans for funding approval, audit and oversight. Agile teams can meet these needs without writing a detailed baseline that will be out of date in a month.

Governance needAgile answer
Approve fundingFund a stable team for a set runway, with a clear outcome and success measures; review continuation at agreed points
Know when things will be readyRelease forecast as a range, updated every Sprint
See real progressWorking increments at Sprint Reviews, plus a burnup chart of completed scope
Control scopeOrdered backlog; any addition shows what moves down or out
Manage riskShort, living risk and decision log; risky work scheduled early
Stage gates or phase reviewsGates aligned with releases (pilot, MVP, full rollout) rather than with design or build phases

Frameworks such as PRINCE2 Agile combine stage-based governance with Agile delivery: the project and stage plans focus on features and outcomes, while teams plan their own work. Our guides to project governance and PRINCE2 vs Agile explain the options.

Tip

Align stage gates with releases, not with activities. A gate that asks 'is the pilot working for real customers?' tells the board far more than one that asks 'is the design document signed off?'.

Keeping the plan alive

An Agile plan loses its value as soon as it stops reflecting reality. Build updates into the cadence so they are routine rather than a special exercise.

What to updateWhenWhoTypical trigger
Sprint Backlog and daily planEvery dayDevelopersProgress or new information at the Daily Scrum
Sprint Goal and Sprint planEvery Sprint PlanningScrum TeamStart of each Sprint
Release forecast and burnupEnd of every SprintProduct OwnerNew velocity data or scope change
Risk and decision logEvery Sprint, and when something happensProject manager or Product OwnerNew risk, issue or decision
RoadmapMonthly or quarterlyProduct Owner or product managerLearning from releases, market or strategy change
Budget and runwayMonthlyProject manager or sponsorSpend reports, team changes

Changes are expected; what matters is that each one is visible. Keep a short change history in the plan (date, change, reason, impact on forecast).

Tracking progress against an Agile plan

Progress in an Agile project is measured by what is finished and usable, not by how many tasks have started. These measures show whether the plan is on track:

  • Burnup chart: completed scope against total scope over time. It shows both progress and scope growth on one chart.
  • Velocity range: points (or items) completed per Sprint. Use the range for forecasts; see our guide to the Agile velocity chart.
  • Release forecast: earliest and latest likely Sprint for each release, recalculated each Sprint.
  • Outcome measures: the success measures in the plan, such as fewer status calls or faster processing, tracked once releases are live.

The 2020 Scrum Guide notes that practices such as burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flow are useful for forecasting but do not replace empiricism: only what has already happened can be used for forward-looking decisions. In other words, re-forecast with real data rather than defending the original estimate. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs covers more measures and their pitfalls.

Hybrid plans: when part of the work is predictive

Many projects mix Agile delivery with work that must be planned predictively: hardware lead times, procurement, building works, regulatory submissions or a fixed go-live event. In these cases the project plan often has two layers:

  • A milestone-level schedule for fixed dates and external dependencies, sometimes shown as a Gantt chart for stakeholders.
  • Agile release and Sprint plans for the work the team can deliver incrementally, linked to those milestones.

Keep the Gantt chart at milestone or release level; day-to-day work stays in the backlog and on the board. Our guide to Agile Gantt charts shows how to combine the two without creating two competing plans.

Common mistakes with Agile project plans

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
Having no plan at allSponsors lose confidence and impose a predictive plan insteadWrite a short layered plan with a goal, forecast, budget and risks
Planning every Sprint up frontDetail goes out of date quickly and creates false certaintyPlan only the next one or two Sprints in detail
Forecasting with one numberThe first slow Sprint looks like failureUse a velocity range and show earliest and latest dates
Committing to everythingNo room to absorb change; quality suffersCommit to the minimum; forecast the rest
Hiding scope changesDates slip with no visible reasonShow scope growth on a burnup and in the change history
Treating the backlog as the whole planBudget, risks, stakeholders and milestones go unmanagedKeep a one-page plan that links to the backlog
Never re-forecastingThe plan becomes fictionUpdate the forecast at the end of every Sprint

Tools for Agile project planning

A spreadsheet works for small projects; a project management tool keeps the planning levels connected.

  • Wrike (our #1, 9.4) suits hybrid plans well: Gantt charts, dependencies and milestones sit alongside boards, request intake and portfolio reporting, so governance and team work stay in one place.
  • Jira is strongest for software Scrum teams: backlogs, epics, story points, Sprints and velocity reports.
  • monday.com (9.1) is easy to adopt and offers timeline and Gantt views for stakeholder-facing plans.
  • Asana (9.0) links work to goals and portfolios, which helps when plans roll up to business objectives.
  • ClickUp (8.9) combines Sprints, Gantt views, docs and dashboards at a modest price.

Frequently asked questions

What role does the project plan play in Agile development?

It sets direction and forecasts delivery rather than fixing every task. The plan aligns stakeholders on the goal, states constraints such as budget and fixed dates, forecasts what is likely by when and makes trade-offs visible. Detailed work is planned just in time, Sprint by Sprint.

Do Agile projects have a project plan?

Yes. Agile projects plan in layers: a vision, a roadmap, release plans, Sprint plans and a daily plan. Many organisations also keep a short project plan that ties these together with the budget, milestones, risks and reporting.

What are the five levels of Agile planning?

Vision, roadmap, release, iteration (Sprint) and daily. Detail increases as the horizon gets shorter. The model is often drawn as a planning onion, and some authors add strategy and portfolio layers outside the vision.

What should an Agile project plan include?

The goal, success measures, scope boundaries, stakeholders, team and roles, cadence, initial backlog, release forecast, milestones and dependencies, budget, risks and assumptions, and how progress will be reported.

Who is responsible for the project plan in Agile?

Ownership is shared. The sponsor owns the vision and funding, the Product Owner owns the roadmap, backlog order and release forecast, the Developers own Sprint and daily plans, and a project manager, where there is one, often owns the overall plan, budget and reporting.

Is the product backlog the same as the project plan?

No. The backlog is the ordered list of work and is a core part of the plan, but a full Agile project plan also covers goals, success measures, stakeholders, cadence, budget, milestones and risks.

How does an Agile project plan handle a fixed budget?

Usually by funding a stable team for a set number of Sprints. Divide the budget by the cost per Sprint to find the runway, then forecast how much of the ordered backlog fits, committing to the minimum and forecasting the rest.

How does Agile handle a fixed deadline?

Count the Sprints before the date, forecast how much work fits at the slow end of the velocity range and commit only to that. Lower-priority items are delivered if the team runs faster.

Can you use a Gantt chart in Agile?

Yes, at milestone or release level, especially for stakeholders and for dependencies on predictive work such as procurement. Day-to-day planning stays in the backlog and on the board.

How often is an Agile project plan updated?

The Sprint plan is updated every Sprint and the daily plan every day. Re-forecast releases at the end of every Sprint, review the roadmap monthly or quarterly, and change the vision only when strategy changes.

What is the difference between a release plan and a project plan in Agile?

A release plan forecasts which backlog items are likely to be ready by which date. The project plan is broader: it includes the release plan plus goals, budget, team, stakeholders, milestones, risks and governance.

How do you report Agile progress to a steering committee?

Show working increments, a burnup chart of completed versus total scope, the latest release forecast as a range, budget burn and the top risks and decisions. Align formal reviews with releases.
