How to Learn Agile and Get Certified: Step-by-Step Path

To learn Agile, start with the Agile Manifesto and the Scrum Guide, practise the ideas on real work as soon as you can, and then choose a certification that matches your role and the employers you want to work for. You can understand the basics in a couple of weeks; becoming good at Agile takes months of practice. This guide gives you a syllabus, a 90-day learning plan, ways to get experience, and a side-by-side comparison of the main Agile certifications.

Key takeaways

  • Learn the foundations first: the Agile Manifesto and its principles, the Scrum Guide, and the basics of Kanban. All are free.
  • Practise early. A personal Kanban board, running a retrospective or volunteering on a sprint-based project teaches more than any course.
  • Choose a certification by role and market: PSM I or CSM for Scrum Masters, PSPO or CSPO for Product Owners, PMI-ACP for experienced practitioners, SAFe or PRINCE2 Agile where employers use them.
  • Entry-level Scrum certifications need no prior experience; the PMI-ACP requires Agile experience and 21 hours of training.
  • Renewal rules differ: PSM I does not expire, CSM renews every two years and SAFe annually, so factor them into the cost.
  • Certification opens doors, but employers hire for evidence that you can apply Agile. Build both.

What does learning Agile involve?

Learning Agile means understanding the values and principles behind Agile ways of working, learning at least one framework such as Scrum or Kanban in detail, and building the practical skills to use them: planning in short cycles, working with a backlog, facilitating team events and improving through feedback. Knowledge comes quickly; skill comes from practice.

It helps to separate three layers:

  • Mindset: the values and principles in the Agile Manifesto, such as responding to change, collaborating with customers and delivering working results often.
  • Frameworks and methods: specific ways of applying those ideas, most commonly Scrum and Kanban, plus scaling frameworks such as SAFe and hybrid approaches such as PRINCE2 Agile.
  • Practices and skills: user stories, estimation, backlog ordering, facilitation, coaching, stakeholder management and the habit of inspecting and adapting.

A common beginner mistake is to treat Agile and Scrum as the same thing. Scrum is one Agile framework; Agile is the wider set of values it is built on. Our guide to Agile vs Scrum explains the difference.

Step-by-step: how to learn Agile

The path below works whether you are new to project work or an experienced project manager adding Agile to your toolkit.

  1. Learn the foundations: values, principles and the main frameworks.
  2. Go deeper on Scrum and Kanban, the two frameworks you will meet most often.
  3. Practise on real work, even in a small way.
  4. Choose and take a certification that fits your role.
  5. Specialise in a role: Scrum Master, Product Owner, Agile project manager or coach.
  6. Keep learning through communities, mentoring and reflection.

Step 1: Learn the foundations

Read the Agile Manifesto and its twelve principles; they take ten minutes to read and much longer to understand. Then read the official Scrum Guide. The current edition is the 2020 version by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland; it is short, free at scrumguides.org and the basis of most Scrum exams. Finish this step by working through our overview of the Agile methodology so you can see how the pieces fit together.

Step 2: Go deeper on Scrum and Kanban

Reread the Scrum Guide until you can explain each event and artefact without looking. Learn why each element exists, not just what it is: for example, why the Sprint Goal matters more than the list of items. For Kanban, learn the core practices (visualise, limit work in progress, manage flow, make policies explicit, improve) and the basic metrics such as cycle time and throughput.

Step 3: Practise on real work

Practice turns knowledge into skill. Start a personal Kanban board for your own tasks, offer to facilitate a retrospective for your team, or run a small project in two-week sprints. Keep notes on what worked and what did not; they become useful evidence in interviews. The section below on getting experience without an Agile job gives more options.

Step 4: Choose a certification

Once you understand the basics and have tried them, choose a certification that matches your goal. Taking an exam before you have practised at all tends to produce people who can recite the Scrum Guide but struggle in a real Sprint Planning. The comparison table later in this guide shows the main options.

Step 5: Specialise in a role

Agile teams need different skills in different roles. Decide which one you are aiming for and study it specifically: the Scrum Master role, the Scrum Product Owner, or the Agile project manager, who often works across Agile and predictive delivery.

Step 6: Keep learning

Agile practice keeps evolving. Join a community, find a mentor, teach others, and run retrospectives on your own work. Explaining Agile to someone else is one of the fastest ways to find the gaps in your own understanding.

Free and low-cost ways to learn Agile

You do not need to spend money to learn Agile well. Most of the primary sources are free.

Primary sources

  • The Agile Manifesto and its twelve principles at agilemanifesto.org.
  • The Scrum Guide (2020) at scrumguides.org, available in many languages.
  • Free Kanban guides published by Kanban University and by ProKanban.org.
  • The Agile Practice Guide, published by PMI with Agile Alliance; useful if you come from a PMI background.
  • Free practice assessments offered by several certification bodies, such as Scrum.org's open assessments.

Books worth reading

  • *Scrum: The Art of Doing Twice the Work in Half the Time* by Jeff Sutherland, for the story and reasoning behind Scrum.
  • *Agile Estimating and Planning* by Mike Cohn, for estimation, velocity and release planning.
  • *User Story Mapping* by Jeff Patton, for turning ideas into a backlog that makes sense.
  • *Kanban* by David J. Anderson, for the origins of the Kanban Method in knowledge work.
  • *The Phoenix Project* by Gene Kim, Kevin Behr and George Spafford, a novel about flow and DevOps that many people find an easy way in.

Communities and events

Local and online Agile meetups, Agile Alliance and Scrum Alliance community groups, and conference talks published online are all good ways to hear how other teams solve real problems. Ask questions; practitioners are generally generous with their time.

Our free guides and templates

This site's Agile, Scrum and Kanban guides are free and written to be read in order. Pair them with practical templates: a Kanban board template to run your own work and a sprint retrospective template to facilitate your first retrospective.

How to get Agile experience without an Agile job

Many entry-level roles ask for experience, which can feel like a catch-22. These options let you build real, discussable experience:

  • Use Agile on your current work. Even in a traditional role, you can visualise your team's work on a board, hold short planning sessions and run retrospectives at the end of a phase.
  • Volunteer to facilitate. Offer to run a retrospective or a planning session for a team that does not have a Scrum Master.
  • Run a personal project in sprints. A home renovation, a course project or a side business all work. Keep a backlog, plan two-week sprints and review results.
  • Help a charity or community group. Many small organisations welcome someone who can help them organise their work.
  • Shadow an Agile team. Ask to observe Sprint Reviews or Daily Scrums in another department.
  • Join hackathons or open-source projects, where short, goal-driven cycles are normal.

When you describe this experience on a CV or in an interview, be specific: what problem the team had, what you did, and what changed. "Facilitated six retrospectives for a five-person marketing team; two actions cut campaign approval time from ten days to four" is far stronger than "familiar with Agile". Agile is used well beyond software, so experience from marketing, HR or operations counts; see is Agile only for software?

PMI-ACP eligibility in detail

PMI currently lists a secondary diploma plus one of these experience routes: two years of Agile experience in the past five years; a degree from a GAC-accredited programme plus one year of Agile experience; a third-party Agile certification plus one year of Agile experience; or an active PMP. All routes also need 21 hours of formal Agile training. Because a Scrum certification counts as a third-party Agile certification, many people take PSM I or CSM first and the PMI-ACP later.

About PRINCE2 Agile and the 2025 update

PeopleCert released version 2 of PRINCE2 Agile in 2025, aligned with PRINCE2 7. Practitioner candidates need PRINCE2 Agile Foundation (either version). If your organisation runs projects under PRINCE2 governance, read our comparison of PRINCE2 and Agile.

PSM I or CSM?

Both are well recognised entry-level Scrum Master certifications. The main differences are format and renewal. PSM I is an exam you can take whenever you are ready, with an 85% pass mark and no expiry. CSM requires a live 16-hour course with a Certified Scrum Trainer, which many people value for the interaction, and renews every two years. If you learn well on your own and want the lowest ongoing cost, PSM I fits; if you want guided training and your employer will pay for the course, CSM fits. Our guide to Scrum certification and training compares them in more detail.

PMI-ACP or PMP?

They do different jobs. The PMP is PMI's flagship project management certification and its exam covers predictive, Agile and hybrid approaches. The PMI-ACP focuses entirely on Agile and suits practitioners who already work on Agile teams. Many project managers hold both.

Should you start with a scaling framework?

Usually not. SAFe and other scaling frameworks build on team-level Scrum and Kanban. Learn those first unless your employer already uses SAFe and expects you to join a train straight away.

How to prepare for an Agile certification exam

  1. Read the source material the exam is based on, such as the Scrum Guide for PSM and CSM, or the official handbook or exam content outline for the PMI-ACP and PRINCE2 Agile.
  2. Read it again, slowly. Exam questions often turn on exact wording, such as who is accountable for what.
  3. Take a free practice assessment to find your weak areas before you spend money.
  4. Study the reasoning, not just the rules. Many questions describe a situation and ask what a Scrum Master or Product Owner should do.
  5. Use a study group or forum to discuss questions you got wrong.
  6. Keep taking practice tests until you pass comfortably and consistently, not just once.
  7. Plan the exam day. Check the time limit, whether the exam is open book, your internet connection for online exams, and the retake policy.
Tip

For PSM I, aim to score well above the 85% pass mark in practice before you book. The time limit (80 questions in 60 minutes) leaves little room for looking things up.

Is Agile certification worth it?

Certification is worth it when it solves a specific problem for you. It is less useful as a substitute for experience.

Certification helps most when…Experience matters more when…
You are changing career and need to get past CV screeningYou are applying for senior Scrum Master, coach or Agile lead roles
Employers in your market list a specific certification in job advertsThe hiring manager asks for examples of teams you have improved
Your organisation requires it (for example SAFe or PRINCE2 Agile)You already have a strong track record in Agile delivery
You want a structured way to learn with a clear finish lineYou are learning mainly to improve your current team's work

Our view: for most people, one well-recognised entry-level certification plus real practice is the right combination. Collecting many certificates without practice adds little. Before you choose, look at twenty job adverts for the role you want and note which credentials they mention most often.

Common mistakes when learning Agile

  • Learning the rules without the reasons. Following Scrum mechanically without understanding why each element exists leads to empty ceremonies.
  • Treating Agile as only Scrum. Kanban, Lean and hybrid approaches suit many types of work better.
  • Skipping practice. Reading about facilitation is not the same as running a tense retrospective.
  • Choosing a certification by price or difficulty alone. Choose by role and by what employers in your market ask for.
  • Starting with scaling. Scaling frameworks make more sense once you understand team-level Agile.
  • Ignoring traditional project management. Many roles combine Agile with predictive governance, budgets and milestones. Knowing both makes you more useful.
  • Stopping after the exam. The certificate is the start of learning, not the end.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start learning Agile?

Read the Agile Manifesto and its twelve principles, then the 2020 Scrum Guide, then the basics of Kanban. Next, apply what you learn on real work, for example with a personal Kanban board or by facilitating a retrospective, before choosing a certification.

How long does it take to learn Agile?

You can learn the basics in one to two weeks of part-time study. Becoming confident usually takes a few months of practice on real work, and becoming highly skilled takes years of experience with different teams.

Can I learn Agile for free?

Yes. The Agile Manifesto, the Scrum Guide and Kanban guides are free, several certification bodies offer free practice assessments, and free guides such as this one cover the rest. You only need to pay if you want a certification.

What is the best Agile certification for beginners?

Entry-level Scrum certifications such as PSM I from Scrum.org or CSM from Scrum Alliance are the most common starting points. PSPO I or CSPO suit aspiring Product Owners, and PRINCE2 Agile Foundation suits PRINCE2 environments.

Can I get Agile certified without experience?

Yes. PSM I, CSM, PSPO I, CSPO, PRINCE2 Agile Foundation and ICAgile ICP have no experience requirement. The PMI-ACP does require Agile experience.

What is the difference between PSM and CSM?

PSM I from Scrum.org is an online assessment with no required course, an 85% pass mark and no expiry. CSM from Scrum Alliance requires a 16-hour course with a Certified Scrum Trainer, has a 74% pass mark and renews every two years.

What are the PMI-ACP requirements?

A secondary diploma, 21 hours of Agile training, and one of: two years of Agile experience in the past five years; an accredited degree plus one year; another Agile certification plus one year; or an active PMP. The exam has 120 questions in 180 minutes.

Which Agile certification is best for project managers?

Experienced project managers often choose the PMI-ACP, or PRINCE2 Agile in PRINCE2 organisations. A Scrum certification is a good first step if you are not yet eligible for the PMI-ACP.

Is Agile certification worth it?

It is worth it when you need to pass CV screening, your employer requires it, or you want a structured way to learn. It does not replace experience, so combine it with practice.

Do Agile certifications expire?

Some do. PSM I does not expire. CSM renews every two years with 20 SEUs and a fee, SAFe certifications renew annually, and the PMI-ACP requires 30 PDUs every three years.

How much does Agile certification cost?

It depends on the credential, provider and country. Exam-only credentials such as PSM I usually cost less than course-based ones such as CSM or SAFe. Include renewal fees and check current prices on the issuer's website.

Is Scrum the same as Agile?

No. Agile is a set of values and principles; Scrum is one framework for applying them. Kanban, XP and Lean are other Agile approaches.

Can I learn Agile without working in IT?

Yes. Agile is used in marketing, HR, finance, education, construction and many other fields. The principles are the same, though practices are adapted to the work.

Should I learn Agile or traditional project management?

Ideally both. Many organisations combine predictive governance with Agile delivery, and project managers who can choose and blend approaches are in demand.

What comes after a first Agile certification?

Specialise in a role, gain a year or two of practice, then consider an advanced credential such as PSM II, Advanced CSM, PMI-ACP or a role-specific SAFe certification.
