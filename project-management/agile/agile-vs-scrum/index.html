Agile vs Scrum: Key Differences, Examples and When to Use

The difference between Agile and Scrum is that Agile is a set of values and principles for delivering work iteratively, while Scrum is one specific framework, with defined accountabilities, events and artefacts, for putting those principles into practice. All Scrum teams are working in an Agile way, but Agile teams can also use Kanban, XP, Lean or their own hybrid. This guide compares the two side by side, shows the same project run both ways, and gives you a step-by-step process for deciding whether Scrum suits your team.

Key takeaways

  • Agile is the philosophy, set out in the 2001 Agile Manifesto as four values and twelve principles. Scrum is a lightweight framework that applies it.
  • Scrum adds structure that Agile does not prescribe: three accountabilities, five events, three artefacts and fixed-length Sprints of one month or less.
  • Scrum is older than the Agile Manifesto: it was first presented in 1995, and both Scrum co-creators signed the Manifesto in 2001.
  • You can be Agile without Scrum (Kanban, XP, Lean), but running Scrum events without Agile values gives you the meetings without the benefits.
  • Choose Scrum for a dedicated team building a product in planned increments; consider Kanban for unpredictable, continuous-flow work.

Agile vs Scrum: the short answer

Agile is a way of thinking about work: deliver in small, usable increments, collaborate closely with customers, and adapt the plan as you learn. Scrum is a specific framework that turns that thinking into a working routine, with fixed-length Sprints, a Product Owner, a Scrum Master, Developers and a set of regular events. Agile tells you what to value; Scrum tells you one way to organise a team around those values.

An everyday comparison helps. "Eating healthily" is a set of principles; a specific meal plan is one way to follow them. Some people follow a structured plan, others build their own habits, and both can be healthy. Agile is the principle, and Scrum is one well-tested plan.

What is Agile?

Agile is an approach to project and product work that delivers results in short cycles, uses feedback to steer, and treats the plan as something to update rather than defend. It was named in February 2001, when 17 software practitioners met in Snowbird, Utah, and wrote the Agile Manifesto. The Manifesto sets out four values:

  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation.
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.
  • Responding to change over following a plan.

The authors stress that the items on the right still have value; the items on the left are valued more. Twelve supporting principles add detail, such as delivering working software frequently, welcoming changing requirements, sustainable pace, and regular reflection on how to become more effective.

What Agile does not contain is just as important. It prescribes no roles, no meetings, no iteration length and no documents. That is deliberate: it is a set of ideas that teams apply through frameworks and practices. The best known are Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming (XP), Lean, and scaled frameworks such as SAFe.

What is Scrum?

The Scrum Guide describes Scrum as a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organisations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. It is maintained by its creators, Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, and the 2020 edition is still the official version. In June 2025 Jeff Sutherland released a Scrum Guide Expansion Pack, but it is an optional supplement and does not replace the 2020 Scrum Guide.

Scrum is built on empiricism (knowledge comes from experience and decisions are based on what is observed) and lean thinking (reduce waste, focus on essentials). Its three pillars are transparency, inspection and adaptation, and its five values are commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage.

How Scrum puts the Agile values into practice

One way to see the relationship is to map each Agile value to the parts of Scrum that make it happen. This is our own mapping, not part of either official document.

Agile valueHow Scrum supports it
Individuals and interactions over processes and toolsSmall, cross-functional, self-managing team; the Daily Scrum is a conversation, not a status report; the Retrospective improves how people work together
Working software over comprehensive documentationEvery Sprint must produce at least one usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done
Customer collaboration over contract negotiationThe Product Owner represents stakeholders daily; the Sprint Review invites stakeholders to inspect the Increment and shape what comes next
Responding to change over following a planThe Product Backlog is reordered continuously; each Sprint is a short planning horizon; the plan is inspected and adapted at every event

The same exercise works for the principles. "Deliver working software frequently" becomes the Sprint; "at regular intervals, the team reflects" becomes the Sprint Retrospective; "business people and developers must work together daily" becomes the Product Owner's role.

What Agile and Scrum have in common

Because Scrum is an Agile framework, the two share a lot:

  • Iterative and incremental delivery: both build the product in small usable slices and refine it with feedback.
  • Customer focus: value to the customer decides what is built next.
  • Empirical control: inspect real results, then adapt the plan.
  • Self-organising or self-managing teams: the people doing the work decide how to do it.
  • Continuous improvement: regular reflection on the way the team works.
  • Transparency: visible backlogs, boards and progress so everyone sees the same reality.

Which came first: Agile or Scrum?

Scrum came first. Many people assume Scrum was created to implement the Agile Manifesto, but the timeline runs the other way.

YearEvent
1986Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka publish "The New New Product Development Game" in Harvard Business Review, using the rugby term "scrum" for fast, team-based product development
1995Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland present Scrum as a software development framework at the OOPSLA conference
200117 practitioners, including Schwaber and Sutherland, write the Agile Manifesto in Snowbird, Utah
2010The first Scrum Guide is published
2020The current Scrum Guide is released: shorter, less software-specific, adds the Product Goal and the three commitments

So Scrum was one of the lightweight methods whose shared ideas the Manifesto put into words.

Worked example: the same project run two Agile ways

A team of six (a product lead, four developers and a designer) is building online class booking for a chain of fitness studios. The goal is the same either way: members can book, cancel and join waiting lists online. Here is how the work looks under Scrum and under Kanban, both of which are Agile.

Option A: Scrum with two-week Sprints

WhenWhat happensWho
Day 1, morningSprint Planning: agree the Sprint Goal "Members can book and cancel a class online", select backlog items, plan the first daysWhole Scrum Team
Every day, 15 minutesDaily Scrum: inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adjust the planDevelopers
Mid-SprintBacklog refinement: split and clarify the waiting-list items for the next SprintProduct Owner and Developers
Day 10, morningSprint Review: studio managers try the booking flow; feedback changes the order of the backlogScrum Team and stakeholders
Day 10, afternoonSprint Retrospective: agree one or two improvements, such as earlier testingScrum Team

The product lead acts as Product Owner and one developer, trained in Scrum, acts as Scrum Master. Stakeholders know that new requests go to the Product Owner and are considered at the next Sprint Planning. Progress is forecast from velocity; see Agile release planning.

Option B: Kanban with continuous flow

  • The team uses a board with columns Ready, Design, Build, Test, Done, and a work-in-progress limit of three items in Build.
  • A short weekly replenishment meeting picks the next most valuable items for Ready.
  • Each item is released as soon as it is done, so "cancel a booking" might go live on a Tuesday and "join a waiting list" the following Thursday.
  • The team holds a stand-up at the board and a fortnightly review of flow metrics such as cycle time.

What the comparison shows

QuestionScrum versionKanban version
When can a stakeholder add a request?Any time to the Product Backlog; normally taken into the next SprintAny time; pulled when there is capacity
How is delivery forecast?Velocity per SprintThroughput and cycle time
When is work released?Whenever an Increment is ready; at least one usable Increment per SprintContinuously, item by item
Who removes obstacles?The Scrum Master is accountable for thisWhoever the team agrees

Both are Agile: they deliver in small slices, collaborate with the studios and adapt. Scrum suits this project well because the work is a defined product feature with a dedicated team. If the same team then moved on to supporting the system and fixing urgent issues, Kanban might suit better.

Roles: Agile team vs Scrum Team

Agile does not define job titles, which is why organisations use many names. Scrum defines three accountabilities, and they are not job titles either: a person can hold one alongside their job title.

RoleWhere it comes fromFocus
Product OwnerScrumMaximises the value of the product; orders the Product Backlog; owns the Product Goal. See Scrum Product Owner
Scrum MasterScrumAccountable for establishing Scrum and the team's effectiveness; coaches, removes impediments. See Scrum Master role
DevelopersScrumAnyone who creates the Increment: engineers, designers, testers, analysts, writers
Agile coachCommon Agile practiceHelps teams and leaders adopt Agile, often across several teams and frameworks
Project managerTraditional and hybrid settingsNot a Scrum accountability; in Agile settings often coordinates budgets, governance, dependencies and stakeholders. See Agile project manager
Tip

A Scrum Master is not a project manager under another name. The Scrum Master does not assign tasks or own the plan; the Developers manage their own work and the Product Owner orders the backlog.

How to choose between Scrum and other Agile approaches

Use these steps with your team. They take about an hour in a workshop.

  1. Describe your work. List the last month of work items. Were most of them planned in advance, or did they arrive unexpectedly and need fast handling?
  2. Check your team set-up. Is there a stable team of about 10 or fewer people who mainly work on one product? Scrum assumes this; Kanban does not.
  3. Check for a decision-maker. Is there someone with the authority and time to be Product Owner and order the backlog? Without one, Scrum struggles.
  4. Test the cadence. Could the team commit to a Sprint Goal for two weeks without frequent urgent interruptions? If not, consider Kanban or Scrumban.
  5. Consider the organisation. Do stakeholders need regular demos and forecasts? Scrum's Sprint Review and velocity help. Are there many teams? Look at scaling frameworks.
  6. Run an experiment. Try your chosen approach for three Sprints or six weeks, then review what improved and what did not. Agile applies to your way of working too.
Tip

If the answers are mixed, start with Scrum's events and a Kanban board, then adjust. Many teams settle on a hybrid that keeps the Scrum events and adds WIP limits.

How to start using Scrum in an Agile team

If you decide Scrum fits, a simple start works better than a big launch:

  1. Read the Scrum Guide together. It is short. Agree that the core rules are not optional while you learn.
  2. Assign the accountabilities. Choose a Product Owner and a Scrum Master; everyone else is a Developer.
  3. Build the first Product Backlog. List and order the work, and refine the top items until they are small and clear. Our product backlog template helps.
  4. Agree a Definition of Done. Write down what "done" means for every item.
  5. Pick a Sprint length. Two weeks suits most teams starting out. See what is a sprint.
  6. Run the first Sprint Planning. Set a Sprint Goal and choose a realistic amount of work. Use the free sprint planning template.
  7. Hold every event. Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective, each within its time-box. The sprint retrospective template gives you a ready format.
  8. Inspect and adapt after three Sprints. Review what is working, and improve one thing at a time.

Common misconceptions and mistakes

  • "Agile and Scrum are the same thing." Scrum is one Agile framework among several.
  • "We do Scrum, so we are Agile." Holding the events without empowering the team or acting on feedback is sometimes called "mechanical Scrum". It gives you meetings without the benefits.
  • "Agile means no planning." Agile teams plan at several levels, from the roadmap to the day. They simply expect the plan to change.
  • "Agile means no documentation." The Manifesto values working results more than documents, not instead of them. Write the documents people actually use.
  • "The Scrum Master is the team's manager." The Scrum Master serves the team and the organisation; they do not assign work.
  • "Scrum only works for software." The 2020 Scrum Guide removed most software-specific language, and Scrum is used in marketing, research, HR and other fields.
  • Changing Scrum's core rules too early. Dropping the Retrospective or extending Sprints hides the problems Scrum is designed to reveal. Learn the framework first, then adapt around it.

Agile vs Scrum certifications

The same distinction shows up in certification. Agile certifications cover the broad mindset and several methods; Scrum certifications focus on the Scrum framework and a specific accountability.

  • Agile-wide: PMI's PMI-ACP covers Agile principles and several frameworks, including Scrum, Kanban, XP and Lean.
  • Scrum-specific: Scrum.org's Professional Scrum Master (PSM) and Professional Scrum Product Owner (PSPO), and Scrum Alliance's Certified ScrumMaster (CSM) and Certified Scrum Product Owner (CSPO).

If you work in a Scrum team, a Scrum certification is the more direct choice; if you lead projects across different approaches, an Agile-wide certification is broader. Requirements and fees change, so check the certifying body before you book.

Frequently asked questions

Is Scrum Agile?

Yes. Scrum is one of the most widely used Agile frameworks. It applies the Agile values and principles through fixed-length Sprints, three accountabilities, five events and three artefacts.

What is the main difference between Agile and Scrum?

Agile is a philosophy: a set of values and principles for iterative delivery. Scrum is a specific framework that puts that philosophy into practice with defined roles, events, artefacts and time-boxes.

Can you be Agile without Scrum?

Yes. Teams can be Agile using Kanban, Extreme Programming, Lean, Scrumban or their own tailored practices, as long as they deliver in small increments, seek feedback and adapt.

Can you use Scrum without being Agile?

You can hold the Scrum events without following Agile values, but you will not get the benefits. If the team cannot self-manage, the Product Owner cannot make decisions, or feedback is ignored, you are using Scrum's vocabulary without its intent.

Which came first, Agile or Scrum?

Scrum. It was presented in 1995 and the Agile Manifesto was written in 2001. Scrum's co-creators, Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, were among the Manifesto's 17 authors.

Is Scrum a methodology or a framework?

The Scrum Guide calls Scrum a lightweight framework. It defines a minimal set of rules and deliberately leaves many practices, such as estimation methods or technical practices, for teams to choose.

Is Kanban Agile or Scrum?

Kanban is an Agile approach but it is not Scrum. It uses continuous flow and work-in-progress limits instead of Sprints, and it does not prescribe roles. See Kanban vs Agile.

Which is better, Agile or Scrum?

They are not alternatives: Scrum is a way of being Agile. The real choice is between Scrum and other Agile approaches, and the best one depends on your type of work, team set-up and how predictable your requests are.

What is the difference between a Scrum Master and an Agile coach?

A Scrum Master is accountable for Scrum's effectiveness in one Scrum Team (sometimes two or three). An Agile coach usually works more broadly, helping several teams and leaders adopt Agile across different frameworks.

Is Jira Agile or Scrum?

Neither: Jira is software that supports Agile ways of working. It offers both Scrum boards (with Sprints and backlogs) and Kanban boards. Compare options in our project management tools ranking.

Can Scrum be used outside software development?

Yes. The 2020 Scrum Guide uses general language, and Scrum is used for marketing, research, product design, HR and other complex work. It works best where a team can deliver a usable result every Sprint.

Should I get an Agile or a Scrum certification?

Choose a Scrum certification (such as PSM or CSM) if you work in or with Scrum teams, and an Agile-wide certification (such as PMI-ACP) if you manage projects using several approaches. See how to learn Agile and get certified.

What are the Scrum values?

The Scrum Guide lists five values: commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage. They support Scrum's three pillars of transparency, inspection and adaptation.

Does Agile mean no planning or documentation?

No. Agile teams plan continuously, at the roadmap, release, sprint and daily levels, and they keep documentation that people actually use. They simply value working results and adaptation more highly.
