What Is an Agile Team? Roles, Size, Structure and Examples

An Agile team is a small, stable, cross-functional group of people who together have every skill needed to turn backlog items into finished, usable results, and who decide for themselves how to do that work. This guide explains the characteristics and roles of an Agile team, who decides what, how big it should be and how it differs from a traditional project team. It then shows you how to build one step by step, with an illustrative worked example, a sample working agreement and the most common problems and fixes.

Key takeaways

  • An Agile team is small, stable, cross-functional and self-managing, and it delivers usable increments of work in short cycles.
  • The common roles are a Product Owner (what to build and in which order), a Scrum Master or Agile coach (how well the team works) and Developers (everyone who does the work).
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide says a Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer people; communication paths grow quickly beyond that, so larger groups should split into several teams.
  • Agile teams are long-lived: work flows to a stable team, rather than a new team being assembled for every project.
  • Build a team deliberately: define its mission, map skills, choose a structure, agree a working agreement and protect it from constant reshuffling.
  • Psychological safety, clear goals and stable membership matter more to performance than any framework or tool.

What is an Agile team?

An Agile team is a small group of people, typically ten or fewer, who have all the skills needed to deliver a valuable result and who manage their own work. The team works from a single prioritised backlog, delivers in short cycles such as two-week sprints, reviews results with stakeholders and improves its way of working each cycle.

The idea comes from the Agile Manifesto, whose principles say that the best designs emerge from self-organising teams and that projects should be built around motivated individuals who are trusted to get the job done. Frameworks such as Scrum, Kanban and XP each describe Agile teams slightly differently, but they share the same core.

An Agile team is not simply a team that holds daily stand-ups or uses a board. A group whose manager assigns every task, whose members belong to five projects at once and who hand work to another department for testing is still a traditional team, whatever its meetings are called.

Characteristics of an Agile team

Use this table to judge whether your team has the essential characteristics. The warning signs are what you see when a characteristic is missing.

Agile team roles and responsibilities

Agile teams have few formal roles. The most widely used set comes from Scrum, where the 2020 Scrum Guide calls them accountabilities rather than job titles.

RoleMain responsibilityTypical activities
Product OwnerMaximising the value of the productOwns and orders the backlog, sets the Product Goal, talks to stakeholders, accepts or rejects work
Scrum Master or Agile coachThe team's effectivenessCoaches the team and organisation, facilitates events, removes impediments, protects focus
DevelopersCreating a usable increment each cyclePlan the sprint, build, test, document, release and maintain quality

In Scrum, Developers means everyone who does the work, including designers, analysts, testers, writers and data specialists, not only programmers. There is one Product Owner and one Scrum Master per Scrum Team, and the Scrum Guide says there are no sub-teams or hierarchies.

See Scrum team roles for each accountability in detail, including what changed in the 2020 Scrum Guide.

Roles around the team

  • Stakeholders and customers give feedback at reviews and help the Product Owner understand value. They are not team members.
  • Line managers look after people's careers, skills and wellbeing, and shape the environment the team works in, but do not assign the team's daily work.
  • Agile project managers appear in some organisations to coordinate budgets, dependencies, reporting and governance across teams. See our guide to the Agile project manager role.
  • Specialists such as security or legal advisers may support several teams. Plan their involvement so they do not become a bottleneck.

Roles in Kanban teams

Kanban does not require any new roles: teams start with their existing roles and evolve. Many Kanban teams still have someone who orders incoming work, often called a service request manager, and someone who looks after flow, often called a service delivery manager. These are optional responsibilities, not mandatory job titles.

Who decides what in an Agile team?

Unclear decision rights cause more friction than any other team design problem. Agree them explicitly when the team forms.

DecisionWho decidesWho is consulted
What goes in the backlog and its orderProduct OwnerStakeholders, Developers
How much work to take into a sprintDevelopersProduct Owner
How to do the work (design, tools, techniques)DevelopersSpecialists, Scrum Master
Who works on which itemDevelopers, by pulling workEach other
Whether an item meets the Definition of DoneDevelopersProduct Owner
Whether to release an incrementProduct OwnerDevelopers, stakeholders
How the team improves its processThe whole teamScrum Master facilitates
Who joins or leaves the teamUsually managers, ideally with the team's inputTeam members

For decisions that involve people outside the team, such as approvals, sign-offs and specialist input, a RACI matrix template makes responsibilities visible without adding bureaucracy.

Agile team size

The 2020 Scrum Guide says a Scrum Team should be small enough to remain nimble and large enough to complete significant work within a sprint, typically 10 or fewer people including the Product Owner and Scrum Master. SAFe also describes Agile teams as ten or fewer people. Many organisations aim for around five to nine.

The main reason is communication. A team of n people has n(n−1)/2 possible one-to-one communication paths, and that number grows much faster than headcount.

Team sizeCommunication pathsWhat it feels like
33Fast, but few skills and fragile if someone is away
510Easy to coordinate; skills may be thin
721A common sweet spot for many teams
1045Workable with discipline; events start to run long
1266Coordination overhead grows; sub-groups tend to form
15105Usually better as two teams

When and how to split a team

Split a team when events consistently overrun, people stop knowing what others are working on or informal sub-teams appear. Split around customer value, for example by user journey or product area, rather than by skill, so each new team stays cross-functional. Teams working on the same product should keep one Product Goal and one product backlog, and coordinate through a practice such as a Scrum of Scrums.

Agile team vs traditional project team

Traditional project teamAgile team
StructureSpecialists grouped by function, often in a hierarchyOne cross-functional unit with no internal hierarchy
FormationAssembled for a project, disbanded at the endLong-lived; new work flows to the existing team
Work allocationAssigned by a project managerPulled by team members from a prioritised backlog
PlanningDetailed plan up frontRolling plans for the next sprint or release
HandoffsWork passes between departmentsMost work stays within the team
Customer contactAt milestones and sign-offsEvery iteration, at reviews
Success measureTasks completed to planValuable outcomes delivered
ImprovementLessons learned at the endRetrospective every iteration

Neither model is wrong in every situation. A traditional team can suit a short, well-defined piece of work with specialist sign-offs. An Agile team suits work where requirements will change and early feedback is valuable.

Benefits of an Agile team

When the characteristics above are in place, organisations typically see these benefits:

  • Faster delivery of value. Fewer handoffs and smaller batches mean usable results reach customers sooner.
  • Earlier feedback and less rework. Stakeholders see working output every iteration, so misunderstandings surface while they are still cheap to fix.
  • Better quality. Testing and review happen inside the team, against a shared Definition of Done, rather than at the end.
  • More reliable forecasts. A stable team builds a track record of throughput or velocity that supports realistic planning.
  • Greater engagement. People who decide how to do their own work, and see its effect, tend to take more ownership of it.
  • Resilience. Shared knowledge and T-shaped skills mean the team copes better when someone is away or leaves.

These benefits are not automatic. A team that is Agile in name only, with part-time members and a manager assigning tasks, will see few of them.

Types of Agile teams

Feature teams and component teams

A feature team delivers complete customer features across every layer of a product. A component team owns one part, such as a payments service or mobile app, and serves other teams. Feature teams reduce handoffs; component teams build deep expertise. Most organisations use mainly feature teams with a few component teams.

Team Topologies team types

The book *Team Topologies* by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais describes four team types that many Agile organisations now use:

  • Stream-aligned team: delivers a continuous flow of value for one product, service or user journey. Most teams should be this type.
  • Platform team: provides internal services, such as infrastructure, that stream-aligned teams use on a self-service basis.
  • Enabling team: specialists who help other teams adopt a new skill or practice, then step back.
  • Complicated-subsystem team: owns a part that needs rare expertise, such as a pricing engine.

Teams of teams

When one product needs several teams, they are grouped into a larger unit: an Agile Release Train in SAFe, a requirement area in LeSS, or tribes and squads in the so-called Spotify model. See squads, tribes, chapters and guilds and Agile team structures to compare the options.

How to build an Agile team: step by step

  1. Define the team's mission. Write one or two sentences describing the customer, product or value stream the team will own and the outcome it is accountable for. A project charter template can be adapted into a short team charter.
  2. List the skills the work needs. Look at the next three to six months of work and list every skill required to take an item from idea to release.
  3. Map the skills you have. Use a skills matrix template to rate candidate members and spot gaps and single points of failure.
  4. Choose a structure and size. Decide between a feature or component team and aim for around five to nine people. If the skills list needs more, plan two teams.
  5. Appoint a Product Owner with authority. Give one person the authority to order the backlog and accept work, and make sure they have the time to do it.
  6. Appoint a Scrum Master or coach. For a new team, an experienced coach shortens the learning curve considerably.
  7. Agree a working agreement and Definition of Done. Hold a team launch workshop to agree how you will work and what "done" means.
  8. Set up the cadence. Choose a sprint length or Kanban review cadence, schedule the events and create the board. A sprint planning template helps the first planning session run smoothly.
  9. Agree how you will communicate with stakeholders. Decide who attends reviews, how progress is shared and how requests reach the Product Owner. A communication plan template keeps this simple.
  10. Protect the team for at least three months. Keep membership stable, limit outside requests and review how the team is working at each retrospective.

Worked example: forming an Agile team

This example is illustrative. A mid-sized insurer wants to rebuild its online claims journey. Today, a claim passes through a web team, a separate testing team and an operations team, and a small change takes around two months to go live.

Step 1: the skills needed

The work needs front-end development, back-end development, user experience design, testing, claims process knowledge, data analysis and release skills.

Step 2: the skills matrix

Candidate members are rated from 0 (no skill) to 4 (expert). The matrix shows where the team is strong and where it depends on one person.

Asha421200
Ben140212

Dev204110
Elif010421

Two risks stand out. Only Dev has strong design skills, and only Farid knows the claims process in depth. The team agrees that Asha will pair with Dev on design work and that Farid will run short sessions on claims rules in the first sprints. This is how T-shaped skills are built deliberately rather than hoped for.

Step 3: the team

  • Product Owner: a claims operations manager, given authority to order the backlog and four days a week for the role.
  • Scrum Master: an experienced coach, shared with one other team for the first six months.
  • Developers: the six people in the matrix.

That makes eight people in total, inside the typical size limit. Release skills are covered by Ben and Carla, with help from the platform team.

Step 4: the first three sprints

SprintFocusWhat the team learned
1First slice: report a simple claim online, end to end, in a test environmentThe team underestimated test data set-up; it added a test data item to the Definition of Done
2Add photo upload and status updatesThe Product Owner was too busy to answer questions; the team agreed a daily 15-minute slot with her
3First live release to a small group of customersReleasing within the team removed the old handoff to operations

By the third sprint the team has a working, released increment and a baseline velocity it can use for forecasting. The outcome measure the insurer cares about, time from idea to live change, can now be tracked sprint by sprint.

Agile team working agreement example

A working agreement is a short list of rules the team writes for itself. Keep it visible and revisit it at retrospectives. Here is an example:

  • Core collaboration hours are 10:00 to 15:00; meetings go in those hours only.
  • The Daily Scrum starts on time and lasts 15 minutes or less.
  • We finish work in progress before starting new items.
  • Anyone blocked for more than two hours says so in the team channel.
  • Every change is reviewed by a second person before it is done.
  • We disagree openly in the room and support the decision afterwards.
  • Cameras on for team events when working remotely, unless someone needs a break.
  • We review this agreement every third retrospective.
Tip

Write the working agreement with the whole team, not for it. Rules the team creates are followed; rules handed down are worked around.

Stages of Agile team development

Bruce Tuckman's model of team development describes stages that most new teams pass through. The stages are not strictly linear, and a team can slip back when membership changes.

StageWhat you seeWhat helps
FormingPoliteness, uncertainty, reliance on the coach or leadClear mission, roles and working agreement
StormingDisagreement about approach, priorities and standardsFacilitated retrospectives; making conflict safe and focused on work
NormingShared habits, growing trust, stable velocityGradually handing facilitation to the team
PerformingHigh autonomy, quick problem solving, reliable deliveryProtecting the team from reshuffles and outside interruptions
AdjourningThe team disbands or changes substantiallyCapture lessons; keep long-lived teams where possible

This is one reason Agile teams are kept stable: every change in membership restarts part of the cycle.

What makes an Agile team high-performing?

  • Psychological safety. Google's Project Aristotle, which studied 180 of its teams, found psychological safety to be the most important of five team dynamics, followed by dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact. People need to feel safe to admit mistakes, ask questions and challenge ideas.
  • A clear, meaningful goal. Teams perform better when they understand why the work matters and how it connects to customers.
  • Stable membership. Stable teams learn how each other work and make more reliable forecasts.
  • T-shaped skills. Deep expertise in one area plus the ability to help in others reduces bottlenecks and lets the team swarm on urgent work.
  • Autonomy with boundaries. The team decides how to work within clear constraints on budget, standards and goals.
  • Fast feedback. Frequent reviews with real users keep the team building the right thing.
  • Metrics used for learning. Track cycle time, throughput or velocity to improve the team's own forecasting. Never use velocity to compare teams. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs explains which measures help.

Leading and managing an Agile team

Agile teams still need leadership, but it looks different. Leaders set direction, provide context and remove obstacles, rather than planning and assigning tasks. The Scrum Guide describes Scrum Masters as true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the wider organisation.

  • Give the team outcomes to achieve, not task lists.
  • Shield the team from unplanned requests by routing them through the Product Owner.
  • Fix systemic problems the team cannot solve alone, such as slow approvals or missing environments.
  • Invest in skills, coaching and tools.
  • Ask questions in reviews rather than issuing instructions.

Agile teams outside software

Agile teams are now common in marketing, creative, operations, HR and even construction design. The principles carry over; the details change.

  • Marketing teams work from a campaign backlog, run short sprints and review results with data. See Agile marketing.
  • Operations and support teams often prefer Kanban, because work arrives continuously rather than in planned batches.
  • Creative teams use short review cycles with stakeholders to avoid late, large rounds of changes.

Tools for Agile teams

A good tool makes the team's work visible to the team and to stakeholders without extra reporting effort. Choose one that matches how your team works.

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, works well for Agile teams that collaborate with other departments: boards for sprint work, request forms for intake, dashboards for stakeholders and Gantt views when a release must fit a wider plan.
  • Jira offers deep support for software teams, with backlogs, sprints, story points and Agile reports.
  • monday.com is easy to adopt and suits business teams running Agile-style boards and sprints.

Start with the simplest set-up that shows the backlog, the current sprint or flow, and what is done. Add fields and automation only when the team asks for them.

Frequently asked questions

What is an Agile team in simple terms?

A small group of people who together have all the skills to deliver a piece of valuable work, who plan and organise that work themselves, and who deliver it in short cycles while getting regular feedback from the people who will use it.

What is the ideal Agile team size?

Most guidance points to around five to nine people. The 2020 Scrum Guide says a Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer, including the Product Owner and Scrum Master. Larger groups should split into several teams working from one backlog.

What roles are on an Agile team?

Usually a Product Owner, who decides what to build and in which order; a Scrum Master or Agile coach, who helps the team work effectively; and Developers, meaning everyone who does the work, including designers, testers and analysts.

Who leads an Agile team?

There is no single boss inside the team. The Product Owner leads on priorities, the Scrum Master leads on ways of working, and the Developers decide how to do the work. Line managers support people and the environment from outside the team.

What is the difference between an Agile team and a Scrum team?

A Scrum Team is one type of Agile team: it follows the Scrum framework, with a Product Owner, a Scrum Master, Developers, sprints and the Scrum events. Other Agile teams may use Kanban or a hybrid and have fewer defined roles.

What does cross-functional mean in an Agile team?

The team includes every skill needed to take work from idea to release, such as design, development, testing and analysis, so it does not wait on handoffs to other departments.

What does self-managing mean in an Agile team?

The team decides who does what, when and how, within the goals and boundaries set by the organisation and the Product Owner. Nobody outside the team assigns individual tasks.

Can someone be on more than one Agile team?

It is possible but costly. Switching between teams reduces focus, makes capacity hard to plan and slows every team involved. Aim for each person to belong mainly to one team; specialists who support several teams should plan their time explicitly.

Can Agile teams work remotely?

Yes. Many Agile teams are fully remote or hybrid. They rely more heavily on a shared digital board, agreed core hours, clear written communication and well-facilitated online events.

What is an Agile team working agreement?

A short set of rules the team writes for itself, covering things such as core hours, meeting etiquette, how to ask for help, code or content review and how decisions are made. It is reviewed regularly at retrospectives.

What are T-shaped skills?

Deep expertise in one area (the vertical bar of the T) combined with working knowledge of other areas (the horizontal bar). T-shaped team members can help each other, which reduces bottlenecks.

How long should an Agile team stay together?

As long as possible. Stable, long-lived teams learn to work well together and become more predictable. Bring new work to existing teams rather than forming a new team for every project.

Does an Agile team need a project manager?

Scrum has no project manager role, and the team manages its own work. Some organisations still use an Agile project manager to coordinate budgets, dependencies and governance across several teams, outside the team itself.
