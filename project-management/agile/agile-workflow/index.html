Agile Workflow: Stages, Examples and How to Design One

An Agile workflow is the sequence of states a piece of work moves through from idea to done, such as backlog, ready, in progress, review and done, usually shown as columns on a board. Making that path visible, agreeing rules for each step and limiting work in progress turns the board from a status display into a tool for finishing work faster. This guide explains the stages, compares Scrum and Kanban workflows, and walks you through designing your own with an illustrative example.

Key takeaways

  • An Agile workflow describes how individual work items flow from start to finish. It is different from the Agile lifecycle (ideation to retirement) and from the cadence of Sprint events.
  • Design the workflow from how work really happens today, not from an ideal process. Map the current steps first, then improve them.
  • Every column needs an entry policy: a short rule stating what must be true before a card can move in. Policies make quality and readiness explicit.
  • Work-in-progress (WIP) limits are what make a board a management tool. When a column is full, the team helps finish work downstream instead of starting more.
  • Measure flow with four metrics from the Kanban Guide: WIP, throughput, work item age and cycle time. Review them in retrospectives and change one thing at a time.
  • Scrum, Kanban and Scrumban teams all use workflows; they differ in cadence, how the board resets and how WIP is controlled.

What is an Agile workflow?

An Agile workflow is the series of states that a unit of work passes through, from the moment a team commits to it to the moment it is finished. It is usually shown as columns on a board, with explicit rules for moving between states and limits on work in progress, so the team can see and improve flow.

The idea comes from two places. Scrum Teams use a board to make their Sprint Backlog visible. Kanban teams treat the workflow itself as the thing to manage, with the goal of moving work through it smoothly and predictably. Most modern teams borrow from both.

A visible workflow helps in three ways:

  • Transparency. Everyone can see what is being worked on, what is waiting and what is blocked, without a status meeting.
  • Focus on finishing. Limiting work in progress shifts attention from starting new items to completing the ones already started.
  • Improvement. When the workflow is explicit, you can measure it, spot bottlenecks and test changes.

If you are new to boards, start with our guide to Kanban boards, which explains columns, cards and swimlanes.

Agile workflow vs lifecycle vs process

Search for 'Agile workflow' and you will find pages describing three different things. They all matter, but they answer different questions.

The stages of a typical Agile workflow

Most Agile workflows start from five stages. The entry policies below are examples; write your own to match your team's quality standards.

StageWhat it meansExample entry policyUsually pulled by
BacklogIdeas and requests, ordered by priorityHas a clear title and a requesterProduct Owner adds and orders
ReadyRefined and ready to startAcceptance criteria written, estimated, no open blocking questionsProduct Owner with the team
In progressActively being worked onSomeone has capacity and the column is under its WIP limitDevelopers or team members
Review or testBeing checked against acceptance criteriaAll tasks complete; automated tests passA different team member or reviewer
DoneMeets the Definition of DoneAccepted and, ideally, released or ready to releaseTeam, confirmed by the Product Owner where needed

Some teams add Released or Live after Done to separate 'finished by us' from 'in users' hands'. Others split Backlog into Options (not yet committed) and Committed so stakeholders can see what is actually planned. The Definition of Done is effectively the exit policy for the whole workflow.

What moves through the workflow? Usually user stories, bugs and other backlog items. Tasks inside a story often go on a separate task board or checklist. Our guide to epics vs stories vs tasks explains which level belongs where.

Elements of a well-defined workflow

The Kanban Guide by John Coleman and Daniel Vacanti (edition 2025.5, May 2025) sets out a minimum Definition of Workflow that any team, Kanban or not, can use as a checklist. According to the guide, it should state:

  1. The units of value that move through the workflow, called work items. Decide whether these are stories, tickets, requests or something else.
  2. When work items are started and finished. Agree the exact points. 'Started' might be when an item enters Ready or In progress; 'finished' might be Done or Released.
  3. One or more defined states between started and finished, such as development, review and test.
  4. How WIP will be controlled, for example column limits, a limit for the whole board or a limit per person.
  5. Explicit policies for how items flow through each state, such as entry rules and who can move a card.
  6. A service level expectation (SLE): a forecast of how long an item should take from start to finish, with a probability, for example '85% of items finish within 8 days'.

The guide adds three practices: defining and visualising the workflow, actively managing items in it, and improving it. The rest of this page follows that order.

Tip

Write your Definition of Workflow on one page and pin it to the board. New team members learn the rules in minutes, and arguments about whether a card 'should' move become a quick check against the policy.

Scrum, Kanban and Scrumban workflows compared

All three approaches use a visible workflow. They differ in how often the board resets and how work in progress is controlled.

CadenceFixed Sprints of one month or lessContinuous flow; cadences chosen by the teamRegular planning and review, continuous flow inside
What is on the boardItems in the current Sprint BacklogAll work in the systemWork in the system, often with a planning trigger

A Scrum board and a Kanban board can look almost identical. The difference is how the team uses them. Our guides to Scrum boards and Kanban vs Scrum cover the trade-offs in more depth.

How to design an Agile workflow, step by step

Allow 60 to 90 minutes with the whole team for the first version. Use a whiteboard or a blank board in your tool.

  1. Agree the unit of work. Decide what a card represents (a story, a request, a ticket) and roughly how big it should be. Mixed sizes make metrics hard to read.
  2. Map the current process. Take three or four recently finished items and trace every step they went through, including hand-offs and waiting. Draw what actually happened, not what the process document says. A quick value stream map helps here.
  3. Set the start and finish points. Mark where the team commits to an item (the start of your cycle time) and where it is truly finished.
  4. Choose the states. Turn the steps into columns. Combine steps that always happen together and drop any that add nothing. Most teams end up with four to seven columns.
  5. Show waiting explicitly. Split busy columns into 'doing' and 'done' sub-columns, or add a 'waiting for' column, so queues are visible instead of hidden inside 'In progress'.
  6. Write entry policies. For each column, write one to three rules a card must meet to enter it. Keep them short enough to fit on the column header.
  7. Set WIP limits. Start with a limit per active column of roughly the number of people who work in that column, or slightly more, then adjust from experience.
  8. Add swimlanes or classes of service if needed. An expedite lane with a limit of one handles genuine emergencies; lanes per product area or work type help larger teams. See our guide to Kanban swimlanes.
  9. Agree how you will measure. Record the date each item starts and finishes so you can calculate cycle time and throughput from day one. After a few weeks, use the data to set a service level expectation.
  10. Review and evolve. Inspect the workflow in every retrospective or monthly review. Change one thing at a time and watch the metrics.

Worked example: redesigning a product team's board

This illustrative example follows a team of six (four developers, one tester and one designer) building a customer portal.

Before

The board has three columns: To do, In progress and Done. In a typical week, about 18 items sit in In progress and the team finishes about 6 items. Stakeholders complain that nothing seems to finish, and the tester is overwhelmed at the end of every fortnight.

A quick calculation shows why. Little's Law says that, in a reasonably stable system, average cycle time equals average WIP divided by average throughput. With 18 items in progress and 6 finished per week, the average item takes about 3 weeks from start to finish.

Mapping the real process

Tracing four recent items shows the real steps: refine, design (for some items), develop, code review, test, Product Owner acceptance, release. Much of the 3 weeks was waiting: for review, for the tester, and for acceptance.

After

ColumnEntry policyWIP limit
BacklogOrdered by the Product OwnerNone
ReadyAcceptance criteria agreed; design attached if needed; estimated8
Development: doingA developer has capacity4
Development: doneCode complete, unit tests passShared with the column above
Code reviewPull request opened2
TestReview approved, deployed to test environment3
AcceptanceAll acceptance criteria pass2
DoneMeets the Definition of Done; released or in the next releaseNone

An expedite swimlane with a limit of one handles urgent production fixes. Developers agree that when Code review or Test is full, they help review or test before starting new work.

Result

The active columns now hold at most 11 items. If throughput stays at about 6 items per week, Little's Law suggests an average cycle time of under 2 weeks (11 ÷ 6 ≈ 1.8 weeks), without anyone working harder. In practice, finishing work sooner often raises throughput too, because there is less context switching. After six weeks of recorded data, the team sets a service level expectation from its own cycle times, such as '85% of items finish within 10 working days', and reviews it monthly.

Tip

Little's Law describes averages in a stable system. Use it to understand why too much WIP slows everything down, not as a precise forecast for an individual item.

WIP limits in practice

A work-in-progress limit caps how many items can be in a column, a group of columns or the whole workflow at once. Limits create a pull system: work moves forward only when the next step has room, which exposes bottlenecks quickly.

Setting the first limits

  • Count the people who work in each active column and start with a limit equal to that number or slightly above it.
  • For shared steps such as review, start low (two or three) so queues are noticed early.
  • Apply a limit to 'doing' and 'done' sub-columns together, so finished work waiting for the next step still counts.
  • Do not limit the Backlog or Done columns.

When a column is full

  1. Stop starting new work in the upstream column.
  2. Look right on the board: can you help finish, review or test something already in progress?
  3. If an item is blocked, swarm on the blocker or escalate it.
  4. If a limit is constantly hit and nobody can help, discuss in the retrospective whether the limit, the policy or the team's skills mix needs to change.

Treat breaking a limit as a signal to discuss, not a crime. Our guide to WIP limits covers more ways to set and adjust them.

Measuring and improving your workflow

The Kanban Guide names four flow measures. Together they tell you whether work is flowing smoothly.

MetricDefinitionWhat to look for
WIPNumber of items started but not finishedRising WIP usually means rising cycle times
ThroughputNumber of items finished per unit of timeStable or rising throughput shows predictability
Work item ageTime since an item started, for items still in progressOld items are at risk; discuss them in the Daily Scrum
Cycle timeTime from start to finish for completed itemsUse percentiles (for example the 85th) to set expectations

Cycle time and throughput look backwards; work item age looks at what is happening now, which makes a WIP aging chart one of the most useful daily views. A cumulative flow diagram shows how much work sits in each state over time: a widening band means a queue is growing.

You can also calculate flow efficiency: active working time divided by total cycle time. If an item took 10 days and someone actively worked on it for 2, its flow efficiency is 20%. Low figures are common and point to waiting, not slow people.

Questions for your next retrospective

  • Where do cards pile up, and why?
  • Which items aged far beyond our usual cycle time, and what held them up?
  • How often do cards move backwards (for example from Test to Develop)? What would prevent that?
  • Are any policies ignored? Should we enforce them or change them?
  • What is one change we will try before the next review?

Automating your workflow in tools

A physical board works well for co-located teams, but most teams now run their workflow in software. Whatever tool you use, configure it to match the workflow you designed rather than adopting its default columns.

  • Statuses and columns: create a status for each state and map statuses to board columns.
  • WIP limits: many tools let you set column limits; check whether yours blocks moves or only warns.
  • Automation rules: for example, move a card to Code review when a pull request opens, assign the reviewer, or notify the requester when an item reaches Done.
  • Required fields: enforce entry policies by requiring fields such as acceptance criteria before a status change.
  • Reporting: turn on cycle time, throughput or cumulative flow reports, or export dates to a spreadsheet.

Wrike, our top-ranked tool, suits organisations that want custom workflows, request forms and cross-project dashboards in one place, and its board view is available even on the Free plan. Jira is the most complete option for software teams that need Scrum and Kanban boards with configurable workflows. monday.com is strong for visual, easy-to-adopt workflows across non-technical teams, and Trello is a simple way to start with Kanban boards.

Whichever tool you choose, set up the workflow on paper first. Teams that start from a tool's default template often end up with columns that do not match their work, and then stop trusting the board.

Who owns and changes the workflow

The workflow belongs to the people who do the work. In Scrum, the Developers own the Sprint Backlog and how they organise it, so they decide the columns, policies and limits. The Scrum Master helps the team see problems in the flow and coaches them to improve it, and the Product Owner influences what enters the workflow through the order of the Product Backlog.

Stakeholders and managers should be able to see the board and its metrics, but they should not add columns or bypass WIP limits without the team. If a stakeholder needs a different view, such as a status summary across several teams, create a report or dashboard rather than changing the team's workflow.

Agree a simple rule for changes: anyone can propose one, the team decides at its next retrospective or review, and the change is written into the policies on the board.

Agile workflow checklist

Use this checklist when you set up a new board or audit an existing one.

  • Everyone can say what one card represents.
  • Start and finish points are agreed and recorded with dates.
  • Columns reflect real steps, including waiting states.
  • Each column has a written entry policy.
  • Active columns have WIP limits, and the team knows what to do when one is full.
  • Blocked items are marked visibly and discussed daily.
  • The Definition of Done is visible and used as the final exit policy.
  • Cycle time, throughput, work item age and WIP are tracked.
  • A service level expectation is set from the team's own data.
  • The workflow is reviewed at least once a month.

If your workflow keeps growing more complex, step back and ask whether it reflects one team's work or several. Splitting a board by team or value stream is often simpler than adding more columns. Frequently asked questions

What is an Agile workflow?

The series of stages work passes through from commitment to done, such as backlog, ready, in progress, review and done, usually shown as columns on a board with policies and WIP limits.

What are the stages of an Agile workflow?

A typical flow is backlog, ready, in progress, review or test, and done. Many teams add released, waiting or acceptance states. Adapt the stages to how your work actually happens.

What are the phases of the Agile lifecycle?

Commonly described phases are ideation, inception, iteration, release, maintenance and retirement. The lifecycle covers a whole product, while a workflow describes how individual work items move.

How do you create an Agile workflow?

Agree the unit of work, map how work really flows today, set start and finish points, choose states, show waiting, write column policies, set WIP limits, record dates for metrics and review regularly.

Is an Agile workflow the same as a Kanban board?

Not quite. The workflow is the set of states and rules; a Kanban board is the most common way to visualise it. Scrum Teams use similar boards for their Sprint Backlog.

How many columns should an Agile board have?

As few as possible to show the real stages. Most teams use four to seven columns, plus sub-columns where waiting time matters.

What is a WIP limit?

A cap on how many items can be in a workflow stage at once. When a column is full, the team helps finish work downstream instead of starting more.

What are column policies?

Short, explicit rules stating what must be true for a card to enter or leave a column, such as having acceptance criteria before moving to Ready.

What is a service level expectation?

A forecast of how long a work item should take from start to finish, with a probability, for example '85% of items finish within 8 days'. The Kanban Guide includes it in the Definition of Workflow.

What is the difference between a Scrum and a Kanban workflow?

A Scrum board usually shows the current Sprint's items and resets each Sprint, with WIP controlled through the Sprint forecast. A Kanban workflow is continuous and uses explicit WIP limits.

How do you measure an Agile workflow?

Track WIP, throughput, work item age and cycle time. A cumulative flow diagram and a WIP aging chart help you spot queues and stuck items.

How is an Agile workflow different from a waterfall workflow?

A waterfall workflow moves the whole project through phases in sequence. An Agile workflow moves many small items through short, repeated stages, so value is delivered and feedback gathered continuously.

Can non-software teams use an Agile workflow?

Yes. Marketing, support, HR, recruitment, design and operations teams all use boards with stages that reflect their own work.

What is a swimlane in an Agile workflow?

A horizontal row that separates types of work, such as an expedite lane for urgent items or a lane per product area.
