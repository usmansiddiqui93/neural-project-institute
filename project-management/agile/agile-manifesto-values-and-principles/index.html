The Agile Manifesto: 4 Values and 12 Principles Explained

The Agile Manifesto is a short statement of four values and twelve principles, written by seventeen software practitioners in February 2001, that defines what it means to work in an Agile way. It values individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change more than processes and tools, comprehensive documentation, contract negotiation and following a plan. This guide explains each value and principle in plain English, with practical examples, the history behind it, common misreadings and a step-by-step way to apply it in your own team.

Key takeaways

  • The Manifesto has four values and twelve principles. The values say what to prioritise; the principles say how to work.
  • Each value ranks one thing above another. The items on the right (processes, documentation, contracts, plans) still matter; they come second.
  • It is not a method. Scrum, Kanban, XP and other frameworks are ways of putting the Manifesto into practice.
  • Agile teams still plan. They plan in layers, in detail only for the near term, and keep every plan easy to change.
  • Replace "working software" with "working results" and the Manifesto applies to almost any knowledge work.
  • The text has not changed since 2001. It remains the shared reference point for every Agile framework.

What is the Agile Manifesto?

The Agile Manifesto, officially the *Manifesto for Agile Software Development*, is a one-page declaration published in 2001 that sets out four values and twelve supporting principles for developing software in an adaptive, collaborative and customer-focused way. It does not prescribe roles, meetings or tools; instead, it describes the priorities that any Agile method should share.

The Manifesto opens with a short preamble. Its authors say they are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it, and that through this work they have come to value the four statements that follow. It closes with the line that is most often forgotten: while there is value in the items on the right, they value the items on the left more.

That structure matters. The Manifesto is not a list of rules; it is a set of trade-offs. When two good things pull in different directions, such as following the plan versus responding to new information, it tells you which one to favour. To see how these ideas shape a whole approach to delivery, read our overview of Agile methodology.

The four values of the Agile Manifesto

Each value compares two things that both have value. The Agile preference is for the item on the left.

We value……overWhat it means in practiceExample
Individuals and interactionsProcesses and toolsTalk to each other before reaching for a form or a ticket. Tools should serve the team, not the other way round.A developer walks over (or starts a call) to clarify a requirement instead of raising a ticket and waiting two days.
Working softwareComprehensive documentationMeasure progress by results people can use. Write the documentation that is genuinely needed.The team demonstrates a working checkout page at the end of the sprint instead of presenting a 40-page design specification.
Customer collaborationContract negotiationWork with the customer throughout, rather than relying on a fixed specification signed at the start.The client joins each review and reprioritises the backlog, instead of raising change requests against the contract.
Responding to changeFollowing a planPlans are useful, but when the facts change, the plan should change too.User testing shows customers ignore a planned feature, so the team drops it and builds what testing revealed people need.
Tip

A common misreading is that Agile rejects documentation, contracts, processes or plans. It does not. The Manifesto says plainly that the items on the right have value; it ranks them below the things they are meant to support.

1. Individuals and interactions over processes and tools

Processes and tools exist to help people work together. When they start to replace conversation, for example when every question must go through a ticketing system, they slow teams down and hide misunderstandings. In practice this value leads to small, cross-functional teams, frequent short conversations, and tools chosen by the team to fit how it works. Outside software, a marketing team might replace a weekly email status round-up with a ten-minute stand-up.

2. Working software over comprehensive documentation

Before 2001, many software projects produced extensive requirements and design documents long before anything ran. The Manifesto shifts the measure of progress to something users can actually try. Documentation still has a place: user guides, regulatory evidence, architecture decisions and handover notes are often essential. The question is whether each document helps someone, not whether it completes a template. For teams outside software, read "working software" as "working results": a published campaign, a piloted process or a tested prototype.

3. Customer collaboration over contract negotiation

A contract that fixes every requirement at the start assumes the customer knows exactly what they need. Often they do not, and both sides spend energy arguing about scope rather than building the right thing. Agile teams involve the customer (or a product owner representing them) throughout, show work regularly and adjust priorities together. Contracts still exist; Agile-friendly contracts tend to fix the budget and timebox and let scope flex, or pay per increment delivered.

4. Responding to change over following a plan

Plans are made with the best information available at the time, which is usually the least information you will ever have. This value asks teams to treat new information, such as user feedback, market shifts or technical discoveries, as a reason to update the plan rather than as a threat to it. It does not mean having no plan; Agile teams plan often, in short cycles, and keep plans light enough to change cheaply. The section on planning below explains how.

Which value matters most?

The Manifesto does not rank its four values against each other. Many practitioners point to individuals and interactions as the foundation, because the other three depend on it: collaborating with customers, delivering working results and responding to change all require people who communicate well and trust each other. If a team gets that right, the other values become much easier to live by. Our guide to the Agile mindset explores the behaviours behind this value.

The twelve principles behind the Agile Manifesto

Behind the values sit twelve principles that turn them into practical guidance. The table gives a short form of each principle as published on agilemanifesto.org, with what it means for a team today.

#Principle (short form of the official wording)What it means in practice
1Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.Deliver something useful soon, then keep delivering. Value reaches users in small pieces rather than one big release.
2Welcome changing requirements, even late in development.Treat change as an opportunity to give the customer an advantage, not as a failure of planning.
3Deliver working software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale.Work in short cycles. Many teams now release far more often than every two weeks.
4Business people and developers must work together daily throughout the project.Close, continuous collaboration between the people who know the problem and the people who build the solution.
5Build projects around motivated individuals; give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done.Hire good people, remove obstacles and avoid micromanagement.
6Face-to-face conversation is the most efficient and effective way of conveying information to and within a team.Prefer direct conversation. For remote teams, a video call usually beats a long message thread.
7Working software is the primary measure of progress.Judge progress by what is done and usable, not by documents produced or hours spent.
8Agile processes promote sustainable development; sponsors, developers and users should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely.No permanent crunch. A steady pace protects quality, morale and predictability.
9Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility.Quality makes change cheap. Cutting corners builds up debt that slows everything later.
10Simplicity, the art of maximising the amount of work not done, is essential.Build only what is needed. Every feature not built is time saved for something more valuable.
11The best architectures, requirements and designs emerge from self-organising teams.The people closest to the work should decide how to do it.
12At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behaviour accordingly.Hold regular retrospectives and act on what you learn.

The principles are easier to remember in five themes. The examples below show each theme in a software team and a non-software team.

Delivering value (principles 1–3)

These principles shift the goal from finishing a plan to delivering value early and often. A software team that once released twice a year moves to a release at the end of every two-week sprint. An HR team redesigning onboarding releases a new first-day checklist to one department, gathers feedback and improves it before rolling it out more widely. Both get feedback months earlier than a big-bang launch would allow. Delivering in small pieces is the idea behind iterative and incremental development.

Working together (principles 4–6)

Agile depends on short feedback loops between the people who need something and the people who make it. In Scrum, the product owner fills this role every day; in a marketing team, the campaign owner sits with the designers and copywriters rather than reviewing work only at the end. Trust is central: principle 5 asks leaders to give motivated people what they need and then step back.

Measuring and sustaining progress (principles 7–8)

Progress is measured by results, which is why Agile teams agree a Definition of Done and demonstrate finished work at reviews. Sustainable pace means planning to a capacity the team can keep up indefinitely. A team that hits every deadline through weekend work is not succeeding; it is borrowing against future quality and retention.

Quality and simplicity (principles 9–10)

Technical excellence keeps the cost of change low. Teams that skip testing and refactoring accumulate technical debt, and every later change becomes slower and riskier. Simplicity is the partner principle: build the smallest thing that meets the need. Writing work as small, testable user stories helps teams stay focused on what users actually need.

Self-organisation and improvement (principles 11–12)

Teams that decide how to do their own work tend to find better solutions and own the results. Regular reflection, usually a sprint retrospective, is how they get better over time. Our free sprint retrospective template gives you a simple structure to start.

Common misreadings of the Manifesto

Because the Manifesto is short, it is easy to quote half of a sentence. These are the misreadings we see most often.

MisreadingWhat the Manifesto actually says
"Agile means no documentation."Documentation has value; working results come first. Write what is genuinely useful.
"Agile means no planning."Responding to change is preferred over following a plan, not over planning. Agile teams plan frequently.
"Agile means no deadlines."Nothing in the Manifesto rules out dates. Many teams fix the date and let scope flex.
"Agile means no process."Processes and tools have value. They should serve people rather than replace conversation.
"Agile means the customer can change anything at any time."Change is welcomed, but it is managed through collaboration and prioritisation, such as at the start of each iteration.
"Agile is Scrum."Scrum is one framework that applies the Manifesto. Kanban, XP and others do too.
"Agile is only for software."The wording is about software, but the values apply to most knowledge work. See is Agile only for software?

Who wrote the Agile Manifesto, and why?

According to the official history on agilemanifesto.org, seventeen people met at The Lodge at Snowbird ski resort in Utah on 11–13 February 2001 to talk, ski and look for common ground. They represented a range of so-called lightweight methods, including Extreme Programming (XP), Scrum, DSDM, Adaptive Software Development, Crystal, Feature-Driven Development and Pragmatic Programming, along with others who wanted an alternative to heavyweight, documentation-driven processes.

The seventeen signatories were Kent Beck, Mike Beedle, Arie van Bennekum, Alistair Cockburn, Ward Cunningham, Martin Fowler, James Grenning, Jim Highsmith, Andrew Hunt, Ron Jeffries, Jon Kern, Brian Marick, Robert C. Martin, Steve Mellor, Ken Schwaber, Jeff Sutherland and Dave Thomas. They agreed on the word "Agile" and the four values at the meeting, and the group named itself the Agile Alliance; the twelve principles were refined by the group shortly afterwards.

What united them was frustration with projects that spent months on documents and approvals before delivering anything, then discovered too late that requirements had changed. For the longer story, from 1950s iterative development to today's frameworks, read our history of Agile.

How the Manifesto addresses planning

"Responding to change over following a plan" is often read as "don't plan". In practice, Agile teams plan more often than traditional teams, just at different levels of detail and over different horizons.

LevelHorizonDetailTypical output
Vision1–3 yearsVery lowProduct vision and goals
Roadmap3–12 monthsLow: themes and outcomesAgile roadmap
Release1–6 monthsMedium: likely featuresRelease plan
Iteration1–4 weeksHigh: committed workSprint goal and backlog
Day24 hoursVery highPlan agreed at the daily stand-up

The principle is to plan in detail only as far ahead as you can see clearly, and to keep every plan easy to change. That is what makes responding to change affordable. For a side-by-side comparison with plan-driven delivery, read Agile vs Waterfall.

How to apply the Manifesto: a step-by-step team workshop

Reading the Manifesto is easy; living by it is harder. This 90-minute workshop helps a team see where it stands and pick a few concrete improvements.

  1. Read the values and principles together (10 minutes). Ask each person which statement they find most and least true of the team today.
  2. Score each value (15 minutes). Everyone privately scores how well the team lives each value, from 1 (rarely) to 5 (consistently). Reveal scores together and note the spread.
  3. Discuss the biggest gaps (25 minutes). Focus on the lowest-scoring value and the value with the widest disagreement. Ask for specific recent examples.
  4. Map gaps to principles (15 minutes). Use the mapping table above to find the principles behind each gap.
  5. Agree two or three experiments (15 minutes). Each should be small, have an owner and be reviewable in the next retrospective.
  6. Review and repeat (10 minutes now, then quarterly). Re-score in three months to see whether the experiments helped.

Worked example

A six-person product team runs the workshop. The average scores and agreed actions look like this:

ValueAverage score (1–5)Example givenExperiment agreed
Individuals and interactions4.2Good daily conversation within the teamNone needed now
Working software2.5Features sit "done but not released" for weeksRelease to production at least once per sprint
Customer collaboration2.0Stakeholders see work only at quarterly demosInvite two customer representatives to every sprint review
Responding to change3.4Plan changes are possible but slow to approveProduct owner can reorder the top of the backlog without sign-off

The lowest score, customer collaboration, links to principles 1 and 4. The team's first experiment addresses it directly. Three months later, the team re-scores and finds customer collaboration has risen, while working software still needs attention, so it adds an experiment on automated testing (principle 9). Is the Agile Manifesto still relevant?

Yes. The wording reflects software development in 2001, but the trade-offs it describes still apply wherever work is uncertain and feedback is possible. Every major Agile framework still refers back to it, and many modern practices, such as continuous delivery, DevOps and product discovery, are extensions of its principles, especially early and continuous delivery and technical excellence.

Criticisms worth knowing

  • It is software-centric. The language of developers and working software can feel foreign to other teams, although the ideas translate well.
  • It is easy to misuse. Teams sometimes cite it to avoid documentation or planning that is genuinely needed, which it does not support.
  • It is silent on scale. It says little about large organisations, portfolios or regulated environments, which is why frameworks such as SAFe and LeSS emerged.
  • The authors were a narrow group. Commentators have noted that the seventeen signatories came from similar backgrounds, which shaped its emphasis.

Later statements

The Manifesto itself has not been revised. Some practitioners have written their own short statements to restate Agile for today: for example, Joshua Kerievsky's *Modern Agile* and Alistair Cockburn's *Heart of Agile*. These are separate works by individuals, not updates to the Manifesto.

Frequently asked questions

Who wrote the Agile Manifesto?

Seventeen software practitioners, including Kent Beck, Martin Fowler, Ken Schwaber, Jeff Sutherland, Alistair Cockburn, Ward Cunningham and Robert C. Martin. They represented methods such as XP, Scrum, DSDM, Crystal and Feature-Driven Development.

When and where was the Agile Manifesto written?

At a meeting held on 11–13 February 2001 at The Lodge at Snowbird ski resort in Utah, USA. See our history of Agile for the background.

What are the 4 values of the Agile Manifesto?

Individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; and responding to change over following a plan.

How many principles are in the Agile Manifesto?

Twelve. They cover early and continuous delivery, welcoming change, frequent delivery, daily collaboration, motivated individuals, face-to-face conversation, working software as the measure of progress, sustainable pace, technical excellence, simplicity, self-organising teams and regular reflection.

Which Agile value is the most important?

The Manifesto does not rank its values, but many practitioners see individuals and interactions as the foundation, because collaboration, delivery and responding to change all depend on people communicating well.

What is the most important Agile principle?

The principles are not ranked either, but the first, satisfying the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable work, is often seen as the purpose that the other eleven serve.

Does the Agile Manifesto say documentation is bad?

No. It values working results more than comprehensive documentation and states that the items on the right still have value. Teams should write the documentation that is genuinely useful.

Is the Agile Manifesto a methodology?

No. It is a short statement of values and principles. Frameworks such as Scrum, Kanban and XP are the methods that put it into practice.

What is the difference between the Agile Manifesto and Scrum?

The Manifesto describes values and principles shared by all Agile approaches. Scrum is one specific framework, with defined accountabilities, events and artefacts, that applies those values.

Is the Agile Manifesto still relevant?

Yes. Its wording is software-focused, but its values describe how most successful knowledge-work teams operate, and it remains the shared reference point for every Agile framework.

Has the Agile Manifesto ever been updated?

No. The text published in 2001 has not been revised. Later statements such as Modern Agile and Heart of Agile are separate works by individual authors.

Can I reproduce the Agile Manifesto text?

The authors allow the Manifesto to be freely copied in any form, but only in its entirety and with their notice. The official text is published at agilemanifesto.org.

What does sustainable pace mean in the Agile Manifesto?

Teams, sponsors and users should be able to keep a constant pace indefinitely, without relying on overtime. Constant crunch lowers quality and leads to burnout, which undermines delivery.

Can non-software teams use the Agile Manifesto?

Yes. Read "working software" as "working results", such as a live campaign or a piloted process, and the values and principles apply to marketing, HR, research, events and many other teams.
