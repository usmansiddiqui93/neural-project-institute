Agile Gantt Chart: How to Use Gantt Charts in Agile

An Agile Gantt chart is a high-level timeline that shows sprints, releases, epics and key milestones against dates, rather than a detailed schedule of every task. Used at that level, and updated after every sprint, a Gantt chart helps Agile teams communicate plans and dependencies to stakeholders without fixing scope in advance.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 6 September 2026
  • Last updated 14 October 2026
  • 18 min read

Key takeaways

  • Gantt charts can work in Agile if they show sprints, releases, epics and external milestones, not individual tasks.
  • Place epics using a velocity range, and show confidence: committed for the next sprint, forecast for the next few, indicative beyond that.
  • Treat the chart as a forecast, not a promise: scope is the variable, and the chart is updated after every sprint review.
  • It is most useful for stakeholder communication, cross-team dependencies, fixed external dates and hybrid projects.
  • Keep daily work on the task board and the backlog; the Gantt chart should be generated from them, not maintained separately.
  • Pair it with a release burnup chart, which shows the real rate of progress behind the bars.

What is an Agile Gantt chart?

An Agile Gantt chart is a timeline view of an Agile plan that shows time-boxes (sprints or iterations), releases, epics or features and key milestones as bars and markers against a calendar. Unlike a traditional Gantt chart, it does not schedule individual tasks, and its later bars are forecasts based on the team's measured velocity, not commitments.

Think of it as a picture of the release plan. The detail of what the team is doing this week lives in the sprint backlog and on the task board. The Gantt chart answers different questions: roughly when will each large piece of work land, what depends on what, and are we still on course for the dates that matter?

Its main audiences are stakeholders outside the team (sponsors, senior managers, marketing, operations, clients), other teams that depend on the work, and project managers or PMOs who need to fit Agile delivery into a wider plan.

Can you use a Gantt chart in Agile?

Yes, with care. Nothing in Agile forbids a timeline. The Agile Manifesto values "responding to change over following a plan", while saying there is value in the items on the right, plans included. The Scrum Guide does not mention Gantt charts at all; it neither requires nor bans them, and leaves the choice of planning practices to the team.

The problem is not bars on a calendar. It is a particular style of Gantt chart: every task listed, fixed dates for months ahead, and a baseline used to judge the team. That style assumes the scope is known and stable, which is exactly what Agile does not assume.

Why Agile teams are wary of Gantt charts

  • False precision. A bar for a task in five months' time looks as certain as a bar for tomorrow, even though it is a guess.
  • Fixed scope by stealth. Once a detailed chart is shared, stakeholders tend to treat every bar as a promise, which undermines the Product Owner's freedom to reorder the backlog.
  • Maintenance effort. A task-level chart goes out of date within days in a sprint-based team and becomes a second plan that someone has to keep in step with the backlog.
  • Top-down control. Detailed charts can encourage managers to assign and schedule work, rather than letting the team plan its own sprint.

Where a Gantt chart helps Agile teams

  • Communicating a release plan to stakeholders who are used to timelines.
  • Coordinating several teams whose work depends on each other.
  • Showing fixed external dates, such as a regulatory deadline, trade show or contract milestone.
  • Hybrid projects where some workstreams (procurement, construction, training) are planned in a predictive way and others iteratively.
  • Governance and portfolio reporting, where a PMO needs one view across Agile and non-Agile projects.

The answer is to change the level of detail and the meaning of the bars, not to avoid the format.

When to use an Agile Gantt chart (and when not to)

An Agile Gantt chart earns its place when someone outside the team needs to understand timing. Use one when:

  • Stakeholders ask "when will we get it?" and a release burnup on its own does not answer the question in a form they use.
  • Two or more teams depend on each other's epics, so sequence matters.
  • The release has a fixed external date and you need to show which scope is safe and which is at risk.
  • The project mixes Agile delivery with predictive workstreams that are already on a timeline.
  • Your PMO or governance board requires a timeline view for every project.

Skip it, or keep it very light, when:

  • A single team works on a continuous flow of small items, as in many Kanban teams; a Kanban board and cycle time data serve better.
  • There are no external dates and stakeholders are happy with the product backlog and sprint reviews.
  • Nobody would read it. A chart produced only because "we always have one" is waste.
Tip

If a stakeholder insists on a task-level Gantt chart, ask which decisions they need it for. Usually the real need is a date for a few key outcomes and early warning of risk, and an epic-level chart meets that need better.

Leave out individual user stories, tasks, named people and hours. Those belong in the backlog and on the board. If you need a refresher on the hierarchy, see epics vs stories vs tasks.

Three levels of confidence

  • Committed: the current or next sprint, based on sprint planning. Show as solid bars.
  • Forecast: roughly the next two to four sprints, based on the velocity range and a refined backlog. Show as shaded bars.
  • Indicative: anything further out, based on rough epic sizes. Show as outlined or hatched bars, and say so in the legend.

How to create an Agile Gantt chart step by step

  1. Set the horizon and the time-boxes. Decide what the chart covers, usually one release or one quarter. Add each sprint as a fixed-length block with its dates, and mark holidays or other known capacity changes.
  2. Mark the fixed dates. Add release dates and any external milestones: regulatory deadlines, events, supplier deliveries, contract dates.
  3. Size the epics. With the Product Owner and team, make sure each epic in the release has a rough size in story points or a similar unit. See our guide to Agile epics.
  4. Work out capacity as a range. Use the team's recent velocity to give a low, typical and high figure per sprint, adjusted for holidays. A range is more honest than a single number, and it lets you show which scope is safe and which is at risk.
  5. Place the epics in priority order. Take the epics in product backlog order and fill each sprint up to typical capacity. Each epic's bar spans the sprints it is expected to use.
  6. Add dependencies between epics and teams only. Draw links where one epic needs another, or needs an external milestone. Do not link stories or tasks. Our task dependencies guide covers link types.
  7. Mark confidence. Make the near-term bars solid and the later ones shaded or hatched, and add a short legend.
  8. Check the forecast against the range. Compare total scope with low and high capacity. If the release only fits at the high end, decide now which epic is the first to move, and label it.
  9. Agree the update rhythm. Update the chart after every sprint review, and agree with stakeholders that it is a forecast that will change.

Worked example: a six-sprint release plan

A team is building the first release of a fitness studio's class booking app. It works in two-week sprints, and its velocity over the last six sprints has ranged from 26 to 34 story points, averaging 30.

Tip

Give stakeholders the forecast as a range, not a single date: "At our usual pace all five epics will be in the 2 April release. If we run at our slowest recent pace, Reminders will move to the next release."

Updating the chart after a sprint review

The value of an Agile Gantt chart comes from keeping it honest. Here is what happens to the example at the end of sprint 2 (5 February).

What has changed

  • Progress: the team completed 27 points in sprint 1 and 31 in sprint 2, a total of 58. The timetable is done and 33 of the 40 booking points are done.
  • New scope: at the sprint review, the studio owner asked for class reviews (15 points), and the Product Owner placed it above Payments.
  • External dependency: the payment provider now says the sandbox API will be ready on 1 March, a week late, which is the second week of sprint 4.

Recalculating

Remaining scope is 160 − 58 + 15 = 117 points. Remaining capacity (sprints 3 to 5 plus the short sprint 6) is 99 at low velocity, 114 at typical and 129 at high. At typical velocity the plan is about 3 points short; at low velocity it is 18 points short. The Product Owner decides to move Reminders (20 points) to the next release, which brings remaining scope to 97 points: within capacity even at the low end.

When you share the updated chart, say what moved and why in two or three lines. Stakeholders accept change much more readily when they can see the reasons and the trade-off, and when the change was flagged as possible from the start.

Pair the chart with a release burnup, which plots completed points against total scope sprint by sprint, so people can see the trend behind the bars.

Agile Gantt charts for multiple teams

Gantt-style timelines are most valuable when several teams contribute to one release, because the main risk moves from "how fast is the team?" to "will the pieces arrive in the right order?"

  • Use one swimlane per team, with epics or features in each lane and the shared release dates across the top.
  • Show only cross-team dependencies. An arrow from team A's API feature to team B's integration feature is exactly what the chart is for.
  • Put the provider's delivery at least one sprint before the consumer needs it. That gives the consuming team time to integrate.
  • Review it in a joint session, such as a Scrum of Scrums or a quarterly planning event, not separately with each team.

In the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe), the equivalent is the ART planning board (formerly called the program board), built during PI planning. It shows features by team and iteration, with dependencies linked by red string and milestones along the top. An Agile Gantt chart is a calendar-based version of the same idea for organisations that do not use SAFe.

Using an Agile Gantt chart on hybrid projects

Many projects mix approaches: software built in sprints alongside procurement, construction, legal approvals, training or a marketing launch planned in a predictive way. A Gantt chart is often the one view that can show both.

  • Show predictive workstreams at task or phase level, with their normal dependencies.
  • Show Agile workstreams at epic or release level, with sprint blocks and confidence styling.
  • Join them only at the hand-off points: for example, "training materials can start once the booking epic is in a release candidate".
  • Agree which dates are fixed (usually the external ones) and which scope is flexible (usually the lower-priority epics).

This keeps the parts of the plan that should be firm firm, without pretending the Agile work is more certain than it is. Our Agile roadmap guide covers the strategy level above this.

Tools and templates for Agile Gantt charts

The best set-up is a tool that draws the timeline from the same backlog the team uses, so the chart updates when the backlog does. Most mainstream project management tools now combine boards with timeline or Gantt views:

  • Wrike offers interactive Gantt charts with dependencies, critical path and baselines from its Team plan, alongside board and table views. It suits hybrid projects and PMOs that need Agile and predictive work in one place.
  • Jira is built around Scrum and Kanban boards and includes a timeline view on all plans, with advanced cross-team planning on Premium. It suits software teams who want the timeline generated from epics in the backlog.
  • monday.com offers timeline and Gantt views from its Standard plan, which makes it easy to show the same items as a board and a timeline.
  • Asana includes timeline and Gantt views with dependencies and milestones on paid plans.
  • ClickUp combines sprints, boards and a Gantt view in one workspace.
  • Smartsheet suits teams who like a spreadsheet grid with Gantt and dependencies on top.

Pros and cons of Gantt charts in Agile

Pros

  • Familiar format for stakeholders and governance boards
  • Makes cross-team and external dependencies visible
  • Shows fixed dates and what scope is at risk
  • Bridges Agile and predictive workstreams on hybrid projects
  • Helps other functions (marketing, support, training) plan around releases

Cons

  • Easily read as a fixed promise if confidence is not shown
  • Extra maintenance if it is not generated from the backlog
  • Tempts people to add task-level detail
  • Does not show the rate of progress on its own (use a burnup)
  • Can encourage top-down scheduling of team work

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Putting tasks on the chart. Task-level bars create false precision and constant rework. Stop at epics or features.
  • Showing every bar as equally certain. Use committed, forecast and indicative styling, and explain it in a legend.
  • Using a single velocity figure. Plan with a range, and show which scope is at risk at the low end.
  • Keeping the chart separate from the backlog. Two plans always drift apart. Generate the timeline from the backlog in your tool, or update the chart at every sprint review without fail.
  • Treating changes as failures. Moving an epic to the next release because something more valuable came up is Agile working as intended. Explain the trade-off rather than apologising for it.
  • Linking everything to everything. Show only the dependencies between epics, teams and external milestones that could actually delay the release.
  • Updating it daily. The chart is a release-level view. Daily changes belong on the board; update the chart after each sprint review or when a major forecast changes.
  • Forgetting capacity changes. Holidays, onboarding and planned absences reduce velocity. In the example, the Easter sprint was planned at 80%.

Frequently asked questions

Can you use a Gantt chart in Agile?

Yes, at a high level. Show sprints, releases, epics and external milestones rather than individual tasks, mark later bars as forecasts, and update the chart after each sprint review.

What is an Agile Gantt chart?

A timeline view of an Agile plan that shows sprints, releases, epics or features and key milestones against dates. Later bars are forecasts based on the team's velocity, not fixed commitments.

Is a Gantt chart Agile or Waterfall?

The format itself is neutral. It is most associated with Waterfall because traditional Gantt charts schedule every task in advance. What makes a Gantt chart fit Agile is the level of detail (epics, not tasks) and treating it as a forecast that changes.

Does Scrum use Gantt charts?

The Scrum Guide does not mention Gantt charts and does not prescribe any planning chart. Some Scrum teams use a high-level timeline for release planning and stakeholder reporting, alongside the product backlog and sprint reviews.

Why do Agile teams avoid Gantt charts?

Detailed, task-level Gantt charts imply fixed scope and dates, take effort to keep current and can encourage top-down scheduling. Those are problems with how the chart is used, which an epic-level, forecast-based chart avoids.

What do Agile teams use instead of Gantt charts?

Product backlogs, task boards, release burnup or burndown charts and roadmaps. Many teams use these alongside a light Agile Gantt chart rather than instead of it.

How do you show sprints on a Gantt chart?

Add each sprint as a fixed-length block along the top of the chart with its dates, then place epics as bars across the sprints in which they are expected to be worked on.

How detailed should an Agile Gantt chart be?

Epics or features, releases, external milestones and the dependencies between them. Leave stories, tasks, people and hours in the backlog and on the board.

How often should an Agile Gantt chart be updated?

After each sprint review, or sooner if a major forecast changes, such as an external dependency slipping. Daily updates are unnecessary because the chart works at release level.

Should an Agile Gantt chart show dependencies?

Yes, but only between epics, teams and external milestones. Avoid task-level dependency chains, which create maintenance work and false precision.

What is the difference between a Gantt chart and an Agile roadmap?

A Gantt chart is date-based and shows when work is forecast to happen. A roadmap focuses on goals and outcomes, often using broad horizons such as Now, Next and Later rather than dates.

Is there a free Agile Gantt chart template?

You can adapt our free Gantt chart template by using sprint columns instead of weeks and adding epics as rows. It is available for Google Sheets, Excel and PDF.

Which tools offer Gantt charts for Agile teams?

Many tools combine boards with timeline or Gantt views, including Wrike, Jira, monday.com, Asana, ClickUp and Smartsheet. Choose one that builds the timeline from the same backlog the team uses.
