Agile Release Planning: Steps, Example and Template

Agile release planning is the practice of forecasting which backlog items a team is likely to deliver in its next one or more releases, and roughly when, using the team's measured pace and updating that forecast every sprint. It answers the question every stakeholder asks, "When will we get it?", with an honest range instead of a single date fixed on day one. This guide explains what a release plan contains, how to build one step by step, and how the forecasting maths works, with two worked examples you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • A release plan groups ordered backlog items into upcoming releases and forecasts when each is likely to be ready, using a range of velocities rather than one number.
  • It sits between the product roadmap (strategy, months to years) and the sprint plan (detail, weeks).
  • Fix either the date or the scope, never both. Agile teams usually fix the date and let lower-priority scope flex.
  • Sort the backlog into "will have", "might have" and "won't have" for the release, so trade-offs are visible from the start.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide has no release planning event, but forecasting with burn-ups, burn-downs or cumulative flow is a common complementary practice.
  • A release plan is a living forecast: update it after every Sprint Review and show progress on a release burnup chart.

What is Agile release planning?

Agile release planning is a recurring activity in which the Product Owner, the team and key stakeholders decide which product backlog items should go into an upcoming release, estimate how much work that is, and use the team's measured velocity to forecast when the release is likely to be ready. The result is a release plan: a living forecast that is revised every sprint as real progress becomes known.

A release is the point at which a set of finished work is made available to users or customers. Some teams release several times a day; others bundle work into a release every few months because of app-store reviews, customer contracts, hardware, training or regulation. Release planning matters most in the second situation, where stakeholders need to know what is coming and roughly when.

Three ideas separate Agile release planning from a traditional fixed plan:

  • It is a forecast, not a promise. It is based on how fast the team has actually been working, and it states its uncertainty as a range.
  • It is ordered by value. The most important items are scheduled first, so if time runs out, what is left out is the least valuable work.
  • It is updated continuously. Every sprint produces new data (completed work, scope changes), and the plan is adjusted to match.

Where release planning fits: the levels of Agile planning

Agile teams plan constantly, but at different levels of detail and over different horizons. A common way to picture this is the "planning onion", with strategy on the outside and daily work at the centre. Release planning is the middle layer that turns a roadmap into something the team can deliver.

LevelTypical horizonQuestion it answersMain output
Vision and strategy1–3 yearsWhy does the product exist and where is it going?Product vision, Product Goal
Product roadmap3–12 monthsWhich outcomes and themes come in what order?Agile roadmap
Release plan1–6 monthsWhich backlog items are likely in each release, and when?Release plan and burnup chart
Sprint (iteration) plan1–4 weeksWhat will we deliver this sprint, and how?Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog
Daily plan24 hoursWhat will we do today to reach the Sprint Goal?Updated plan from the Daily Scrum

Each level is planned in more detail the closer it is in time. That is deliberate: detailed plans for work six months away tend to be wrong, so Agile teams keep the far future coarse and refine it as it approaches.

Release plan vs product roadmap vs sprint plan

These three documents are often confused. The table shows how they differ in purpose, content and audience.

Product roadmapRelease planSprint plan
PurposeCommunicate strategy and prioritiesForecast delivery of specific scopeOrganise the next few weeks of work
ContentGoals, themes, outcomesEpics and features, estimates, sprints, datesBacklog items, tasks, Sprint Goal
Unit of timeQuarters or Now / Next / LaterSprints and release datesDays within one sprint
OwnerProduct manager or Product OwnerProduct Owner with the teamThe Developers, with the Product Owner
AudienceLeadership, customers, whole organisationStakeholders, support, marketing, salesThe Scrum Team

A practical rule: the roadmap says why and in what order, the release plan says what and roughly when, and the sprint plan says exactly what next and how. If you need a roadmap first, start with the free product roadmap template.

Is release planning part of Scrum?

Not formally. The current Scrum Guide (the 2020 edition, still the official version by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland) defines five events: the Sprint, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. There is no release planning event.

Instead, the Scrum Guide gives the Product Backlog a long-term objective, the Product Goal, and says the Product Owner is accountable for maximising value. On forecasting, it says that "various practices exist to forecast progress, like burn-downs, burn-ups, or cumulative flows", and adds that these do not replace the importance of empiricism. It also allows Increments to be delivered to stakeholders before the end of a sprint, so a release does not have to wait for the Sprint Review.

In practice, many Scrum teams still do release planning as a complementary practice, because their organisations need delivery forecasts. They typically:

  • Use the Product Goal and roadmap as the release goal.
  • Use backlog refinement to size the items in the release.
  • Revisit the forecast during each Sprint Review, where stakeholders discuss progress towards the Product Goal.
  • Plan each sprint in Sprint Planning with the release forecast in mind.

Release planning has older roots in Extreme Programming (XP), whose "planning game" includes a release planning step, and it is formalised at scale in the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe), where teams plan together in PI Planning at the start of each Planning Interval.

What an Agile release plan includes

A good release plan fits on one page or one screen. It usually contains:

  • Release goal: one or two sentences on the outcome the release should achieve, for example "customers can manage their own billing without calling support".
  • Target date or target scope: whichever is fixed (see the next section).
  • Ordered list of features or epics: taken from the product backlog, with the most valuable first.
  • Estimates: in story points, T-shirt sizes or item counts.
  • Velocity range: the team's slow, average and fast pace, based on recent sprints.
  • Sprint allocation: which items are expected in which sprint, at least roughly.
  • Capacity adjustments: holidays, public holidays, training, new joiners, planned support work.
  • Dependencies: work needed from other teams, suppliers or platforms, with dates.
  • Risks and assumptions: what could change the forecast, and what you are assuming.
  • Definition of Done and release criteria: what "ready to release" means, including testing, documentation and approvals. See Definition of Done.
  • Will / might / won't split: which items are very likely, possible and unlikely to make the release.

Fixed date or fixed scope?

Every release is constrained by time, scope, cost (mostly the size of the team) and quality. In Agile, quality is held constant through the Definition of Done, and team size changes slowly, so the real choice is between fixing the date and fixing the scope.

ApproachWhat is fixedWhat flexesTypical exampleForecast question
Fixed dateRelease dateScope (lowest-priority items)Launch at a trade show, regulatory deadline, seasonal campaignHow much can we deliver by this date?
Fixed scopeA minimum feature setRelease dateReplacing a legacy system that cannot be switched off until everything worksWhen will this set of features be ready?
Fixed date and scopeBothQuality, team health (in practice)Contracts written without Agile in mindUsually unanswerable without cutting corners

Agile teams avoid fixing both at once. When both are fixed, the only things left to give are quality and people's evenings, and both create problems later. Our advice: prefer a fixed date with flexible scope where you can. It gives stakeholders certainty about the date and forces healthy prioritisation conversations early.

How to create an Agile release plan: 9 steps

The steps below work for a single team using Scrum or another sprint-based approach. They take a few hours the first time and much less once the backlog is in good shape.

  1. Agree the release goal. Start from the roadmap and Product Goal. Write a short statement of the outcome the release must achieve and how you will know it worked. Without a goal, release plans turn into a list of features nobody can prioritise.
  2. Decide what is fixed. Agree with stakeholders whether the date or the scope is fixed, and record why. If the date is fixed, write it down; if the scope is fixed, write down the minimum set of features.
  3. Select and order the backlog items. Pull the epics and stories that support the goal from the product backlog and order them by value, risk and dependencies. Use MoSCoW (must, should, could, won't) or a simple ranked list.
  4. Size the work. Ask the Developers to estimate the items, in story points or T-shirt sizes for anything far away. Split very large items so that each fits comfortably in one sprint. Estimate only as precisely as the decision needs.
  5. Measure velocity as a range. Take the team's completed points for the last six to eight sprints. Calculate a slow figure (for example, the average of the three worst sprints) and a fast figure (the average of the three best). Our velocity chart guide explains how to read the trend.
  6. Adjust for capacity. Count the sprints available before the date, then reduce for holidays, training, onboarding or planned support work. Our Agile capacity planning guide shows how.
  7. Forecast. Multiply the slow and fast velocities by the number of effective sprints to get a scope range (fixed date), or divide the remaining points by each velocity to get a range of sprints (fixed scope). Draw a line in the ordered backlog at each figure.
  8. Check dependencies and risks. Walk through the plan sprint by sprint with anyone the release depends on: other teams, suppliers, security review, app-store approval. Move items or add buffer where a dependency is uncertain.
  9. Agree, publish and update. Review the plan with stakeholders, confirm the will / might / won't split, and publish it where everyone can see it. Update it after every Sprint Review using actual velocity and scope changes.

Steps 1 to 8 usually happen in a release planning meeting; step 9 continues until the release ships.

Worked example: forecasting a fixed-date release

A team building a customer billing portal runs two-week sprints. The business wants a release in time for a sales event on Friday 22 January 2027. Sprint 1 starts on Monday 2 November 2026, so there are six sprints before the date.

Step A: find the velocity range

Completed story points in the last six sprints were 28, 34, 30, 25, 32 and 31. The average is 30, but one number hides the variation, so the team uses a range:

MeasureCalculationResult
Slow velocityAverage of the three lowest sprints: (25 + 28 + 30) ÷ 327.7 points
Average velocity(28 + 34 + 30 + 25 + 32 + 31) ÷ 630.0 points
Fast velocityAverage of the three highest sprints: (31 + 32 + 34) ÷ 332.3 points

Step B: adjust capacity

Two of the six sprints fall over the Christmas and New Year holidays, when about half the team is away for a week. The team counts each of those sprints as three-quarters of a normal sprint, so it has 5.5 effective sprints instead of six.

Step C: forecast the scope range

ScenarioCalculationPoints the team can expect to finish
Slow27.7 × 5.5About 152 points
Fast32.3 × 5.5About 178 points

Step D: draw the lines in the backlog

The ordered backlog for the release totals 250 points: 140 points of must-have items, 50 of should-have and 60 of could-have. Drawing lines at 152 and 178 points gives this picture:

GroupItems (in priority order)PointsCumulativeForecast
Will haveOnline invoices, card payments, payment history, email receipts140140Very likely: fits even at slow velocity (152)
Might haveDirect debit set-up, downloadable statements38178Possible: fits only if the team runs near its fast velocity
Won't have (this release)Multi-currency, accounting export, saved payment methods72250Unlikely: plan for the next release

The Product Owner now has a clear message for stakeholders: the must-have features will be ready for the sales event, two extra features might make it, and the rest should be planned for the following release. The 12-point gap between the must-haves (140) and the slow forecast (152) is a small buffer for surprises.

Step E: allocate to sprints

SprintDatesPlanned focusPlanned points
12–13 Nov 2026Online invoices30
216–27 Nov 2026Card payments (part 1)30
330 Nov–11 Dec 2026Card payments (part 2), payment history30
414–25 Dec 2026Email receipts, payment history (finish)23 (holiday)
528 Dec 2026–8 Jan 2027Hardening, Direct debit set-up (start)23 (holiday)
611–22 Jan 2027Downloadable statements, release preparation30

Planned points use average velocity with the holiday reduction; the forecast range above is what the team communicates. The allocation is a guide only: each Sprint Planning decides the real content of each sprint.

Worked example: forecasting a fixed-scope release

Now imagine the same team must replace a legacy billing system, and nothing can be switched off until all 250 points of scope are done. Work starts on Monday 1 March 2027, with no holidays in the period.

ScenarioCalculationSprints neededForecast finish
Fast250 ÷ 32.3 = 7.7, rounded up8 sprintsFriday 18 June 2027
Slow250 ÷ 27.7 = 9.0, rounded up to allow for the remainder10 sprintsFriday 16 July 2027

The honest forecast is between mid-June and mid-July 2027. Round up, because a release cannot ship part-way through the last item. If the business needs certainty sooner, it has three levers: reduce scope (move items to a later release), add capacity early (new people slow a team down at first), or accept the later date.

For larger or riskier releases, a Monte Carlo simulation of past sprint results can give probabilities such as "85% chance of finishing by 2 July" instead of a simple range.

Tip

No velocity history yet? Run one or two sprints before making a forecast, or ask the team to plan a sample sprint from the backlog and use that as a first, very rough, velocity. Say clearly that the forecast will tighten after three or four sprints.

Running a release planning meeting

Release planning is often done in a single workshop at the start of a release, then revisited briefly every sprint. For a single team and a release of three to six months, half a day to a day is usually enough.

Who attends

  • Product Owner (leads the meeting and owns the backlog order).
  • Developers (estimate, flag technical risks and dependencies).
  • Scrum Master or Agile delivery lead (facilitates and keeps it time-boxed).
  • Key stakeholders for the parts that affect them: customer support, marketing, sales, operations, security, and any team the release depends on.

Sample agenda

TimeItemOutput
20 minRelease goal, context and the roadmap it supportsAgreed release goal
15 minFixed date or fixed scope, and whyAgreed constraint
45 minWalk through the ordered backlog; clarify and split large itemsOrdered, understood list
60 minEstimate items that are not yet sizedSized backlog for the release
20 minVelocity range and capacity for the release periodEffective sprints and velocity range
30 minForecast; draw will / might / won't linesDraft release plan
30 minDependencies, risks and assumptionsRisk and dependency list with owners
20 minConfidence check and agreementAgreed plan, next review date

End with a quick confidence vote: everyone holds up one to five fingers for how confident they are in the plan. Any score of one or two is a signal to talk about the concern before leaving the room.

Tracking the release with a burnup chart

A release burnup chart plots two lines across sprints: the total scope of the release and the cumulative work completed. The release is done when the lines meet. Unlike a burndown, a burnup shows scope changes clearly, which is why many teams prefer it for releases.

After sprintTotal release scope (points)Completed (cumulative)What it tells you
125029On track for the slow forecast
225061Slightly ahead of average
326290Scope grew by 12 points: discuss what moves out
4262112Holiday sprint, as expected
5255135Seven points moved to the next release
6255166Must-haves done; part of the might-have group delivered

To forecast from a burnup, extend the completed line at your slow and fast velocities and see where each crosses the scope line. That gives an updated date range every sprint. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs covers burnups, burndowns and cumulative flow in more depth.

Communicating release forecasts to stakeholders

These habits help stakeholders trust the forecast:

  • Give ranges, not single dates. "Between mid-June and mid-July" is honest. "16 June" sounds like a promise.
  • Show the will / might / won't split. Stakeholders can see exactly what is at risk and argue about priority, not effort.
  • Share the burnup chart every sprint, with one sentence on what changed and why.
  • Explain the trade-offs when scope grows. Adding 20 points to a fixed-date release means 20 points move out, or the date moves.
  • Raise risks early. A dependency that slips is easier to handle six weeks before the release than six days before.

A sample update: "After sprint 3 we have completed 90 of 262 points. The must-have features remain on track for 22 January. Downloadable statements are now unlikely because 12 points of payment-provider changes were added; we suggest moving them to the March release." For more on keeping stakeholders aligned, see project stakeholder management.

Release planning with continuous delivery

Teams that practise continuous delivery can deploy any finished item to production at any time. For them, the technical act of releasing is cheap, so a big release plan matters less. Release planning still has a role, but it shifts:

  • Plan feature releases, not deployments. Code ships continuously, often behind feature toggles, and the release plan forecasts when a feature will be switched on for customers.
  • Forecast by throughput. Teams using Kanban often forecast with item counts and throughput instead of story points.
  • Coordinate the non-technical work. Marketing, training, documentation and support readiness still need dates.

Kanban teams can therefore do release planning too, using throughput and cycle time instead of sprints.

Release planning across multiple teams

When several teams contribute to one release, planning separately creates hidden dependencies and late surprises. Common approaches:

  • Joint release planning: all teams plan together, map dependencies on a shared board and agree the order of work. This is the core of SAFe PI Planning, held at the start of each Planning Interval, which SAFe describes as a timebox of 8 to 12 weeks.
  • Agile release trains: in SAFe, a long-lived team of Agile teams plans and delivers together on a shared cadence. See Agile release train.
  • Shared backlog or coordination meeting: Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS) keeps one backlog and one Product Owner for many teams, while a Scrum of Scrums brings team representatives together to manage dependencies.

Whatever the framework, the same rules apply: forecast with ranges, make dependencies visible and update the plan every cycle.

Common release planning mistakes

  • Treating the forecast as a commitment. The plan is the best estimate today. If leaders treat it as a contract, teams start padding estimates and the forecast loses value.
  • Using one velocity number. An average hides variation. Always forecast with a slow and a fast figure.
  • Fixing date and scope together. Something has to flex. If it is not scope, it will be quality.
  • Forgetting the non-development work. Testing, security review, documentation, training and app-store approval all take time.
  • Comparing velocity across teams. Story points are relative to each team, so one team's 30 is not another team's 30. See Scrum velocity.
  • Never updating the plan. A release plan that is not revised every sprint becomes fiction within a month.

Frequently asked questions

What is release planning in Agile?

Release planning in Agile is forecasting which product backlog items are likely to be delivered in upcoming releases, and roughly when, based on the team's measured velocity. The plan is updated every sprint as real progress and scope changes become known.

Who is responsible for release planning in Agile?

The Product Owner or product manager owns the release plan and the order of the backlog. The Developers provide estimates and velocity, the Scrum Master facilitates, and stakeholders contribute priorities, dependencies and constraints.

Is release planning part of Scrum?

Not formally. The 2020 Scrum Guide has no release planning event, but it mentions forecasting practices such as burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flow. Many Scrum teams do release planning as a complementary practice and review the forecast at each Sprint Review.

How far ahead should an Agile release plan go?

Usually one to three releases, or roughly three to six months. Beyond that, uncertainty is too high for item-level forecasts, and an outcome-based Agile roadmap is more useful.

How often should a release plan be updated?

After every sprint, ideally straight after the Sprint Review, using the latest velocity and any scope changes. A brief update takes 15 to 30 minutes once the plan exists.

What is the difference between release planning and sprint planning?

Release planning forecasts what will be delivered over several sprints and roughly when. Sprint Planning decides the Sprint Goal, the items for the next sprint and how the team will deliver them. Release planning is coarser and longer-range; sprint planning is detailed and short-range.

What is the difference between a release plan and a product roadmap?

A product roadmap shows strategic direction: goals, themes and outcomes over months or quarters. A release plan shows delivery detail: which features or stories are expected in which release and sprint, based on estimates and velocity.

How do you forecast a release date in Agile?

Divide the remaining story points by the team's slow and fast velocities to get a range of sprints, round up, and convert to dates. For example, 250 points at 27.7 to 32.3 points per sprint needs 8 to 10 sprints.

What happens in a release planning meeting?

The team and Product Owner agree the release goal and whether date or scope is fixed, review and estimate the ordered backlog, calculate capacity and velocity range, draw will / might / won't lines, and agree dependencies and risks.

How long is a release in Agile?

There is no fixed length. Some teams release several times a day, while others release every few weeks or every few months. A release often spans three to six sprints when releases are planned in batches.

What is a release burnup chart?

A release burnup chart shows the total scope of a release and the cumulative work completed, sprint by sprint. Where the two lines meet is the forecast finish, and changes in the scope line show scope growth clearly.

Is release planning the same as PI planning?

No. PI Planning is the SAFe event in which all teams on an Agile release train plan the next Planning Interval together. It is a form of multi-team release planning with specific roles, agenda and outputs. See PI Planning.

Can you do release planning with Kanban?

Yes. Kanban teams forecast with throughput (items finished per week) and cycle time instead of story points and sprints, often using Monte Carlo simulation to produce probability-based dates.

What if the team has no velocity history yet?

Run one to three sprints before forecasting, or ask the team to plan a sample sprint and use that as a provisional velocity. Present early forecasts as wide ranges and narrow them as real data arrives.
