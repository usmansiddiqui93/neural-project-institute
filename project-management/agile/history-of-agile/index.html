History of Agile: When Did It Start? Full Timeline

The history of Agile has two starting points: the word "Agile" was adopted in February 2001, when seventeen software practitioners wrote the Manifesto for Agile Software Development at Snowbird, Utah, but the ideas behind it go back to iterative engineering projects of the 1950s and Toyota's production system. This guide tells the story in order, from those early roots through the lightweight methods of the 1990s, the Manifesto itself, and the frameworks, scaling models and standards that followed, with a full timeline you can use for study or training.

Key takeaways

  • The term Agile dates from the Snowbird meeting of 11 to 13 February 2001, but iterative and incremental development was in use on engineering programmes from the late 1950s.
  • Winston Royce's 1970 paper is often blamed for Waterfall, yet it warned that a purely sequential process was risky and recommended iteration and feedback.
  • Lean manufacturing and Takeuchi and Nonaka's 1986 "rugby" article shaped Agile's focus on flow, waste and self-organising teams.
  • Scrum (presented 1995), DSDM (1994), XP (late 1990s), Crystal, FDD and Adaptive Software Development all existed before the Manifesto.
  • After 2001, Agile spread through training and certification, then scaled to large organisations with SAFe, LeSS, Disciplined Agile and others.
  • Today most organisations mix Agile, Lean and predictive approaches, and standards such as the PMBOK Guide now cover all of them.

When did Agile start? The short answer

Agile started, as a named movement, in February 2001, when seventeen practitioners met at Snowbird, Utah, and wrote the Manifesto for Agile Software Development. The ideas it describes are older: iterative and incremental development dates from the late 1950s, Lean thinking from post-war Toyota, and frameworks such as Scrum and XP from the 1990s.

Both dates matter. The Manifesto gave a shared name and a set of values to methods that had been developing separately for years. Knowing the longer history helps you understand why Agile works the way it does, and why it is not tied to any one framework or tool. For the content of the Manifesto itself, see our guide to the Agile Manifesto values and principles.

Before Agile: iterative and incremental development (1950s–1980s)

Agile is often presented as a rebellion against Waterfall, but iterative development is in fact older than the Waterfall label. In their 2003 article "Iterative and Incremental Development: A Brief History", Craig Larman and Victor Basili traced incremental development back to IBM's Service Bureau Corporation in 1957, and to NASA's Project Mercury in the early 1960s, which used short, time-boxed iterations.

One of the clearest early examples is the Space Shuttle's primary avionics software. Between 1977 and 1980, engineers at IBM's Federal Systems Division, several of whom had worked on Mercury, built it in 17 iterations over 31 months, averaging about eight weeks each. They chose iteration because the shuttle's requirements kept changing. Our guide to iterative and incremental development explains the two ideas in more depth.

What Winston Royce actually said

In 1970 Winston Royce published "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems". Its early diagram shows requirements, analysis, design, coding, testing and operations flowing one after another, and this sequence later became known as the Waterfall model. The word "waterfall" does not appear in the paper.

What is often forgotten is that Royce described the purely sequential version as risky and inviting failure, because problems are discovered only at testing, when they are most expensive to fix. He went on to recommend feedback between stages, building an early version or prototype, and involving the customer. In other words, the paper usually cited as the origin of Waterfall argued for some of the ideas Agile later championed. Even so, sequential life cycles became the norm in many government and corporate software contracts through the 1970s and 1980s. For how the two approaches compare today, see Agile vs Waterfall and our guide to Waterfall project management.

In 1986 Barry Boehm described the spiral model: repeated cycles of planning, risk analysis, building and evaluation, each tackling the biggest remaining risk. Its central idea runs straight through modern Agile practice.

Lean manufacturing and the Toyota Production System

The second root of Agile is manufacturing. From the late 1940s onwards, Taiichi Ohno and colleagues at Toyota developed what became the Toyota Production System. Its core ideas will be familiar to any Agile team:

  • Eliminate waste: anything that does not add value for the customer, including waiting, rework and unfinished inventory.
  • Just-in-time and pull: make only what the next step needs, when it needs it, signalled by kanban cards. See just-in-time (JIT).
  • Stop and fix: any worker can stop the line to fix a problem at its source, rather than passing defects on.
  • Continuous improvement (kaizen): small, frequent improvements made by the people doing the work.
  • Respect for people: front-line teams are trusted to solve problems.

The word "lean" itself was coined by researcher John Krafcik in 1988 and popularised by the 1990 book *The Machine That Changed the World*. In 2003, Mary and Tom Poppendieck's *Lean Software Development* translated these principles explicitly for software, and Lean later shaped the Kanban method.

The rugby approach: Takeuchi and Nonaka, 1986

In January 1986 Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka published "The New New Product Development Game" in the Harvard Business Review. Studying companies such as Honda, Canon and Fuji-Xerox, they contrasted a sequential "relay race" approach to product development with a "rugby" approach in which a small, cross-functional, self-organising team works through overlapping phases together. They used the word scrum to describe it, and Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber later named their framework after it.

The software crisis and heavyweight processes

By the early 1990s, many software organisations followed detailed, document-driven processes designed to bring discipline to large projects. Practitioners increasingly found serious drawbacks:

  • Long cycles. It could take years from requirements to delivery, by which time the business need had changed.
  • Late discovery of problems. Integration and testing happened at the end, when fixing mistakes was slow and expensive.
  • Change was treated as failure. Formal change control made it hard to respond to what users learned once they saw the product.
  • Documents stood in for progress. Plans and specifications could be complete while no working software existed.
  • Separated roles. Analysts, designers, developers and testers handed work over rather than collaborating.

The alternatives that appeared in response were, at first, simply called lightweight methods.

Scrum

Jeff Sutherland first used Scrum at Easel Corporation in 1993, drawing on Takeuchi and Nonaka's article. He and Ken Schwaber presented it formally at the OOPSLA conference in 1995. The earliest Scrum writings already described sprints, and Schwaber described the daily scrum in the late 1990s. Scrum therefore predates the Agile Manifesto by several years.

Extreme Programming

Extreme Programming grew out of Kent Beck's work on a payroll project at Chrysler from 1996. The first public article about it appeared in 1998, and Beck's book *Extreme Programming Explained* followed in 1999. XP contributed many of the engineering and planning practices Agile teams still use, including user stories, short iterations, test-first development, continuous integration and, around 2000, the idea of velocity.

DSDM

DSDM was created in 1994 by a consortium of organisations in the UK that wanted a common, vendor-independent framework for rapid application development. Its principle of fixing time and cost while letting scope flex, and its MoSCoW prioritisation, remain widely used. The consortium rebranded as the Agile Business Consortium in 2016.

2001: the Snowbird meeting and the Agile Manifesto

The path to the Manifesto began in spring 2000, when Kent Beck invited leaders of the XP community, and some people from other lightweight methods, to a retreat at the Rogue River Lodge in Oregon. They discussed the common ground between their approaches but made no formal decisions.

In September 2000 Robert C. ("Uncle Bob") Martin of Object Mentor proposed a follow-up meeting. After some debate between Chicago, Anguilla and Snowbird, the group chose The Lodge at Snowbird ski resort in Utah. The meeting took place from 11 to 13 February 2001.

The seventeen authors

The people who met at Snowbird represented most of the lightweight methods of the time. All seventeen signed the Manifesto.

Why "Agile"?

Until then the methods had been called lightweight, a label many of the group disliked because it sounded insubstantial. According to Martin Fowler's account of the meeting, they considered several names and settled on "agile" because it captured the adaptiveness and response to change at the heart of their approach.

What they agreed

The participants disagreed about many details of method, but agreed on four values, each preferring one thing over another while still valuing the second:

  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation.
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.
  • Responding to change over following a plan.

The twelve principles behind the Manifesto were refined in the weeks after the meeting. They cover early and continuous delivery, welcoming change, daily collaboration, motivated teams, face-to-face conversation, working software as the measure of progress, sustainable pace, technical excellence, simplicity, self-organising teams and regular reflection.

Later in 2001, several of the authors formed the Agile Alliance, a non-profit organisation to support the community, which still runs an annual conference.

2002–2010: Agile spreads

In the decade after Snowbird, Agile moved from a niche of enthusiasts to common practice in software development.

  • Training and certification. Scrum spread quickly through two-day courses and certifications. Ken Schwaber later founded Scrum.org in 2009. Scrum became the most widely used Agile framework.
  • Books turned ideas into techniques. Mike Cohn's *User Stories Applied* (2004) and *Agile Estimating and Planning* (2005) gave teams practical methods for planning and estimating.
  • Leadership and project management. In 2005 a group including Jim Highsmith and Alistair Cockburn published the Declaration of Interdependence, applying Agile values to project and team leadership.
  • Lean and Kanban. The Poppendiecks linked Lean to software in 2003. From 2007, teams reported using kanban systems with work-in-progress limits instead of iterations, and David J. Anderson's book on the Kanban method followed in 2010.
  • DevOps. The first DevOpsDays conference in Ghent in 2009 gave a name to extending Agile collaboration to deployment and operations. See our guide to DevOps.
  • The Scrum Guide. In 2010 Schwaber and Sutherland published the first official Scrum Guide, giving Scrum a single, freely available definition.

As large organisations adopted Agile, they faced a new question: how do dozens or hundreds of teams work together on the same product? Several scaling approaches emerged.

Agile beyond software

From the 2010s, teams outside software began adopting Agile practices. Marketing teams used backlogs and short campaigns, HR and finance teams used boards and retrospectives, and public-sector organisations such as government digital services adopted Agile delivery for online services. Construction, manufacturing and research projects increasingly used hybrid approaches: predictive plans for physical work and fixed milestones, with Agile cycles for design and stakeholder feedback.

The Scrum Guide's own wording reflects this. Since 2017 it has described Scrum's use beyond software, and the 2020 edition removed much of the remaining software-specific language.

Criticism and backlash

Agile's success brought criticism, much of it from the Manifesto's own authors. Understanding it helps you avoid the same traps.

  • "Agile is dead (long live agility)". In 2014 Dave Thomas argued that the word Agile had been turned into a marketing label, and encouraged people to focus on being agile rather than buying Agile.
  • The "Agile industrial complex". In a 2018 talk, Martin Fowler criticised Agile imposed on teams by management and consultants, contrary to the Manifesto's emphasis on teams choosing their own process.
  • Process over people. Some organisations adopted ceremonies and tools without changing how decisions are made, which is the opposite of the first Manifesto value.
  • Misuse of metrics. Velocity and story points, intended as team planning aids, were sometimes turned into performance targets.

None of these critics argued for returning to heavyweight processes. Their message was to return to the values: working results, collaboration, adaptation and respect for the people doing the work.

Agile in the 2020s

Agile is now a mainstream part of project delivery rather than a movement in opposition to it. Several trends define the current period:

  • Agile and predictive as options. The PMBOK Guide 7th edition (2021) replaced its process-based structure with principles and performance domains covering predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches. The 8th edition, published in November 2025, continues this tailored view. See our guide to the PMBOK Guide.
  • Hybrid delivery. Many organisations use a predictive milestone plan for governance with Agile delivery inside each phase.
  • Business agility. Attention has shifted from individual teams to whole organisations: funding, portfolio management and leadership.
  • Outcomes and flow. Teams increasingly pair delivery metrics with measures of customer outcomes and flow metrics such as cycle time.
  • AI-assisted work. Teams are using AI tools to help draft stories, summarise feedback and analyse delivery data, while the Agile emphasis on human collaboration and judgement remains.

For a closer look at where things may go next, read is Agile the future of project management?.

What the history of Agile teaches you today

The history is more than trivia. It explains why Agile works as it does and how to apply it well.

  1. Agile is a set of values, not a single method. The Manifesto was written by people from competing methods. Choose the framework that suits your context; our guide to types of Agile methodologies compares them.
  2. Iteration was always about managing uncertainty. From Project Mercury to the Space Shuttle, teams iterated because requirements changed. Use Agile where uncertainty is high and consider predictive or hybrid approaches where it is low.
  3. Engineering discipline matters. XP's practices were part of Agile from the start. Process changes without technical quality rarely last.
  4. Lean thinking complements Agile. Limiting work in progress and removing waste often improves delivery more than adding meetings.
  5. Metrics are aids, not targets. Velocity and burndown were invented to help teams plan, and history shows what happens when they are turned into performance measures.
  6. Adoption should be invited, not imposed. The strongest criticism of modern Agile is about process forced on teams. Involve the team in shaping how it works.

Frequently asked questions

When did Agile start?

The term Agile was adopted at the Snowbird meeting on 11 to 13 February 2001, when the Agile Manifesto was written. The ideas behind it are older: iterative development dates from the late 1950s, and Scrum, DSDM and XP were in use during the 1990s.

Who invented Agile?

No single person. Agile was defined by the seventeen authors of the Agile Manifesto, building on earlier work in iterative development, Lean manufacturing and the lightweight software methods of the 1990s.

Who wrote the Agile Manifesto?

Kent Beck, Mike Beedle, Arie van Bennekum, Alistair Cockburn, Ward Cunningham, Martin Fowler, James Grenning, Jim Highsmith, Andrew Hunt, Ron Jeffries, Jon Kern, Brian Marick, Robert C. Martin, Steve Mellor, Ken Schwaber, Jeff Sutherland and Dave Thomas.

Where was the Agile Manifesto signed?

At The Lodge at Snowbird ski resort in the Wasatch mountains of Utah, USA, in February 2001.

Why was Agile created?

To offer an alternative to heavyweight, document-driven software processes that were slow, handled change poorly and discovered problems late. The authors wanted to deliver working software frequently, collaborate closely with customers and adapt to change.

What came before Agile?

Iterative and incremental development on engineering programmes, Lean manufacturing from Toyota, Rapid Application Development, and 1990s lightweight methods such as Scrum, DSDM, Crystal, XP and Feature-Driven Development.

Is Scrum older than Agile?

Yes. Jeff Sutherland first used Scrum in 1993 and presented it with Ken Schwaber in 1995, six years before the Agile Manifesto. Schwaber, Sutherland and Mike Beedle were among the Manifesto's authors. See what is Scrum.

Did Winston Royce invent the Waterfall model?

Not in the sense usually claimed. His 1970 paper described a sequential process that later became known as Waterfall, but it warned that a purely sequential approach was risky and recommended iteration, prototyping and customer involvement. The word waterfall does not appear in it.

How did Lean influence Agile?

Lean thinking from the Toyota Production System contributed ideas such as eliminating waste, limiting work in progress, pulling work when needed and continuous improvement. Kanban boards come directly from this tradition.

Why is it called Agile?

The Snowbird participants wanted to replace the label "lightweight methods", which many disliked. They chose "agile" because it described the adaptiveness and response to change at the heart of their approaches.

What is the Agile Alliance?

A non-profit organisation formed by several Manifesto authors in late 2001 to support the Agile community. It runs an annual conference and publishes resources, including a timeline of Agile practices.

When did Agile spread beyond software?

Mainly from the 2010s, when marketing, HR, operations and public-sector teams began adopting Agile practices, and frameworks such as SAFe and PRINCE2 Agile brought it into large organisations.

Is Agile dead?

No. Critics such as Dave Thomas argued in 2014 that the word had become a marketing label, but their point was to focus on agility rather than branded processes. Agile practices remain widely used and are now part of mainstream standards such as the PMBOK Guide.
