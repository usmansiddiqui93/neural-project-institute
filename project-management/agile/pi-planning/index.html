PI Planning in SAFe: Agenda, Steps and Example

PI planning is the regular, usually two-day event in the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) where every team on an Agile Release Train plans the next Planning Interval together, face to face or online. Over the two days the teams turn the highest-priority features into iteration plans, agree dependencies and risks, and commit to PI objectives that Business Owners have valued. This guide explains the agenda, the roles, how to prepare and what each output looks like, with a worked example of one team's plan.

Key takeaways

  • PI planning is a cadence-based event, typically two days every 8 to 12 weeks, in which the whole Agile Release Train (ART) plans the next Planning Interval together.
  • Since SAFe 6.0 (2023), PI stands for Planning Interval; you will still see the older name, Program Increment, in many organisations and tools.
  • The main outputs are committed and uncommitted PI objectives with business value, an ART planning board showing features and dependencies, ROAMed risks and a confidence vote.
  • Most of the success of the event is decided beforehand: a ready ART backlog, known capacity, briefed Business Owners and good logistics.
  • Plan teams below full capacity and keep uncommitted objectives as a buffer, so the plan survives normal surprises.
  • PI planning is only the start: the plan is tracked through the PI and scored at Inspect and Adapt using the predictability measure.

What is PI planning?

PI planning is a cadence-based planning event in the Scaled Agile Framework in which all the teams and stakeholders of an Agile Release Train meet, usually for two days, to agree what they will deliver in the next Planning Interval. Teams create their own iteration plans and PI objectives, expose cross-team dependencies and risks, and finish with a confidence vote.

Scaled Agile describes PI planning as "a cadence-based event for the entire ART that aligns teams and stakeholders to a shared mission and vision", typically held over two days every 8 to 12 weeks and facilitated by the Release Train Engineer.

What does PI stand for?

In SAFe 6.0, released in 2023, Scaled Agile renamed the Program Increment to the Planning Interval. The abbreviation PI did not change, so the event is still called PI planning. The new name emphasises the planning cadence rather than a fixed output, and it fits trains that are not organised as formal programmes. You will meet both terms, and older material (and many tools) still says Program Increment.

How long is a Planning Interval?

A PI is a fixed timebox, typically 8 to 12 weeks. With two-week iterations, a common pattern is four development iterations followed by one Innovation and Planning (IP) iteration. The IP iteration gives room for innovation, learning, final integration and the Inspect and Adapt event, and it is when the next PI planning normally takes place, so planning does not interrupt delivery.

PI planning is the scaled equivalent of sprint planning: instead of one team planning one sprint, five to twelve teams plan several iterations together. To understand the team-of-teams structure that takes part, see our guide to the Agile Release Train.

SAFe has its own vocabulary, and several terms changed in version 6.0. The table covers the words you will hear during the event.

TermMeaning

Planning Interval (PI)A fixed timebox of usually 8 to 12 weeks; formerly called Program Increment
IP iterationThe final Innovation and Planning iteration of each PI, used for innovation, Inspect and Adapt and the next PI planning

Business OwnersSenior stakeholders accountable for the business outcomes of the train; they assign business value to objectives
Product ManagementOwns the ART backlog and presents the vision and the top features
System ArchitectPresents the architectural vision and enablers; helps teams with technical dependencies
PI objectivesA summary, in business terms, of what each team (and the ART) intends to achieve in the PI

Why PI planning matters

Bringing 100 people together for two days is expensive. Scaled Agile's stated benefits include face-to-face communication, aligning development to business goals, identifying dependencies, matching demand to capacity and fast decisions. In practice, the value comes from four things:

  • Alignment. Every team hears the same business context and vision at the same time, from the people accountable for it.
  • Dependencies surface early. A dependency found on day one of planning costs a conversation; the same dependency found in week seven of delivery can cost a feature.
  • Realistic commitments. Teams plan against their own measured capacity and negotiate scope in the room, rather than receiving a plan from above.
  • Speed of decision. Business Owners, architects and Product Management are present, so trade-offs that would normally take weeks of email are settled within hours.

Who attends PI planning

SAFe's guidance is that everyone on the ART attends, along with the key stakeholders who shape or depend on its work. The table shows the main roles and what each does during the event.

RoleWhat they do during PI planning
Release Train Engineer (RTE)Prepares and facilitates the event, keeps time, runs the draft and final plan reviews, the risk review and the confidence vote
Business OwnersPresent business context, take part in management review, assign business value to team objectives, help ROAM risks
Product ManagementPresent the vision and the top features of the ART backlog; negotiate scope with teams
System ArchitectPresent the architectural vision, enablers and development practices; advise teams on technical dependencies
Product OwnersClarify features and stories for their team, help set priorities in breakouts, draft team objectives
Scrum Masters / Team CoachesFacilitate their team's breakout, track capacity, load and dependencies, keep the team to time
Agile team membersPlan iterations, identify risks and dependencies, vote
Shared services and other trainsAgree hand-offs with the teams that depend on them

PI planning vs sprint planning vs release planning

PI planning is often confused with other planning events. The main difference is scale: PI planning coordinates many teams over several iterations, while sprint planning is one team planning one sprint.

FrameworkSAFeScrum (and SAFe iterations)Not a defined event in Scrum; common Agile practice
Who plansAll teams on the ART plus stakeholdersOne Scrum teamProduct Owner, team and stakeholders
HorizonOne PI (8–12 weeks)One sprint (up to one month)One or more releases
TimeboxTypically two daysUp to 8 hours for a one-month sprintVaries
Main outputsPI objectives, ART planning board, ROAMed risks, confidence voteSprint Goal and Sprint BacklogRelease plan and forecast
FacilitatorRelease Train EngineerUsually the Scrum MasterUsually the Product Owner

If your organisation does not use SAFe but still needs to coordinate a few teams, a lighter approach is often enough.

How to prepare for PI planning

Experienced RTEs agree that the event is won or lost in preparation. SAFe groups readiness into three dimensions:

  • Organisational readiness: the scope of the train is clear, Business Owners and Product Management agree on priorities, and the right people are available and invited.
  • Content readiness: the business context is drafted, the vision and near-term roadmap are ready, the top features of the ART backlog (often derived from portfolio epics) are defined with acceptance criteria and rough sizes, architectural enablers are known and each team's capacity is calculated.
  • Logistics readiness: the venue or online space, breakout rooms, boards, video links, tooling, food and agenda are ready, and everyone knows how the event will run.

A suggested preparation timeline

SAFe does not prescribe exact lead times. The timeline below is our own suggestion for a train that already has a few PIs behind it; allow more time for your first event.

WhenActivityOwner
6–8 weeks beforeConfirm dates and venue or online tools; send invitations; agree the PI scopeRTE
4–6 weeks beforePrioritise the top ten or so features (many trains use Weighted Shortest Job First); draft benefit hypotheses and acceptance criteriaProduct Management, Business Owners
3–4 weeks beforeSocialise features with Product Owners and teams; flag known dependencies and enablersProduct Management, System Architect
2–3 weeks beforeReadiness check: features refined, capacity known, presenters briefed; fix any gapsRTE
1–2 weeks beforeTeams calculate capacity per iteration; Product Owners pre-split features into candidate storiesScrum Masters, Product Owners
Final weekDry run of tools and agenda; print or set up boards; confirm working agreementsRTE, Scrum Masters
Tip

Pre-PI work is about reducing discovery, not pre-making the plan. If teams arrive with a plan already filled in for them, the breakouts become theatre and the confidence vote loses its meaning.

The two-day PI planning agenda

SAFe publishes a standard agenda that most trains follow, adjusting times to suit. The table shows a typical version for a co-located train; online events are usually spread over more days.

TimeDay 1Day 2
08:00Business context (Business Owner or senior leader)Planning adjustments (RTE presents changes agreed overnight)
09:00Product and solution vision (Product Management)Team breakout 2
10:30Architecture vision and development practices (System Architect)Team breakout 2 continues; Business Owners circulate to assign business value
11:30Planning context and lunch (RTE explains the process)Final plan review and lunch
13:00Team breakout 1ART risks reviewed and ROAMed

17:00Management review and problem-solvingPlan rework if necessary, then planning retrospective

Day 1: context, breakouts and draft plans

The morning gives everyone the same picture: why the work matters, what is wanted, how it should be built, and how the next day and a half will run.

In the afternoon, team breakout 1 is where the real work starts. Teams turn features into iteration plans and draft objectives (see the next section), while Product Management, architects and Business Owners move between teams to answer questions.

At the draft plan review, each team presents its draft capacity and load, objectives, risks and dependencies in a few minutes. This is where conflicts become visible: two teams relying on a third in the same iteration, a feature that no longer fits, or a risk that needs a decision.

Day 1 ends with management review and problem-solving. Business Owners, Product Management, the RTE and architects decide on adjustments to scope, priority, people or dates. It often runs into the evening.

Day 2: adjustments, commitment and close

Day 2 opens with planning adjustments: the RTE or Product Management explains what management decided overnight. In team breakout 2, teams finalise their plans and objectives, and Business Owners visit each team to assign business value to its objectives.

In the final plan review, each team presents its plan and objectives and asks the Business Owners to accept them. The train then reviews the ART-level risks and handles each one using ROAM. Next comes the confidence vote. If confidence is low, teams rework their plans until it is acceptable. A short planning retrospective closes the event.

What happens in a team breakout

Breakouts are where most of the time is spent. A good Scrum Master or Team Coach keeps the team moving through a predictable sequence:

  1. Confirm capacity. Write each iteration's capacity on the team board, taking holidays, leave, training and support duties into account.
  2. Review the features assigned to the team. Ask Product Management and the Product Owner any questions while they are in the room.
  3. Split features into stories small enough to finish within an iteration. See our guide to user stories.
  4. Estimate the stories, usually in story points, using the team's normal technique.
  5. Load the iterations. Place stories in iterations without exceeding capacity; leave headroom for unplanned work and defects.
  6. Identify dependencies on other teams, suppliers or shared services, agree them in person and record them on the ART planning board.
  7. Draft PI objectives in business language, separating committed and uncommitted objectives.
  8. List risks that could stop the team meeting its objectives, ready for the ART risk review.
  9. Prepare the summary for the draft or final plan review: capacity and load, objectives, risks and dependencies.
Tip

Do not try to plan the IP iteration with feature work. It is there for innovation, learning, integration and planning, and it acts as an estimating buffer. If teams fill it with features, the PI has no slack left.

Worked example: one team's PI plan

The example follows a fictional team, Payments, on a train with a 10-week PI: four two-week development iterations (I1 to I4) plus an IP iteration. Payments has five developers and testers, a Product Owner and a Scrum Master.

Step 1: calculate capacity

Payments is a new team with no stable velocity yet, so it uses SAFe's normalised estimating guidance as a starting point: about 8 story points per full-time developer or tester per two-week iteration, minus 1 point for each person-day of holiday or leave. Step 3: write and value the objectives

The Product Owner drafts objectives with the team, and on day 2 the Business Owners assign business value on SAFe's 1 to 10 scale.

PI objectiveTypePlanned business value
Customers can save a card and pay with one click at checkoutCommitted9

The Refund API objective is a dependency for another team, so it appears on the ART planning board as a red string from Payments' I3 column to the Orders team's I4 column. The team also lists two risks: a third-party payment provider has not confirmed its sandbox dates, and one developer may be moved to an urgent project.

Payments can now summarise its plan in three minutes at the draft plan review.

Writing good PI objectives

PI objectives summarise, in business language, what a team intends to achieve. They describe outcomes someone outside the team can recognise, not a list of features or stories.

Committed vs uncommitted objectives

  • Committed objectives are those the team is confident it can deliver within the PI. Business Owners and other teams plan around them.
  • Uncommitted objectives are planned work that the team is less sure about, usually because of a high unknown or a dependency. They are included in capacity, not a wish list, and give the plan a buffer: if something goes wrong, they are dropped first. They still receive business value, which counts if they are delivered.

Business value

Business Owners assign each objective a business value from 1 (lowest) to 10 (highest). The conversation matters as much as the number, because it tells the team what the business cares about most.

Tip

Write each objective so a Business Owner can say "yes, that was done" or "no, it was not" at the end of the PI. Keep the list short (three to five committed objectives per team is typical) and include enabler and improvement work so its value is visible.

The ART planning board

The ART planning board (called the program board before SAFe 6.0) is the train's shared picture of the plan. It is usually a large wall or digital whiteboard with iterations as columns and teams as rows.

  • Features are placed in the iteration in which the team expects to complete them.
  • Milestones and events (a trade show, a regulatory deadline, a release, a hardware delivery) are placed in a row at the top.
  • Dependencies are drawn as lines, traditionally red string, between the card that needs something and the card that provides it.
  • Other trains, suppliers and shared services get their own rows so their commitments are visible too.

Warning signs include many strings converging on one team in one iteration, a dependency needed before it is delivered, or features clustered in the last iteration. For more on dependency types, see our guide to task dependencies.

Managing risks with ROAM

During the final plan review, the train collects the risks teams have raised that could affect the PI objectives. Each risk is discussed in front of the whole train and placed in one of four categories:

ROAM categoryMeaningExample
ResolvedThe risk has been dealt with and is no longer a concernBusiness Owners confirm the developer will not be moved to the other project
OwnedSomeone takes responsibility for the risk because it cannot be resolved in the roomThe RTE will chase the payment provider for sandbox dates by next Friday
AcceptedThe risk is understood and accepted because nothing practical can be doneA regulator may change a rule mid-PI; the train will adapt if it happens
MitigatedA plan reduces the likelihood or impact of the riskPayments builds a stub so work continues if the sandbox is late

ROAMing risks in public prevents risks being quietly ignored and makes ownership explicit. Owned and mitigated risks should be tracked through the PI, for example in a RAID log or risk register.

The confidence vote

The confidence vote closes the planning part of the event. Everyone votes at the same time by holding up one to five fingers ("fist of five"), first for their team's objectives and then for the ART's plan as a whole.

In common practice, an average of three or more allows the train to commit. Anyone who votes one or two is invited to explain their concern, because they often know something the rest of the room does not. If confidence is low, teams rework their plans, sometimes with help from management, and vote again.

Tip

Protect the honesty of the vote. If leaders react badly to low scores, people will learn to hold up three fingers whatever they think, and the vote stops telling you anything. Simultaneous voting, and anonymous digital voting for online events, help.

After PI planning: execution and the predictability measure

After the event, teams enter their stories into their tools and keep the plan alive through iteration planning, SAFe's coach sync and PO sync meetings, system demos and updates to the ART planning board.

At the end of the PI, the Inspect and Adapt event has three parts: a PI system demo, a quantitative and qualitative measurement review, and a retrospective and problem-solving workshop. During the measurement part, Business Owners score the actual business value each team achieved for every objective, committed and uncommitted, on the same 1 to 10 scale.

Calculating predictability

The team's predictability is actual business value achieved divided by planned business value, where planned value counts only committed objectives. Continuing the Payments example:

ObjectiveTypePlanned BVActual BV
One-click card paymentsCommitted98
Refund API for Orders teamCommitted77
Page loads under two secondsCommitted53
Pay-later prototypeUncommitted(6, not counted)4
Audit evidence automationUncommitted(4, not counted)0
Total2122

Predictability = 22 ÷ 21 = about 105%. Delivering part of an uncommitted objective pushed the team above 100% despite falling short on one committed objective. SAFe guidance treats a range of roughly 80 to 100% as predictable. Far below that suggests over-commitment; consistently far above suggests the team is sandbagging or under-using uncommitted objectives. Use the trend over several PIs to improve planning, not to rank teams. For other measures that help, see our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs.

Remote and distributed PI planning

Many trains now plan online or in a hybrid format. Scaled Agile's guidance for distributed PI planning recommends splitting the agenda across overlapping working hours, often over two and a half to three or more days, rather than asking any location to work through the night.

  • Shorten each day and spread the agenda over more days so every time zone has reasonable working hours.
  • Schedule synchronisation points when teams overlap, for the draft plan review, dependency negotiation and business value scoring (short, pre-booked slots with Business Owners work well).
  • Use one shared digital ART planning board and keep team spaces open for anyone to view.
  • Agree working agreements upfront: cameras on in breakouts, one conversation at a time, microphones for every speaker, time-boxes respected.
  • Vote simultaneously using a digital poll, so no group sees others' votes first.

Common PI planning mistakes

  • Arriving unprepared. Features that are not defined or sized turn breakouts into requirement workshops.
  • Planning to 100% of capacity. Any surprise breaks the plan. Leave room, and use uncommitted objectives.
  • Filling the IP iteration with features. This removes the PI's only buffer and the time for innovation and planning.
  • Handing teams a pre-made plan. If the plan is decided before the event, commitment and the confidence vote become meaningless.
  • Hidden dependencies. Make every dependency visible on the board and agreed by both teams.
  • Business Owners who are not present. Without them, scope decisions wait and business value scores are guesses.
  • Punishing low confidence votes. People stop telling the truth, and problems surface weeks later.
  • Skipping the retrospective. Capture improvements for next time while they are fresh.

PI planning tools and templates

A physical wall, sticky notes and red string still work well for a co-located train. Distributed trains usually use a digital whiteboard for the ART planning board and their work management tool for stories and dependencies.

  • Team planning: our sprint planning template works for each team's iteration capacity and load.
  • Release view: the release plan template helps map features to iterations and forecast dates.
  • Roadmap input: use the product roadmap template to prepare the vision and near-term roadmap.
  • Risks and dependencies: track ROAMed risks and dependencies in the RAID log template.
  • Software: Jira is widely used by software trains for story-level planning, and Wrike is well suited to cross-team and portfolio visibility when your trains include non-software work. Compare more options in our project management tools rankings.

Frequently asked questions

What does PI stand for in PI planning?

PI stands for Planning Interval. Before SAFe 6.0 (2023) it stood for Program Increment, and many organisations and tools still use the older name. Both refer to the same fixed timebox, usually 8 to 12 weeks.

How long is PI planning?

Typically two days for a co-located train. Distributed trains often spread the agenda over two and a half to three or more shorter days so every time zone works reasonable hours.

How often does PI planning happen?

Once per Planning Interval, so typically every 8 to 12 weeks. It is normally held during the Innovation and Planning (IP) iteration at the end of the previous PI.

Who attends PI planning?

Ideally everyone on the Agile Release Train, plus Business Owners, Product Management, the System Architect and any shared services or other trains the ART depends on. The Release Train Engineer facilitates.

What are the outputs of PI planning?

PI objectives with business value, an ART planning board of features and dependencies, ROAMed risks and a commitment confirmed by the confidence vote.

What is the difference between committed and uncommitted objectives?

Committed objectives are those the team is confident it can deliver. Uncommitted objectives are planned but less certain; they act as a buffer and do not count in planned business value.

Who assigns business value to PI objectives?

Business Owners, on a scale of 1 to 10, usually during the second breakout on day 2. At the end of the PI they score the actual value achieved.

What is ROAM in PI planning?

A way of handling ART-level risks in front of the whole train: each is marked Resolved, Owned, Accepted or Mitigated.

What happens if the confidence vote is low?

People who voted one or two explain their concerns and teams rework their plans until confidence is acceptable. In common practice an average of three or more is enough to commit.

What is the IP iteration?

The Innovation and Planning iteration ends each PI. It is used for innovation, learning, integration, Inspect and Adapt and the next PI planning, and acts as a buffer.

Can PI planning be done remotely?

Yes. Many trains plan online with a shared digital board, video breakouts and an agenda spread over more days to suit time zones.

Do you need SAFe to run PI planning?

No. It is a SAFe event, but a regular big-room planning session for several teams can be used without the rest of the framework. Smaller groups may find joint release planning or a Scrum of Scrums enough.
