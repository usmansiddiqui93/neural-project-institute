What Does a Scrum Master Do? Role and Responsibilities

A Scrum Master helps a Scrum Team and its organisation use Scrum well: according to the 2020 Scrum Guide, they are accountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. In practice they coach the team in self-management, help the Product Owner manage the backlog, make sure the Scrum events happen and are useful, and cause impediments to be removed, all without any authority over the work itself. This guide explains the role as the Scrum Guide defines it, what it looks like day to day and across a Sprint, and what it is not, with worked examples, tables and step-by-step processes.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • 20 min read

Key takeaways

  • The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. It is an accountability, not a rank, and it carries no authority over who does what.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide describes three areas of service: to the Scrum Team, to the Product Owner and to the wider organisation.
  • Scrum Masters do not assign tasks, own the Sprint Backlog, run the Daily Scrum for the Developers or cancel Sprints; those belong to the Developers and the Product Owner.
  • Most of the work is coaching, facilitation and causing impediments to be removed, plus changing the organisational conditions that slow the team down.
  • Judge a Scrum Master by how well the team improves and delivers valuable, Done increments, not by velocity or how busy they look.
  • PSM I and CSM are the common entry certifications, but real experience with a team matters more to most employers.

What is a Scrum Master?

A Scrum Master is the member of a Scrum Team who is accountable for establishing Scrum as the Scrum Guide defines it and for the team's effectiveness. They help the team, the Product Owner and the organisation understand Scrum theory and practice, coach the team towards self-management, and make sure obstacles to progress are removed. They lead by serving, not by directing.

The 2020 Scrum Guide, still the current official version in October 2026, describes a Scrum Team as a small group (typically 10 or fewer people) with three accountabilities: the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the Developers. The word 'accountability' matters. The Scrum Master is not a job grade or a manager. It is a set of things someone on the team is answerable for, and in some organisations the person who holds it does other work too.

The guide calls Scrum Masters 'true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organization'. Earlier editions used the term 'servant-leader'; the 2020 wording keeps the idea but stresses that serving is a form of leadership, not a support function. If you are new to the framework itself, start with what Scrum is.

A useful one-line test: if the Scrum Master went on holiday for a Sprint, would the team still plan, inspect and adapt well? A good Scrum Master is steadily making that answer 'yes'.

What does a Scrum Master do? The three areas of service

The Scrum Guide groups the Scrum Master's work into service to the Scrum Team, the Product Owner and the organisation. Each table summarises the guide (left) and gives our practical examples (right).

Serving the Scrum Team

Scrum Guide responsibilityWhat it looks like in practice
Coaching team members in self-management and cross-functionalityAsking the team to decide who picks up work; encouraging pairing so knowledge spreads; not answering questions the team can answer itself
Helping the team focus on high-value Increments that meet the Definition of DoneMaking the Definition of Done visible; challenging 'nearly done' work; helping the team finish before starting
Causing the removal of impediments to progressKeeping an impediment list, deciding with the team who acts, escalating what the team cannot fix
Ensuring all Scrum events take place and are positive, productive and kept within the timeboxBooking the events, preparing formats, keeping time, and stepping back once the team can run them alone

Serving the Product Owner

Scrum Guide responsibilityWhat it looks like in practice
Helping find techniques for effective Product Goal definition and Product Backlog managementIntroducing story mapping, ordering techniques or a lightweight Product Backlog structure
Helping the team understand the need for clear and concise Product Backlog itemsFacilitating backlog refinement so items are understood before Sprint Planning
Helping establish empirical product planning for a complex environmentShowing how to forecast from actual delivery data instead of fixed upfront estimates
Facilitating stakeholder collaboration as requested or neededDesigning a Sprint Review where stakeholders try the product and give feedback

Serving the organisation

Scrum Guide responsibilityWhat it looks like in practice
Leading, training and coaching the organisation in its Scrum adoptionRunning short Scrum sessions for managers and stakeholders; answering 'why do we work this way?'
Planning and advising Scrum implementations within the organisationAdvising on team boundaries, release practices and how Scrum fits governance
Helping employees and stakeholders understand and enact an empirical approach for complex workExplaining empiricism: decisions based on what is observed, not on the original plan
Removing barriers between stakeholders and Scrum TeamsReplacing status reports with Sprint Reviews; connecting the team directly with users

Note the verb: the Scrum Master is accountable for causing the removal of impediments, not removing each one personally. Ideally the team learns to remove its own, and the Scrum Master focuses on those needing organisational change.

What a Scrum Master does not do

Job adverts often describe the Scrum Master as an administrator or a renamed project manager. The 2020 Scrum Guide gives several of those duties to other accountabilities.

Common assumptionWhat the 2020 Scrum Guide says
The Scrum Master assigns tasksDevelopers are self-managing and decide who does what. They create the plan for the Sprint, the Sprint Backlog.
The Scrum Master runs the Daily ScrumThe Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for the Developers. The Scrum Master makes sure it happens and may coach, but the Developers run it. If the Scrum Master is working on Sprint Backlog items, they take part as a Developer.
The Scrum Master decides what gets builtThe Product Owner is accountable for maximising value and for the Product Backlog. The Product Owner is one person, not a committee.
The Scrum Master can cancel a SprintOnly the Product Owner has the authority to cancel a Sprint, and only if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.
The Scrum Master is the team's managerThe role has no line authority. Scrum Masters lead through influence, coaching and facilitation.
The Scrum Master writes the status reportProgress is made transparent through the artefacts and the Sprint Review. A Scrum Master may help make information visible but does not report on the team's behalf.

A Scrum Master may still update a board or book a room; those tasks are simply not the point of the role. If administration fills most of the week, coaching and organisational work are being neglected.

The Scrum Master across a Sprint

A Sprint lasts one month or less. The Scrum Guide sets maximum timeboxes for a one-month Sprint and notes that events are usually shorter for shorter Sprints. The table shows each event and the Scrum Master's contribution.

Event or activityMaximum timebox (one-month Sprint)What the Scrum Master does
Sprint Planning8 hoursChecks the Product Owner has a proposed objective and refined items; facilitates so the team agrees a Sprint Goal and a realistic plan; protects the timebox
Daily Scrum15 minutesMakes sure it happens and stays focused on progress towards the Sprint Goal; coaches the Developers to run it themselves; notes impediments raised
Backlog refinementOngoing activity, not an eventHelps the Product Owner and Developers keep enough work ready for the next one or two Sprints
Sprint Review4 hoursHelps design a working session with stakeholders, not a slide show; keeps attention on the product and the Product Goal
Sprint Retrospective3 hoursChooses a format that fits the team's current problems, keeps it safe and focused, and makes sure the team leaves with one or two concrete improvements

For a two-week Sprint, many teams use around half of the one-month timeboxes, for example up to four hours of Sprint Planning. That is a common practice rather than a rule. A sprint planning template and a sprint retrospective template help new Scrum Masters prepare the first few events.

A typical day for a Scrum Master

The schedule below is an illustrative day for a Scrum Master supporting one team, on day four of a two-week Sprint.

TimeActivityWhy it matters
08:45Check the Sprint board and burndown; review open impedimentsSpot work that has stalled before the Daily Scrum
09:30Attend the Daily Scrum (15 minutes) and observeSee whether the team is talking about the Sprint Goal or just reporting status
09:50Follow up two impediments: a test environment outage and a pending security approvalKeep blocked work moving; agree owners and dates
10:30One-to-one coaching with the Product Owner on splitting a large itemMake the next Sprint Planning easier
13:30Backlog refinement session (60 minutes)Keep enough items ready; check acceptance criteria are clear
15:00Meeting with the release manager about a slow approval processRemove an organisational barrier that affects several teams
16:00Draft next week's retrospective formatTarget the retrospective at a real problem
16:30Update the impediment logMake recurring problems visible

How the week changes across a Sprint

  • Start of the Sprint: facilitating Sprint Planning and helping the team turn the Sprint Goal into a first plan.
  • Middle of the Sprint: more observation, coaching, refinement and impediment work; time with stakeholders and other teams.
  • End of the Sprint: preparing and facilitating the Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective, and checking that last Sprint's improvement actually happened.
  • Throughout: work with managers and other Scrum Masters on cross-team issues, for example through a Scrum of Scrums.

How a Scrum Master removes impediments: step by step

An impediment is anything that slows the team's progress towards the Sprint Goal that the team cannot easily resolve by itself. Examples include a broken build pipeline, a missing decision from a stakeholder, a dependency on another team, or a policy that forces work through a slow approval. A simple process keeps impediments visible and stops the Scrum Master becoming a bottleneck.

  1. Make it visible. Add every impediment to a shared list or a column on the board with the date raised and the item it blocks.
  2. Clarify the impact. Ask which Sprint Goal or backlog item is affected and what happens if it is not resolved this week.
  3. Decide who can solve it. Classify it: the team can solve it; the Scrum Master should act; or it needs someone outside the team.
  4. Coach before acting. If the team can solve it, ask questions that help them do so rather than fixing it for them.
  5. Act or escalate with evidence. For impediments outside the team, take facts to the person who can decide: what is blocked, for how long, and the cost of waiting.
  6. Follow up daily. Agree an owner and a next date. Report the status briefly at or after the Daily Scrum.
  7. Prevent recurrence. Bring repeat impediments to the retrospective and look for the root cause, not just the symptom.

Worked example: coaching a team that keeps missing its Sprint Goal

This example is illustrative, but the pattern is common. It shows the role combining observation, data, facilitation and organisational work.

The situation

A team of six Developers works in two-week Sprints. It has missed its Sprint Goal in three Sprints in a row. In each Sprint it pulled in about 12 backlog items and finished 7 or 8. At the end of each Sprint, 4 or 5 items were 'in progress', and the next Sprint started with carry-over. The Product Owner is frustrated and a manager has asked the Scrum Master to 'make the team commit properly'.

What the Scrum Master did

  1. Observed before acting. Over one Sprint they noted that the team regularly had 8 to 10 items in progress at once, and that most items waited several days for review or testing.
  2. Shared the data, not a judgement. At the retrospective they showed a simple chart of items started versus finished per day and asked the team what they saw.
  3. Let the team choose the experiment. The team agreed to limit work in progress to 4 items and to swarm on items waiting for review before starting new ones. See our guide to WIP limits.
  4. Worked with the Product Owner. They coached the Product Owner to propose one clear Sprint Goal rather than a list of unrelated items, and to split two large items in refinement.
  5. Changed the conversation with the manager. Instead of 'commit harder', they explained that a Sprint Backlog is a forecast and that the team needed fewer items in parallel, not more pressure.
  6. Inspected the result. After two Sprints they reviewed the same chart with the team.

The outcome

MeasureBefore (average of 3 Sprints)After (average of next 2 Sprints)
Items planned129
Items Done7.58.5
Items in progress at Sprint end4.51
Sprint Goal met0 of 32 of 2

The team delivered more by planning less, and the Sprint Goal became meaningful again. The Scrum Master assigned no work and set no target: they made the problem visible, helped the team design its own fix and handled the outside pressure.

Coaching, teaching, facilitating and mentoring

Scrum Masters switch between several ways of helping people. Choosing the right one for the moment is a large part of the skill. The four below are widely used in Agile coaching; the advice on when to use each is ours.

StanceWhat it meansUse it whenExample
TeachingExplaining Scrum theory or a practice directlyThe team or a stakeholder lacks knowledgeExplaining why the Sprint Goal gives the Sprint focus
CoachingAsking questions so people find their own answersThe team has the knowledge but needs to think it through'What would need to be true for us to finish this item tomorrow?'
FacilitatingDesigning and guiding a conversation so a group reaches a decisionA group needs to make a shared decision within a timeboxRunning a retrospective with a structured format and a clear end
MentoringSharing your own experience as guidanceSomeone is learning a role you have doneHelping a new Product Owner prepare their first Sprint Review

Once a team knows the mechanics of Scrum, shift from teaching to coaching. Our retrospective games guide has formats for different situations.

Key Scrum Master skills

Most of what makes a good Scrum Master is interpersonal. Technical knowledge helps but is rarely the deciding factor.

SkillWhy it mattersHow to build it
FacilitationEvents that end in decisions save the team timePractise timeboxed formats; ask for feedback after each event
Coaching and questioningBuilds self-management instead of dependenceLearn open questions; resist giving the answer first
Deep knowledge of Scrum and AgileCredibility and the ability to explain whyReread the Scrum Guide; study Scrum values and empiricism
Influence without authorityThe role has no power to instructBuild relationships before you need them; use data and stories
Conflict resolutionHealthy disagreement improves decisions; unresolved conflict stalls teamsName the tension early; separate people from positions
ObservationPatterns are often invisible to people inside themKeep notes on recurring behaviours and share them neutrally
Organisational changeMany impediments sit outside the teamLearn how decisions are really made in your organisation; see our Agile transformation guide

You do not need a technical background, but understanding how your team's work flows (review, testing, release) helps you ask good questions. Scrum Masters come from development, testing, project coordination, business analysis and teaching.

Scrum Master vs project manager, Product Owner and Agile coach

These roles are often confused, and in smaller organisations one person may hold more than one. The table compares their focus and authority.

Scrum MasterProject managerProduct OwnerAgile coach
Defined byThe Scrum GuideThe organisation; standards such as PMBOK and PRINCE2The Scrum GuideThe organisation
Main focusTeam effectiveness and use of ScrumDelivering agreed scope, time and budgetMaximising product valueAgile ways of working across several teams or the organisation
Authority over the workNoneUsually plans and assigns workOrders the Product BacklogNone; advisory
Typical scopeOne or a few teamsA projectA productSeveral teams, a department or leadership
Success looks likeA team that improves and delivers Done incrementsObjectives met on time and budgetValuable outcomes for users and the businessLasting change in how the organisation works

The biggest practical difference between a Scrum Master and a project manager is control. A project manager typically creates and maintains the plan; in Scrum, the Developers plan their own work and the Product Owner orders the backlog. Many organisations still employ project managers around Scrum Teams, especially for budgets, suppliers and cross-team coordination.

How to tell whether a Scrum Master is effective

The best evidence is in the team, not in the Scrum Master's calendar. Useful signals include:

  • The team regularly meets its Sprint Goal, and Increments meet the Definition of Done.
  • Retrospective actions are completed and visibly change how the team works.
  • Impediments are resolved faster over time, and fewer of them recur.
  • Events finish within their timebox and produce decisions, and the team runs more of them without help.
  • Stakeholders attend the Sprint Review and their feedback reaches the Product Backlog.
  • Flow measures such as cycle time and work in progress become more stable.

Avoid using velocity as a target for the team or the Scrum Master. Velocity is a planning aid for the team; when it becomes a target, estimates tend to inflate and the number stops meaning anything. Our guide to Scrum metrics and KPIs explains which measures help.

Full-time or part-time? How many teams?

The Scrum Guide does not say whether the Scrum Master must be full-time or how many teams one person can support. The scenarios below are our own guidance, based on how much coaching and organisational work each situation tends to need.

SituationTypical arrangementNotes
New team, or an organisation new to ScrumFull-time, one teamMost time goes on teaching, facilitation and organisational impediments
Established team with a stable way of workingOne Scrum Master for two teamsLeaves room for coaching both teams and cross-team work
Very mature, self-managing teamsUp to three teams, or a part-time roleRisk: the role shrinks to booking meetings; watch for that
Small company with one teamA Developer also holds the Scrum Master accountabilityWorks if they protect time for the role; can be hard during busy Sprints

Can the Scrum Master also be the Product Owner? The Scrum Guide does not explicitly forbid it, but we advise against it. The Product Owner pushes for value and scope, and the Scrum Master protects the team's way of working; combining them removes a useful balance. Combining Scrum Master and Developer is more common and workable in small teams.

Common Scrum Master mistakes

Most of these come from slipping back into a manager or administrator role.

MistakeWhat happensBetter approach
Acting as the team's administratorNo time left for coaching or organisational workAutomate or share admin; protect time for coaching
Running the Daily Scrum as a status meetingDevelopers report to the Scrum Master instead of planning togetherStep back; coach the Developers to focus on the Sprint Goal
Solving every impediment personallyThe team becomes dependent and the Scrum Master becomes a bottleneckCoach the team to solve what it can; escalate the rest
Avoiding conflict with managersOrganisational impediments persistBring evidence and options; frame issues in terms of outcomes
Same retrospective format every SprintLow energy and repeated, vague actionsVary formats; follow up on actions at the start of each retrospective
Enforcing Scrum rigidly without explaining whyScrum is seen as bureaucracyLink each practice to the problem it solves
Using velocity to compare or push teamsInflated estimates and lost trustUse velocity for forecasting only, inside the team

Scrum Master certifications

No certificate is required to work as a Scrum Master, but employers often ask for one. The two most common entry-level options are Professional Scrum Master I from Scrum.org and Certified ScrumMaster from Scrum Alliance.

Professional Scrum Master I (PSM I)Certified ScrumMaster (CSM)
Awarded byScrum.orgScrum Alliance
Course requiredNo (a course is recommended, not required)Yes: a live course with a Certified Scrum Trainer, typically 14 to 16 hours over two days
Exam80 questions, 60 minutes, 85% to pass50 questions, 60 minutes, 74% to pass
CostUS$200 per attempt (assessment only)Included in the course fee, which varies by trainer
RenewalLifetime; no renewal feeEvery two years: US$100 and 20 Scrum Education Units

PSM I facts are from Scrum.org (checked 25 October 2026). CSM facts are as published for Scrum Alliance at the time of writing; check its website before you book, as course formats and fees vary. Both organisations offer advanced levels (such as PSM II and Advanced CSM) for experienced Scrum Masters.

Experience counts more than any certificate: facilitating retrospectives or acting as Scrum Master for a team gives you real examples to discuss.

Will AI replace the Scrum Master?

AI tools can already summarise meetings, flag stalled work and spot patterns in delivery data. That reduces administration and leaves more time for the parts of the role that depend on trust and judgement: coaching a team through conflict, persuading a manager to change a policy, or noticing that a quiet team member has stopped contributing. In our view, AI changes how Scrum Masters spend their time rather than removing the need for the accountability. Our guide to Scrum software tools covers boards and assistants that support the role.

Frequently asked questions

What does a Scrum Master do every day?

A typical day includes observing the Daily Scrum, following up impediments, coaching the Product Owner or Developers, supporting backlog refinement, preparing upcoming events such as the retrospective, and working with managers or other teams to remove organisational blockers.

Is a Scrum Master a manager?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes Scrum Masters as true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the organisation. They have no authority to assign work or decide how it is done; they lead through coaching, facilitation and influence.

Who does a Scrum Master report to?

Scrum does not specify a reporting line. In practice, Scrum Masters often report to a head of delivery, an Agile coach, an engineering manager or a PMO.

Does the Scrum Master run the Daily Scrum?

No. The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for the Developers. The Scrum Master makes sure it takes place and stays useful, and may coach the team, but the Developers run it. If the Scrum Master is also working on Sprint Backlog items, they take part as a Developer.

Can the Scrum Master also be a Developer or the Product Owner?

A Scrum Master can also be a Developer, which is common in small teams. The Scrum Guide does not explicitly forbid combining Scrum Master and Product Owner, but we advise against it because the two accountabilities balance each other.

How many teams can a Scrum Master support?

Our guidance is one team for a new team, two for established teams and up to three for very mature, self-managing teams.

Does a Scrum Master need a technical background?

Not necessarily. Understanding how the team's work flows helps you ask good questions, and many Scrum Masters come from development or testing. Facilitation and coaching skills matter more.

What is an example of an impediment?

Anything that slows the team's progress that it cannot easily fix alone: a broken test environment, a decision a stakeholder has not made, a dependency on another team, a slow approval process, or team members repeatedly pulled into other work.

Does a Scrum Master need a certification?

Not formally, but employers often ask for one. PSM I from Scrum.org is an assessment with no course requirement and no renewal fee; CSM from Scrum Alliance requires a live course and renewal every two years. Practical experience with a team matters more.

What is the difference between a Scrum Master and a project manager?

A Scrum Master is accountable for the team's effectiveness and use of Scrum and has no authority over the work. A project manager typically plans and directs work to deliver agreed scope on time and on budget.

What does 'true leaders who serve' mean?

It is the 2020 Scrum Guide's description of Scrum Masters, replacing the earlier term 'servant-leader'. It means the Scrum Master leads by helping others succeed: removing obstacles, coaching and creating the conditions for good work, rather than by giving instructions.

Can a Scrum Team work without a Scrum Master?

Not in Scrum as defined by the Scrum Guide, which makes the Scrum Master one of the three accountabilities. Experienced teams sometimes share the duties, but someone still needs to be accountable for the team's effectiveness and the use of Scrum.
