Agile Team Structures: Types, Examples and How to Choose

An Agile team structure is the way an organisation divides people and work into small, long-lived, cross-functional teams, decides what each team owns and sets how those teams work with one another. Most organisations build mainly feature or stream-aligned teams that deliver end to end, supported by a few platform, enabling or specialist teams. This guide compares the options, explains how to choose between them and shows how to test a candidate structure against your real backlog before you reorganise.

Key takeaways

  • Team structure involves three decisions: where each team's boundary sits (what it owns), who is in each team (composition) and how teams connect (interaction modes, line management and multi-team coordination).
  • Feature or stream-aligned teams that can deliver a customer outcome alone should be the default; platform, enabling and complicated-subsystem teams exist to make those teams faster, not to own slices of every feature.
  • Conway's law explains why structure matters: the system you build tends to mirror the communication structure of the teams building it, so team boundaries become architecture.
  • Test a proposed structure by mapping your next ten to twenty features to the teams that would touch them; count cross-team handoffs, not boxes on an organisation chart.
  • Keep teams stable and change structure deliberately, a few times a year at most, when dependency, flow or cognitive-load measures show the current design no longer fits.

What is an Agile team structure?

An Agile team structure is the deliberate arrangement of an organisation's delivery people into small, stable, cross-functional teams, each with a clear area of ownership, together with the rules for how those teams cooperate, who manages the people in them and how several teams align on one product. It is an organisation-design choice that sits above any single team.

Designing one means answering three questions:

  • Boundaries: what does each team own? A customer journey, a product, a market, a technical component or a shared platform?
  • Composition: which skills sit inside each team, and how deep or broad does each member's expertise need to be?
  • Connections: how do teams depend on and help one another, who are people's line managers, and how do several teams plan and integrate when they work on the same product?

This guide deals with those organisation-wide choices. For the anatomy of a single team (its characteristics, roles, size and how to form it), start with what an Agile team is. For one well-known multi-team design in depth, see the Spotify model of squads, tribes, chapters and guilds.

Why team structure matters

Conway's law: structure becomes architecture

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that "any organization that designs a system (defined broadly) will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure". Separate front-end, back-end and database teams tend to produce systems with hard seams between those layers, and every customer feature then has to cross the seams. Some organisations now use the inverse Conway manoeuvre, a term coined by Jonny LeRoy and Matt Simons in 2010: they shape teams around the architecture they want, so the system grows in that direction.

Dependencies turn into queues

Each time a piece of work has to pass to another team, it usually waits until that team can fit it into its own plan. A feature that touches three teams has two handoffs and can wait twice. Over many features those waits, rather than the hands-on work, often make up much of the lead time, which a value stream map will show for your own process. Reducing the number of teams each item must pass through is often the single biggest flow improvement available.

Cognitive load limits what one team can own

The *Team Topologies* approach, published in 2019 by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais, treats a team's cognitive load (the amount it must understand to work safely) as a design constraint: overloaded teams "make poor decisions and move slowly". A team that owns too many systems, domains or technologies will struggle however capable its members are. Boundaries should therefore keep each team's area small enough to understand well.

Ways to draw team boundaries

Most Agile organisations combine several of the following team types. The question is not which single type to use, but which should be the default and which should be exceptions.

Team typeWhat it ownsUse it whenMain risk
Feature or stream-aligned teamA flow of customer value: a journey, product, customer segment or market, across all technical layersDefault choice for most delivery workNeeds broad skills and shared engineering standards across code it does not exclusively own
Component teamOne technical part of the system, such as a search service or identity and loginThe component is reused by many teams, needs rare expertise or carries high risk if built inconsistentlyFeatures need several teams; handoffs and late integration
Platform teamInternal services other teams use on a self-service basis, such as build pipelines, hosting or data platformsSeveral stream-aligned teams would otherwise build the same capabilityBuilding what nobody asked for; ignoring its internal customers
Enabling teamExpertise it lends to other teams for a limited period, such as test automation or security practicesTeams need a new capability quicklyBecoming a permanent dependency instead of stepping back
Complicated-subsystem teamA part needing deep specialist knowledge, such as a fraud-scoring model or signal-processing algorithmFew people have the expertise and spreading it thinly would be unsafeGrowing into a component team for ordinary work
Functional or specialist teamOne discipline, such as testing, analysis or designRarely, for example a small central design-systems groupSequential handoffs, more documentation and reviews; PMI's Disciplined Agile notes higher communication overhead and project risk
Internal open-source modelA shared component that one team maintains and other teams contribute toMany teams need changes to one component and its owner would become a bottleneckNeeds clear contribution rules and review capacity

Feature teams and component teams

This is the oldest debate in Agile team design. Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS) defines a feature team as "a long-lived, cross-functional, cross-component team" that works on complete customer-centric features, and lists the drawbacks of component teams, among them extra planning to manage dependencies between teams, responsibility for only part of a customer-centric feature, individual code ownership and "waterfall" development. It also warns that hybrid models can have "the drawbacks from both worlds". Mountain Goat Software's guidance is similar but less absolute: most teams should be feature teams, and component teams are reasonable when several feature teams need the component, building it once reduces the overloading of scarce specialists, and the component team builds only what feature teams are ready to use.

In practice the decision turns on how often features cross component lines. If most work changes one component, component teams lose little. If most features touch three or four components, component teams turn each feature into a multi-team project. The worked example below shows how to count this.

How SAFe applies the four team types

The Scaled Agile Framework uses the four *Team Topologies* types (stream-aligned, complicated subsystem, platform and enabling) as a template for organising teams on an Agile Release Train. Its guidance, last updated in April 2023, says most teams should be stream-aligned, that a single train should contain no more than one to three complicated-subsystem teams, and that it is common to have two or three enabling teams working across the enterprise. A rough sense check of our own, not a SAFe rule, follows from those ratios: if more than about a quarter of your teams are not stream-aligned, ask why.

Team composition: generalists, specialists and T-shaped people

Once a team's boundary is set, its membership has to cover the skills the boundary demands. The 2020 Scrum Guide notes that the skills Developers need "are often broad and will vary with the domain of work". Cross-functionality is a property of the team as a whole: no individual needs every skill, but someone in the team must hold each one.

CompositionDescriptionStrengthWeakness
SpecialistsEach member has one deep skillHigh quality within each disciplineBottlenecks when one skill is overloaded; little cover for absence
GeneralistsMembers can do many kinds of taskFlexible; work rarely waits for one personCan lack depth for complex work
T-shaped (generalising specialists)One deep skill plus working ability in neighbouring areasBalances depth with the ability to help where work piles upTakes deliberate time to build

Most Agile teams aim for the T-shaped pattern because it lets people help finish work outside their speciality, which is what makes practices such as agile swarming possible. Build it on purpose: map the team's skills, identify skills held by only one person and plan pairing or training to add a second. The skills matrix template rates each person from 0 to 4 per skill and shows those single points of failure.

Shared specialists

Scarce specialists, such as a security engineer or a data scientist, are often split across several teams. Mountain Goat Software notes that someone who is "20% on five teams" is rarely experienced as 20% available by any of them: they miss conversations, switch context constantly and become a queue. Better options are to give each specialist one primary team, form a small enabling team that coaches others and steps back, or create a complicated-subsystem team if the work genuinely needs concentrated expertise.

How teams work with each other

Boundaries never remove all dependencies, so decide how teams should interact. *Team Topologies* describes three interaction modes.

Interaction modeWhat it meansGood forSignal it has run too long
CollaborationTwo teams work closely together "for a defined period of time to discover new things"New technology, unclear boundaries, early discoveryThe teams still need each other for routine work months later
X-as-a-ServiceOne team provides something and another consumes it with minimal coordinationStable platforms and well-defined interfacesConsumers keep raising tickets to get basic things done
FacilitationOne team helps and mentors anotherEnabling teams spreading a skill or practiceThe helped team cannot proceed without the facilitator

Making the mode explicit helps. A platform team that thinks it is collaborating while its users expect a service will frustrate both sides. A good default is to collaborate briefly while an interface is being discovered, then move to X-as-a-Service once it is stable.

For recurring coordination between teams on one product, lightweight events such as a Scrum of Scrums or joint refinement of a shared Product Backlog are usually enough. If coordination needs more than that every week, treat it as a boundary problem rather than a meeting problem.

Line management and reporting lines

Agile teams are self-managing, and the 2020 Scrum Guide says that "within a Scrum Team, there are no sub-teams or hierarchies". People still need line managers for hiring, career development, pay and performance conversations. Those managers usually sit outside the team, and the choice of where affects how the structure behaves.

OptionHow it worksStrengthWatch out for
Functional line managementPeople report to a head of their discipline (engineering, design, testing) while working in a cross-functional teamStrong professional standards and career pathsManagers pulling people into functional priorities; divided loyalty
Product-aligned managementPeople report to a manager responsible for a product area containing several teamsClear alignment with delivery goalsCraft standards drift between areas
Chapter modelA practising specialist in the same area line-manages people with their skill across several teamsCombines craft leadership with team focusChapter leads with too many reports; see the Spotify model guide
Project-based allocationManagers assign people to temporary project teamsFamiliar to traditional organisationsUnstable teams; works against long-lived Agile teams

Whichever option you choose, keep day-to-day work decisions with the team and its Product Owner, not with the line manager. A RACI matrix template is a quick way to agree who decides on hiring, team membership, priorities and performance. In some organisations an Agile project manager coordinates budgets, reporting and cross-team dependencies; Scrum itself defines no project manager accountability, as the Scrum team roles guide explains.

Structures for several teams on one product

When one product needs more people than one team can hold, the 2020 Scrum Guide's answer is to reorganise "into multiple cohesive Scrum Teams, each focused on the same product", which "should share the same Product Goal, Product Backlog, and Product Owner". Scaling frameworks add structure on top of that principle.

StructureTypical scaleWhat it addsGuide

A scaling framework does not decide your team boundaries for you. Each still assumes mostly feature or stream-aligned teams, so the boundary choices in this guide come first. The Scaled Agile Framework guide and the LeSS guide compare the heavier options in detail.

How to choose an Agile team structure

The "Use it when" column in the boundary table above tells you when each supporting team type earns its place. For the default delivery teams, and for two situations that table does not cover, use the signals below. Several will usually apply; the structure that removes the most frequent handoffs normally wins.

If you see thisLean towardsBecause
Most features touch several technical componentsFeature or stream-aligned teamsOne team can finish a feature without waiting for others
Work divides naturally by customer journey, product or marketStream-aligned teams on those linesBoundaries match how value reaches customers
Most work stays inside one componentComponent teams lose little; keep them if they already workFew features would cross team lines
Teams are overloaded with too many systemsSplit boundaries or move systems to a platformReduces cognitive load
Regulation requires separation of dutiesKeep the separation in the workflow, not in separate teams, where the regulator allowsAvoids recreating handoffs that compliance does not actually require

A structure suits your situation when, for most upcoming work, one team can take an item from idea to release, each team can explain its area in a sentence, and no team's people spend most of their week waiting for another team.

How to design your team structure, step by step

  1. Map how value flows. Trace how a typical request travels from idea to customer and where it waits. Value stream mapping gives you the data.
  2. List the next ten to twenty features. Use real items from the roadmap or Product Backlog, not hypothetical ones.
  3. Mark what each feature touches. For each feature, note the components, systems and skills it needs.
  4. Sketch two or three candidate structures. For example, by customer journey, by product line and by component. Include any platform or enabling teams you think you need.
  5. Count handoffs for each candidate. For every feature, count how many teams it would pass through; handoffs equal teams touched minus one. Prefer the candidate with the fewest.
  6. Check demand balance and cognitive load. Make sure each team has a reasonable share of the work and an area small enough to understand.
  7. Allocate people and check skills. Place people so that each team covers its skills, and use a skills matrix to find single points of failure.
  8. Agree interaction modes and decision rights. Decide which relationships are collaboration, X-as-a-Service or facilitation, and who manages whom.
  9. Pilot, then review. Start with one or two teams if you can, measure flow for three to six months, and adjust boundaries before rolling out further.
Tip

Involve the people affected. Teams that help design their own boundaries, or choose their team within agreed constraints, tend to adopt the new structure faster than teams that receive an organisation chart.

Worked example: redesigning an online retailer's teams

This example is illustrative. An online retailer has 24 delivery people in four component teams of six: web front end, mobile, checkout services and database. Each team does its own testing. A typical feature needs several teams, so items queue between them and even simple changes take weeks.

Step 1: map upcoming features to components

The product leaders list ten features from the roadmap and mark which components each would change (W = web front end, M = mobile, C = checkout services, D = database). They also note which customer journey each belongs to and whether it needs new shared infrastructure.

FeatureJourneyComponentsTeams (component structure)Teams (feature structure)

Add a digital wallet payment optionBuyW, M, C31

Product recommendation carousel (needs a new hosted service)BrowseW, M, D32 (Browse + platform)
Change delivery address after orderingAccountW, M, C, D41
Fix promotion-code validationBuyC11
Wish listAccountW, M, D31
Show stock availability on product pagesBrowseW, M, D31
Guest checkoutBuyW, M, C, D41

Step 2: count teams and handoffs

Handoffs per feature = teams touched − 1. Under the component structure the ten features touch 30 teams in total, an average of 3.0 teams per feature, and only one feature (the promotion-code fix) can be finished by a single team. That gives 30 − 10 = 20 handoffs. Under a structure of three journey-aligned feature teams (Browse, Buy and Account) plus a platform team, the features touch 11 teams, an average of 1.1, and nine of the ten can be finished by one team. Only the recommendation carousel needs the platform team, so there is one handoff. Handoffs fall from 20 to 1, a reduction of 95%.

To turn handoffs into time, the team makes one explicit assumption: in two-week Sprints, a request passed to another team waits on average about half a Sprint, or 5 working days, before that team starts it. Expected waiting = handoffs × 5 working days. Under the component structure that is 20 × 5 = 100 working days of waiting across the ten features, or 10 working days per feature on average. Under the feature structure it is 1 × 5 = 5 working days in total, or 0.5 days per feature.

Step 3: check demand and allocate people

Demand is reasonably balanced: Buy has four of the ten features, Browse and Account three each. The component counts show where skills are needed: web front end appears in nine features, mobile and database in eight each, and checkout services in five, four of them in the Buy journey. The 24 people are reallocated accordingly.

Browse22026

Every original specialist is placed and headcount is unchanged. The Buy team holds both of the checkout-services specialists it needs for four features; Browse needs none. The skills matrix shows that the Account team has only one checkout-services person, so the platform team agrees to pair with Account on its one checkout change for the first two Sprints, an explicit, time-limited collaboration.

Step 4: interpret the result

  • The structure removes most cross-team waiting for this set of features, so lead time should fall. The 10 days of average waiting is an estimate; the retailer should measure cycle time before and after.
  • Feature teams now change code they used to hand to others. The retailer agrees shared coding standards, code review across teams for the checkout and database code, and an X-as-a-Service relationship with the platform team.
  • The test is only as good as the feature list. The retailer repeats the count each quarter with the next roadmap; if a growing share of features needs two teams, boundaries need another look.

Limitations: the 5-day wait per handoff is an assumption, and real waits vary with each team's priorities; the count treats every handoff as equal and ignores the size of each feature; and it does not measure the extra knowledge feature teams now need, which will slow them at first.

Changing structure without losing momentum

Restructuring has a cost: new teams must form, agree working practices and learn unfamiliar code. Convert one or two teams, or one journey, at a time rather than everyone on the same day; keep specialists close to the code they know and plan pairing for the areas each new team must learn; then leave the new teams alone long enough for them to settle and for their measures to mean something (six to nine months is a reasonable rule of thumb). Rising cross-team dependencies, teams that cannot describe their area in a sentence, constant escalations or one permanently overloaded team are reasons to revisit boundaries. A new executive's preference is not.

Agile transformation covers the wider organisational change, including leadership, funding and governance, and how to manage Agile teams covers the manager's role during and after a restructure.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best Agile team structure?

There is no single best structure. For most product work, feature or stream-aligned teams that can deliver a customer outcome alone should be the default, supported by a small number of platform, enabling or complicated-subsystem teams where shared services or rare expertise justify them.

What are the main types of Agile teams?

The common types are feature or stream-aligned teams, component teams, platform teams, enabling teams and complicated-subsystem teams. Functional teams organised by discipline exist too, but they create handoffs that Agile structures usually try to remove.

Should I use feature teams or component teams?

Count how many components your typical feature touches. If most features change several components, feature teams remove most handoffs. If most work stays within one component, or a component needs rare expertise and must stay consistent, a component team can make sense.

What is a stream-aligned team?

A stream-aligned team, a term from Team Topologies, is aligned to a flow of work from a segment of the business, such as a product, customer journey or market. It delivers and runs that flow end to end and is the primary team type in the model.

What is a platform team in Agile?

A platform team builds and runs internal services, such as build pipelines, hosting or data platforms, that other teams use on a self-service basis. Its purpose is to reduce the cognitive load on stream-aligned teams, so it should treat those teams as customers.

What is Conway's law?

Conway's law, from Melvin Conway's 1968 article, says that organisations design systems that copy their own communication structures. In practice, team boundaries shape software architecture, which is why team structure is an architectural decision as well as an organisational one.

Who do Agile team members report to?

It depends on the reporting model. Common options are a functional head of discipline, a product-area manager or a chapter lead who practises the same skill. The line manager handles careers and performance; the team and Product Owner decide day-to-day work.

Does an Agile team structure need a project manager?

Scrum defines no project manager accountability. Some organisations keep project managers to coordinate budgets, reporting and dependencies across teams. That role should support the teams rather than assign their work.

How often should you change an Agile team structure?

Rarely. Teams need stability to form and learn, so change structure deliberately and infrequently, when measures such as cross-team dependencies, waiting time or cognitive load show the current boundaries no longer fit the work.

When do you need a scaling framework?

Two or three teams on one product can usually coordinate with a shared Product Owner, Product Backlog and a Scrum of Scrums. Beyond that, frameworks such as LeSS, SAFe or Scrum@Scale add planning and coordination structures, but you still need good team boundaries first.
