Agile IT Operations: Kanban, ITIL and DevOps in Practice

Agile IT operations means running infrastructure, platform and support work in short feedback loops, with all work visible, priorities explicit and improvement built into the week. Because operations teams handle a constant stream of incidents and requests, most adapt Agile through Kanban, capacity reserved for unplanned work, a written interrupt policy and steady automation, while keeping their IT service management practices. This guide shows how, with an illustrative worked example and the metrics to track.

Key takeaways

  • Operations work mixes unplanned incidents, routine requests and planned improvement, so practices built around a fixed sprint commitment usually need adapting.
  • Kanban with WIP limits, classes of service and an explicit expedite rule fits most interrupt-driven teams; Scrum or Scrumban suits teams whose work is mostly planned.
  • Size the capacity you reserve for unplanned work from your own data, ideally eight weeks or more, rather than from a rule of thumb.
  • Agile does not replace ITIL: incident, request and change enablement practices still apply, and Agile changes how the work flows through them.
  • DORA now defines five software delivery metrics; failed deployment recovery time replaced the old MTTR measure in 2023 and covers only failures caused by changes.
  • Treat automation and toil reduction as backlog items with protected capacity; otherwise the same interruptions return every week.

What is Agile in IT operations?

Agile in IT operations is the application of Agile values and Lean flow practices to the teams that run live services, such as infrastructure, network, platform, database, service desk and security operations. It makes every type of work visible, limits work in progress, plans for interruptions and improves the service in small, frequent steps, while keeping reliability and control.

You will also see the label AgileOps. Vendors and analysts use it for the same idea: extending Agile and DevOps ways of working beyond software development into the operation of systems. It is a label rather than a defined framework, so there is no official AgileOps guide, role set or certification behind it.

Agile operations is not a licence to skip change approval, documentation or service levels. The Agile Manifesto values working software over comprehensive documentation, not instead of it, and an operations team still needs runbooks, audit evidence and agreed targets. What changes is how the team decides what to work on next, how much it starts at once and how quickly it learns from failures.

Why operations work needs a different kind of Agile

Scrum was designed for a team that can commit to a goal for a short period and protect it. An operations team rarely has that luxury. A storage array fails on Tuesday, a security patch becomes urgent on Wednesday and fifteen access requests arrive before Friday. Classify the work first, because each type needs a different policy.

Work typeExampleArrival patternTypical policy
IncidentsOutage, failed backup, security alertUnpredictable; urgency variesExpedite lane or on-call rota; strict limit on parallel expedites
Service requestsNew accounts, access changes, firewall ruleSteady, fairly predictable volumeStandard flow; automate or self-serve the most frequent
Planned improvementUpgrade a database cluster, automate backupsChosen by the teamOrdered backlog; protected capacity
Maintenance and compliancePatching, certificate renewals, auditsRecurring, often date-drivenFixed-date class of service; scheduled in advance

The tension is between stability and change. Every change to a live system is a potential incident, and every incident pulls people away from the changes that would prevent the next one. Agile practices help only if they make that trade-off explicit: how much capacity goes to keeping the lights on, how much to improvement, and who decides when an interruption is allowed to displace planned work.

This guide stays with the specific mechanics of operations teams. Read Is Agile only for software? for the wider question of using Agile beyond development.

Kanban, Scrum or Scrumban for an operations team?

Choose the method from your work mix. Measure the share of team effort that goes to unplanned work for several weeks before deciding; guessing tends to underestimate it.

SituationBest starting pointWhy
Unplanned work takes a large and variable share of effortKanbanNo fixed commitment to break; work is pulled continuously and urgent items have explicit rules
Mostly planned project work, such as a data-centre migration, with light support dutiesScrum, with a rotating support roleSprint Goals give focus; one person shields the rest of the team
A steady mix of bothScrumbanKanban flow with a regular planning and review cadence
Separate support and engineering sub-teamsKanban for support, Scrum or Kanban for engineeringEach sub-team uses the method that suits its work

There is no universally agreed threshold at which Scrum stops working. As a decision rule, if your team cannot protect a meaningful Sprint Goal in most sprints because interruptions keep displacing it, start with Kanban. Core practices for Agile operations teams

The practices below reinforce each other. A board without WIP limits becomes a wall of half-finished tickets, and WIP limits without an interrupt policy are broken by the first outage.

One board for all work

Put incidents, requests, maintenance and improvement work on one Kanban board, even if tickets live in a service desk tool. Columns follow your real workflow, for example Ready, Investigating or Doing, Waiting on others, Verifying and Done. If incidents stay in one tool and project work in another, the team looks idle on one screen and overloaded on the other, and nobody can see the trade-offs being made.

An interrupt policy and classes of service

A class of service is a set of policies for how a type of work is treated, based on what happens if it is delayed. Operations teams typically use Expedite for live incidents, Fixed date for patches and certificate expiries, Standard for requests and Intangible for improvements such as technical debt. Write down who may declare an expedite, how many can run at once (usually one) and what happens to planned work when one arrives. The full set of lane rules is in our Kanban swimlanes guide.

WIP limits and pull

Limit how many items each stage may hold, and pull new work only when something finishes. In operations, the *Waiting on others* column needs a limit too, because tickets waiting for a supplier, a change window or a user reply quietly accumulate. Start a little below today's actual work in progress and adjust after a few weeks; WIP limits explains the method and Little's Law behind it.

Reserved capacity sized from data

Reserve a share of team capacity for unplanned work, and size it from your history rather than from someone else's percentage. Log the hours, or at least the items, spent on incidents and unplanned requests each week for eight weeks or more. Then choose a reservation that covers most weeks, not just the average week, because a reservation set at the average is exceeded in roughly half of all weeks. Our Agile capacity planning guide covers focus factors and leave in more detail.

A light cadence

Operations teams need fewer meetings than sprint teams, but they need some. A useful starting set is a short daily meeting at the board focused on blocked, ageing and expedited items; weekly replenishment to choose the next improvement items; a fortnightly or monthly retrospective; and a monthly service review with stakeholders. Kanban meetings and cadences describes each one.

Automation and toil reduction as backlog work

Google's site reliability engineering (SRE) book defines toil as operational work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, has no enduring value and grows linearly as the service grows. Google aims to keep operational work below 50% of each SRE's time so that engineering work can reduce it. You do not need to adopt SRE to borrow the idea: tag toil on the board, count it, and keep a standing improvement item in progress that removes the most frequent kind.

Blameless post-incident reviews

After a significant incident, review how the system, process and information available allowed it to happen, not who made the mistake. Record the timeline, the contributing factors, what went well and the corrective actions, each with an owner and a date. Put those actions on the same board as everything else, or they compete invisibly with new requests and lose. The root cause analysis template provides a 5 whys worksheet and a corrective action plan with a column for how you will verify each fix worked.

Agile alongside ITIL and IT service management

ITIL describes service management practices; Agile and Kanban describe how a team organises and improves its work. They operate at different levels, so most organisations use both. ITIL 4's guiding principles, such as *progress iteratively with feedback* and *focus on value*, are already close to Agile thinking.

PeopleCert describes ITIL (Version 5) as a gradual, phased release and states that ITIL 4 remains available. PeopleCert's own pages give no dates; accredited training providers reported the Foundation course launching in February 2026, with other modules following during the year, and say no retirement date for ITIL 4 has been announced. This guide uses ITIL 4 terms, which remain in wide use while the new version rolls out. Check PeopleCert's site for the current position before choosing a certification.

ITSM practiceAgile way to run itWhat stays the same
Incident managementExpedite class of service; swarming; post-incident review items on the backlogPriority definitions, escalation paths, major incident procedure
Service request managementStandard flow with WIP limits; frequent requests automated or self-servedRequest catalogue and fulfilment records
Problem managementProblems become improvement items with protected capacityKnown error records and workarounds
Change enablementStandard changes and peer review for low risk; automated pipeline evidenceRisk assessment, change authority and audit trail for higher-risk changes
Service level managementService level targets reviewed in a regular service review with usersThe agreed targets themselves; see service level agreements

Change enablement without a weekly bottleneck

ITIL 4 calls the practice change enablement (its first edition used the term change control) and distinguishes standard changes (pre-authorised, low risk, repeatable), normal changes (assessed and authorised according to risk) and emergency changes (implemented quickly to restore or protect service, then reviewed). It also uses the idea of a change authority, which need not be a central board: it can be a peer reviewer or an automated check in a deployment pipeline. Agile operations teams use this flexibility deliberately. They turn their most common changes into standard changes with tested runbooks, use peer review for low-risk normal changes and reserve formal assessment for changes that could seriously affect services.

Project teams sometimes confuse this with project change control, which governs changes to a project's scope, schedule or budget baseline. The difference is explained in change management vs change control.

Agile operations, DevOps and SRE

Agile operations applies Agile and Lean flow practices inside operations teams. DevOps goes further: it joins development and operations into shared ownership of building, releasing and running software, with practices such as infrastructure as code, automated pipelines and shared on-call. Site reliability engineering is one specific way to run operations with software engineering methods, built around service level objectives and error budgets. An operations team can be Agile without full DevOps, but the biggest gains usually come when the wall between the teams that build and those that run a service comes down. Our DevOps guide covers those practices and their origins.

Error budgets: deciding between stability and change

An error budget turns the stability-versus-change argument into a number both sides accept. The formula is:

Error budget = (1 − SLO) × measurement period, where the SLO (service level objective) is the target proportion of time, or of requests, in which the service works as intended, expressed as a decimal, and the measurement period is in minutes (or in requests, for a request-based SLO).

Illustrative calculation. A service has an availability SLO of 99.9% over a 30-day month. The month has 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes. The error budget is (1 − 0.999) × 43,200 = 43.2 minutes of unavailability. A 25-minute outage caused by a failed configuration change uses 25 ÷ 43.2 = 57.9% of the budget, leaving 18.2 minutes for the rest of the month.

Google's SRE book describes halting releases when the budget is exhausted and investing the time in testing and reliability work; slowing releases as the budget runs low is a gentler variant. The calculation assumes the SLO reflects what users actually notice and that monitoring measures it independently. If the SLO is set arbitrarily, the budget gives a precise answer to the wrong question.

How to introduce Agile in an operations team, step by step

The sequence below suits an existing team of roughly 4 to 12 people. The timings are typical, not fixed; a team with poor ticket data will need longer at step 2.

  1. Visualise everything (weeks 1–2). Put all incidents, requests, maintenance and project items on one board without changing anything else. Agree the column names with the team.
  2. Measure the work mix (weeks 1–8). Record, each week, the effort or item count for each work type. Eight weeks gives a more reliable picture than two, especially if your workload is seasonal.
  3. Write the interrupt policy (week 3). Define classes of service, who can declare an expedite, the expedite limit and what happens to displaced work.
  4. Set WIP limits (week 3). Start slightly below current WIP for each active column, including Waiting on others.
  5. Reserve capacity (week 4). Use the measured unplanned effort to decide how much capacity to hold back; review it monthly.
  6. Build an improvement backlog (week 4). List automation, reliability and problem-management items, ordered by the effort or incidents they would remove. Name one person to own the order.
  7. Add the cadence (week 5). Daily board meeting, weekly replenishment, a regular retrospective and a monthly service review.
  8. Streamline change enablement (weeks 5–12). Agree with your change authority which frequent changes can become standard changes.
  9. Review metrics monthly (from week 8). Look at cycle time per class of service, throughput, unplanned share and the change metrics below.
  10. Adjust one thing at a time. Change a WIP limit, the reservation or a policy, then watch the effect for a few weeks before the next change.

Organisation-wide rollouts raise different questions, such as funding, governance and leadership behaviour. See how to implement Agile for that wider programme.

Worked example: an infrastructure team moves to Kanban

This example is illustrative: the team, figures and results are invented to show the calculations. A six-person infrastructure team supports servers, storage, backups and internal environments. Its manager wants to know why upgrade projects keep slipping.

Step 1: measure capacity and unplanned work

Each engineer has about 32 hours a week for ticket and project work after meetings, training and leave, so team capacity is 6 × 32 = 192 hours per week. For eight weeks the team logs hours spent on incidents and unplanned requests.

Week12345678
Unplanned effort (hours)78928510188957099
Share of 192 hours40.6%47.9%44.3%52.6%45.8%49.5%36.5%51.6%

Total unplanned effort is 708 hours, so the mean is 708 ÷ 8 = 88.5 hours per week, or 88.5 ÷ 192 = 46.1% of capacity. Weekly values range from 36.5% to 52.6%.

Step 2: choose the reservation

Reserving the mean (88.5 hours) would be exceeded in four of the eight weeks. The team reserves 50% of capacity, 96 hours, which covers six of the eight weeks. That leaves 192 − 96 = 96 hours a week for planned improvement and maintenance. In weeks when unplanned work exceeds 96 hours, the policy says planned items pause rather than the team working overtime, and the manager sees the overflow on the board. Eight data points are a small sample, so the reservation is reviewed every month.

Step 3: set WIP limits and check them with Little's Law

For standard service requests, the team has on average 36 items in progress and finishes 22.5 a week. Little's Law (average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput) gives 36 ÷ 22.5 = 1.6 weeks, or 8 working days on a five-day week, which matches what requesters complain about. The team sets WIP limits that bring standard requests in progress down to about 18. If throughput stays at 22.5 a week, average cycle time becomes 18 ÷ 22.5 = 0.8 weeks, or 4 working days.

Little's Law holds for averages in a reasonably stable system: arrivals roughly equal departures and items are not abandoned. Treat the 4-day figure as an expectation to test, not a promise; if throughput falls when WIP drops, the cycle time gain will be smaller.

Step 4: pick the first automation

The team's most frequent toil is building test environments: 14 builds a month at about 3 hours each. A scripted build would take about 60 hours to create and cut each build to about 0.25 hours of checking. The monthly saving is 14 × (3 − 0.25) = 38.5 hours, so the payback period is 60 ÷ 38.5 = 1.56 months. That is fast enough to justify starting it first, using the protected 96 hours.

Step 5: interpret the results

Three months later (still illustrative), mean unplanned effort has fallen to 72 hours a week, or 72 ÷ 192 = 37.5% of capacity. The manager should not immediately cut the reservation to match. The right response is to keep it for another month to confirm the new level, then lower it in steps while watching how often it is exceeded. Over the same period the team made 80 infrastructure changes, of which 6 needed a rollback or hotfix: a change fail rate of 6 ÷ 80 = 7.5%. (DORA defines the rate per deployment; here each change is treated as one deployment.) The post-incident reviews show most failures came from manual firewall edits, which becomes the next automation candidate.

Tip

Track unplanned work in hours if you can. Item counts hide the difference between a five-minute password reset and a two-day storage failure, and capacity decisions need effort, not volume.

Metrics for Agile IT operations

Use three groups of measures and review them together. A team that improves flow but breaks more services is not improving.

GroupMeasureWhat it tells you
FlowCycle time per class of serviceHow long each type of work takes from start to finish
FlowThroughputItems finished per week, used for forecasting
FlowWork item age and WIPWhether work is getting stuck before it is late
FlowUnplanned share of effortWhether improvement work is reducing interruptions
DORA throughputChange lead time; deployment frequency; failed deployment recovery timeHow quickly and often changes reach production, and how fast you recover when a change fails
DORA instabilityChange fail rate; deployment rework rateHow often changes need immediate intervention, and how many deployments are unplanned fixes for incidents
ServiceIncident resolution time; SLA and SLO performance; customer satisfactionWhether users get the service they were promised

DORA's software delivery metrics changed recently. In 2023 the measure long known as MTTR or time to restore service was redefined as failed deployment recovery time, which covers only failures caused by a change; in 2024 deployment rework rate was added as a fifth metric. Outages with other causes, such as a hardware failure, are not part of that measure, so keep a separate incident resolution time for them. DORA treats reliability as operational performance rather than as a delivery metric.

See Kanban metrics for definitions and charts of the flow measures, and Agile metrics and KPIs for presenting measures without encouraging gaming.

Roles in an Agile operations team

Neither Agile nor Kanban prescribes operations roles, so use what the work needs.

  • Service owner or product owner: owns the order of the improvement backlog and balances requests from different stakeholders. Many ops teams lack this role, which is why improvement work loses every priority argument. The Product Owner guide describes the Scrum version.
  • Team lead or flow manager: looks after the system of work: policies, WIP limits, cadences and impediments. In Scrum this overlaps with the Scrum Master accountability.
  • Engineers: pull work, swarm on incidents, write runbooks and automation, and take part in on-call.
  • On-call or shield role: one person, rotating weekly, absorbs interruptions so that others can finish planned work. This is a common pattern, not a framework requirement.

When Agile operations practices are unsuitable or limited

These practices assume the team has some control over how it works. They are harder to apply, or need heavy adaptation, in a few situations:

  • Outsourced operations under a fixed contract, where the supplier's scope and SLAs define the work and the client cannot change WIP or priorities directly. Improvement then happens through contract and service reviews.
  • Very small teams of one or two people, where a board and reserved capacity still help but a formal cadence adds little.
  • Highly regulated change regimes, where every change requires fixed approval steps. Agile practices still improve flow before and after approval, but cannot remove the gate.
  • Teams with no reliable data, where reserved capacity and Little's Law calculations rest on guesses. Fix the measurement first.

In each case, Lean tools such as value stream mapping can still reveal where requests wait, even when the team cannot change its operating model.

Frequently asked questions

What is Agile in IT operations?

It is the use of Agile values and Kanban-style flow management by teams that run live IT services. They make all work visible, limit work in progress, reserve capacity for incidents and improve the service in small steps, while keeping service management controls.

What is AgileOps?

AgileOps is an informal label for extending Agile and DevOps ways of working into IT operations. It is not a formal framework, so there is no official AgileOps guide or certification.

Is Scrum or Kanban better for IT operations?

Kanban usually fits teams whose work is largely unplanned, because it has no fixed commitment to break and handles urgent work through explicit policies. Scrum suits teams doing mostly planned project work, often with one person rotating on support.

Can operations teams run sprints?

Yes, if most of their work is planned or if a shield role absorbs interruptions. If the Sprint Goal is displaced by incidents in most sprints, switch to Kanban or Scrumban.

How do Agile operations teams handle incidents?

Through an expedite class of service or on-call rota, with a limit on how many expedites run at once, followed by a blameless post-incident review whose actions go on the team's board.

How much capacity should an operations team reserve for unplanned work?

Measure it rather than adopt a fixed percentage. Log unplanned effort for eight weeks or more and choose a reservation that covers most weeks, not the average week, then review it monthly.

Can Agile and ITIL work together?

Yes. ITIL describes service management practices such as incident, request and change enablement; Agile changes how the team organises and improves its work within them. PeopleCert states that ITIL 4 remains available while ITIL (Version 5) is introduced in phases.

What is the difference between Agile operations and DevOps?

Agile operations applies Agile practices inside operations teams. DevOps joins development and operations into shared ownership of building, releasing and running software, with automation, shared on-call and infrastructure as code.

What metrics should an Agile operations team track?

Flow measures (cycle time per class of service, throughput, work item age, unplanned share), DORA's five delivery metrics for changes, and service measures such as incident resolution time and SLA performance.

Do Agile operations teams need a product owner?

Not formally, but someone should own the order of improvement work and balance stakeholder requests. Many teams call this person a service owner.

How long does it take to make an operations team Agile?

A board, interrupt policy and WIP limits can be in place within a month. Reliable capacity data takes about two months, and measurable reductions in unplanned work usually take several months of steady automation.
