Agile in IT Operations: How Ops Teams Apply Agile and DevOps

Agile in IT operations means applying Agile values and practices, such as visible work, short feedback loops and continuous improvement, to the teams that run infrastructure, networks, service desks and production systems. Because operations work mixes planned projects with unplanned incidents, ops teams often favour Kanban and close collaboration with development through DevOps.

Key takeaways

  • Operations teams apply Agile by making all work visible and planning for interruptions.
  • Kanban with classes of service and WIP limits usually fits ops better than strict sprints.
  • Reserve capacity for unplanned work and invest steadily in automation.
  • Close collaboration with development through DevOps multiplies the benefits.

Why operations is different

Development teams can usually plan a sprint and protect it. Operations teams face a steady stream of interruptions: incidents, service requests, security patches and urgent changes. A strict sprint commitment often breaks within days. Agile still helps, but the practices need adapting.

Work typeExampleTypical approach
UnplannedOutage, security alert, urgent fixHandled immediately through an expedite lane
Routine requestsNew user accounts, access changesStandardised and automated where possible; flow through a board
Planned improvementUpgrade a database cluster, automate backupsBroken into small items and prioritised in a backlog
MaintenancePatching, certificate renewalsScheduled as recurring work with capacity reserved

Agile practices that work for ops teams

  • Kanban boards with WIP limits: visualise all work types and limit how much is in progress; see WIP limits.
  • Classes of service: an expedite lane for incidents, a fixed-date lane for deadlines and a standard lane for everything else; see Kanban swimlanes.
  • Reserved capacity: keep a share of capacity, for example 30–40%, for unplanned work, based on your own history.
  • Short daily stand-ups at the board: focus on blocked and ageing items.
  • Regular retrospectives and post-incident reviews: blameless reviews that lead to concrete improvements.
  • Automation as backlog work: treat automating repeated tasks as planned improvement items.
  • Shared ownership with development: joint on-call, shared metrics and infrastructure as code; see DevOps.

How to introduce Agile in an operations team

  1. Make all work visible: put incidents, requests and projects on one board for two to four weeks without changing anything else.
  2. Measure the mix: count how much work is unplanned versus planned.
  3. Set classes of service and WIP limits: agree rules for what can interrupt planned work.
  4. Reserve capacity: set aside enough for the unplanned work you measured.
  5. Prioritise improvement work: create a backlog of automation and reliability items with a clear owner.
  6. Add a cadence: a weekly replenishment meeting and a fortnightly retrospective are a good start; see Kanban ceremonies.
  7. Review metrics monthly: look at cycle time, throughput and incident trends, then adapt.

Worked example

A six-person infrastructure team tracks four weeks of work and finds that 45% of completed items were unplanned. They reserve about half their capacity for unplanned work, introduce an expedite lane limited to one item at a time and set a WIP limit of eight across the board. Each week they pull the top improvement items from the backlog. After two months the team automates its three most frequent requests (password resets for a legacy system, test environment builds and log-access requests), and the share of unplanned work falls to around a third. Average cycle time for standard requests drops from eight days to four.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing two-week sprint commitments on a team that is mostly interrupt-driven.
  • Leaving unplanned work off the board, so the team looks idle when it is busy.
  • Never protecting time for improvement, so the same incidents keep happening.
  • Treating Agile and IT service management frameworks as enemies; their processes can coexist with Agile ways of working.
  • Separating development and operations so tightly that problems are thrown over the wall.

Frequently asked questions

Can IT operations teams use Agile operations practices?

Yes. They adapt it to handle unplanned work, usually with Kanban, reserved capacity and close collaboration with development.

Is Scrum or Kanban better for Agile IT operations?

Kanban usually fits better because it handles a continuous flow of interruptions. Teams with mostly planned project work can use Scrum. See Kanban vs Scrum.

How do Agile ops teams handle incidents?

Through an expedite lane or class of service that lets urgent work bypass the normal queue, with a strict limit on how many expedited items run at once.

What is the difference between Agile operations and DevOps?

Agile operations applies Agile practices inside ops teams; DevOps joins development and operations into a shared way of building, releasing and running software.

How much capacity should Agile operations teams reserve for unplanned work?

Base it on your own history. Track several weeks of work, measure the unplanned share and reserve around that amount.

Can Agile IT operations work with ITIL?

Yes. ITIL describes service management practices; Agile describes how teams work. Many organisations use both.

What metrics should an Agile ops team track?

Cycle time, throughput, planned versus unplanned work, time to restore service, change failure rate and service level performance.

Do Agile operations teams need a product owner?

Many benefit from someone who prioritises improvement work and balances requests; some call this a service owner.

How do Agile ops teams run stand-ups?

Briefly, at the board, focusing on blocked, ageing and expedited items rather than individual status updates.

What is a blameless post-incident review in Agile operations?

A review that looks at how the system and process allowed an incident to happen, rather than who caused it, and agrees improvements.
