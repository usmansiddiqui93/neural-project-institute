Agile Roadmap: Formats, Example and How to Build One

An Agile roadmap is a high-level, regularly updated plan that shows the goals and outcomes a product or team is working towards, ordered by priority over broad time horizons rather than fixed dates. This guide explains how it differs from a traditional plan and a release plan, compares the main formats, walks through a worked example and gives you nine steps to build and maintain your own.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • Last updated 4 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • An Agile roadmap communicates direction and priorities; the backlog and release plan hold the detail.
  • Good roadmaps are built around outcomes and problems to solve, not lists of features with dates.
  • Now-Next-Later, outcome-based and theme-based timelines are the most common formats.
  • Detail and confidence should fall the further out an item sits; show genuine deadlines, avoid invented ones.
  • Roadmap items become epics, epics become user stories, and stories are planned into releases and sprints.
  • Review the roadmap on a fixed cadence, usually monthly or quarterly, and after any major learning.

What is an Agile roadmap?

An Agile roadmap is a short, visual plan that shows where a product or team is heading and why. It lists the goals, problems or outcomes the team will work on, in priority order, across broad time horizons such as now, next and later or the coming quarters. It is reviewed and updated as the team learns.

The term usually means an Agile product roadmap, owned by a Product Owner or product manager. The same idea is used for Agile project roadmaps in non-software teams, and for portfolio roadmaps that combine several products. What makes any of them Agile is the approach: outcomes before outputs, honest uncertainty, and frequent revision.

Why use an Agile roadmap?

  • Alignment: everyone can see how today's work connects to strategy.
  • Better conversations: stakeholders discuss goals and trade-offs instead of arguing over feature dates.
  • Focus: a short list of priorities makes it easier to say no to everything else.
  • Honest expectations: confidence levels show what is committed and what is still being explored.
  • Context for the team: developers understand why items matter, which leads to better solutions.

Product Owners and Agile product managers typically spend much of their stakeholder time on these conversations.

Is a roadmap part of Scrum?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide defines three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Increment) and does not mention a roadmap. It does introduce the Product Goal, a long-term objective that the Product Backlog works towards, and makes the Product Owner accountable for the Product Backlog. Many Scrum Teams use a roadmap to show the sequence of goals between today and that Product Goal. See our guides to the Product Owner and the product backlog.

Where the roadmap fits: from vision to sprint

Agile planning works in levels. Each one adds detail over a shorter horizon, and the roadmap sits in the middle, linking strategy to the backlog.

LevelQuestion it answersTypical horizonUsual owner
Product visionWhy does the product exist and who is it for?Several yearsProduct leadership
Strategy and goalsWhich goals, such as OKRs, matter most now?One yearProduct leadership with the Product Owner
Agile roadmapWhich outcomes will we pursue, in what order?Six to eighteen monthsProduct Owner or product manager
Release planWhat will ship in the next few releases?One to six monthsProduct Owner with the team
Product backlogWhat exactly is next, in what order?OngoingProduct Owner
Sprint backlogWhat will we do this sprint?One to four weeksDevelopers

The roadmap answers "where and why"; the release plan answers "what and roughly when"; the backlog and sprint planning answer "exactly what next". Keeping these apart is what stops the roadmap turning into a Gantt chart.

What to include in an Agile roadmap

  • Vision or Product Goal at the top, so every item can be traced back to it.
  • Goals or outcomes, each with a measure, such as "increase trial-to-paid conversion".
  • Initiatives or themes: the problems or opportunities the team will tackle to reach each goal.
  • Time horizons: now, next and later, or quarters, with detail falling as you go further out.
  • Status or confidence for each item, so readers know what is committed and what is exploratory.
  • Key dependencies and genuine deadlines, such as regulatory dates or a trade show.
  • Owner and last-updated date, so people know whom to ask and whether it is current.

What to leave out

Individual user stories, task assignments, story point estimates and exact dates for work more than a release or two away. They belong in the backlog and release plan. A roadmap that tries to hold them becomes out of date within weeks and stops being trusted.

Types of Agile roadmap

Choose the format that fits your audience and the amount of certainty you have. Many teams keep one underlying roadmap and show it in two formats.

FormatHow it is laid outBest forWatch out for
Now-Next-LaterThree columns by priority and certainty, no datesProducts with high uncertainty; teams moving away from date-driven plansStakeholders who need dates may push back
Outcome-based (goal-oriented)Each item is a measurable goal with initiatives beneath itEmpowered teams and leadership using OKRsGoals that are too vague to measure
Theme-based timelineQuarters or months with broad themesOrganisations that need some time structure for budgeting and salesThemes quietly turning into fixed feature commitments
Feature or epic timelineEpics on a timeline, often in a toolNear-term delivery coordination for one or a few teamsFalse precision further out
Portfolio roadmapSeveral products or teams in swimlanesLeaders coordinating across teamsToo much detail for an executive audience

Now-Next-Later roadmap

Popularised by Janna Bastow, co-founder of ProdPad, the Now-Next-Later roadmap replaces dates with three columns. Now holds work in progress or about to start and is the most detailed. Next holds what comes after that, shaped but not yet refined. Later holds problems and opportunities you intend to explore, described broadly. Items move left as they become clearer and closer, and can be removed if priorities change.

Outcome-based and GO roadmaps

An outcome-based roadmap lists goals such as "reduce time to first value for new customers" instead of features. Teams are then free to find the best solution. A well-known version is Roman Pichler's goal-oriented (GO) product roadmap, which has five elements for each period: date, name, goal, features and metrics. Our OKR template pairs well with this format.

Theme-based timeline

A theme-based timeline keeps quarters or months along the top but places broad themes, such as "self-service onboarding", rather than features in each period. It gives finance and sales a sense of timing while leaving the detail open.

How to create an Agile roadmap in 9 steps

  1. Start from the vision and strategy. Write the product vision and the Product Goal in a sentence each. If leadership has set OKRs or strategic goals, list them; every roadmap item must support one.
  2. Gather inputs. Collect customer feedback, analytics, sales and support themes, technical debt, regulatory changes and stakeholder requests. Group them into problems rather than feature requests.
  3. Choose three to five goals for the coming period. Express each as an outcome with a measure and a target, such as "raise 30-day retention from 40% to 50%".
  4. Identify initiatives for each goal. For each goal, list the problems or bets that could move the measure. Keep them broad; these are candidate epics, not stories.
  5. Prioritise. Score the initiatives with a simple method such as RICE or cost of delay (see below), and agree the order with key stakeholders.
  6. Place items on time horizons. Put the top-priority, best-understood items in Now, the next ones in Next and the rest in Later. Check that Now fits the team's real capacity.
  7. Mark dependencies, deadlines and confidence. Note any genuine external dates, dependencies on other teams and a confidence level for each item.
  8. Review with the team and stakeholders. Walk through the draft with developers for feasibility and with stakeholders for value. Record what you decided not to do and why.
  9. Publish, connect and maintain. Share it in one place, link each Now item to its epic in the backlog, and set a review date.
Tip

Keep the first version small enough to fit on one screen or slide. If it does not fit, you are either including too much detail or trying to do too much at once.

Running a roadmap workshop

Steps 3 to 8 are easiest in a single half-day workshop with the Product Owner, two or three developers and the main stakeholders. An illustrative agenda:

30 minRecap vision, strategy and last period's resultsShared context and lessons

Agile roadmap example

This example is illustrative. A team of eight builds a project management app for small agencies. The vision is "help small agencies deliver client work on time without spreadsheets". Leadership's goals for the year are growth in paying customers and lower churn.

HorizonGoal and measureInitiativesConfidence
Now (this quarter)Improve trial-to-paid conversion from 8% to 11%Guided onboarding with sample project; import from spreadsheetsHigh: discovery done, epics refined
Now (this quarter)Reduce support tickets about invoicing by 30%Redesign invoice set-up; in-app helpHigh
Next (next 3-6 months)Raise 90-day retention for teams of 5+ usersClient portal for approvals; workload viewMedium: problem validated, solutions open
Next (next 3-6 months)Enter the accounting integration marketIntegration with one major accounting toolMedium: depends on partner API access
Later (6-12 months)Grow average account sizeAdvanced reporting; resource planningLow: exploring
Later (6-12 months)Meet enterprise security requirementsSingle sign-on; audit logLow: waiting on customer research

Notice what the roadmap does not show: individual stories, estimates or sprint dates. It also lists a dependency (partner API access) and is honest that Later items may change.

From roadmap item to sprint

Here is how the first Now item flows into delivery:

  1. Roadmap item: improve trial-to-paid conversion with guided onboarding.
  2. Epic: "Guided onboarding", with a hypothesis: if new users create a sample project in their first session, more will convert.
  3. User stories: for example, "As a new trial user, I want a sample client project created for me so I can see how the app works." See our guide to user stories.
  4. Release plan: the team's forecast shows the minimum version of the epic fitting in the next two sprints, with spreadsheet import following in the release after.
  5. Sprints: stories are pulled into sprint planning in priority order.
  6. Measure: after release, the Product Owner checks conversion and updates the roadmap.

How to prioritise roadmap items

Prioritisation decides what goes in Now. Use a simple, transparent method so stakeholders can see why one item beats another.

MethodHow it worksBest for
RICEReach x Impact x Confidence / EffortProduct teams comparing many ideas with some data
Cost of delay / WSJFValue lost by waiting, divided by job sizePortfolios and scaled Agile; time-sensitive work
Value vs effortPlot items on a two-by-two gridQuick workshops with stakeholders
MoSCoWMust, Should, Could, Won't haveFixed-date releases with negotiable scope

Worked example: RICE scores

Continuing the illustrative example, the team scores four candidate initiatives. Reach is users affected per quarter, impact is on a scale from 0.25 (minimal) to 3 (massive), confidence is a percentage and effort is in person-months.

InitiativeReachImpactConfidenceEffortRICE score
Guided onboarding2,000280%21,600
Spreadsheet import1,200180%1960
Client portal600250%3200
Advanced reporting300150%438

Guided onboarding and spreadsheet import go to Now; the client portal goes to Next while the team raises confidence through discovery. Treat scores as an input to a conversation, not a final answer: strategic or regulatory work may still jump the queue.

Dates, deadlines and confidence

Stakeholders will ask "when?". An Agile roadmap can answer honestly without committing to dates the team cannot know.

HorizonWhat to showConfidence
NowA target release or month, based on the team's forecastHigh
NextA quarter or a range, such as "Q2-Q3"Medium
LaterNo date, or a half-year at mostLow
  • Show genuine deadlines. Regulatory dates, contract milestones and events are real constraints. Mark them clearly as milestones.
  • Forecast from data. Base near-term dates on the team's actual throughput or velocity, and give a range.
  • Separate forecasts from commitments. Label which items are committed and which are forecasts; only a few should be commitments.
  • Update dates openly. When a forecast moves, say so and explain why. Hidden slips destroy trust faster than visible ones.

Worked example: forecasting a Now item

Continuing the illustrative example, the guided onboarding epic has been split into 24 stories. Over the last eight two-week sprints, the team finished between 6 and 9 stories per sprint, with roughly half of its capacity going to this epic and half to other work. That gives 3 to 4.5 onboarding stories per sprint, so the epic needs about 6 to 8 sprints, or 12 to 16 weeks. The roadmap therefore shows "Now: target end of Q2, high confidence for the core flow", and the Product Owner agrees with stakeholders which stories form the minimum version if the lower end of the range applies. Our guide to Agile release planning covers this kind of forecast in more detail.

Keeping the roadmap up to date

An Agile roadmap is only useful if it is current. Agree a cadence and stick to it:

  • Every sprint or fortnight: the Product Owner checks Now items against progress and moves anything finished.
  • Monthly: review Next and Later with key stakeholders; add, remove or reorder items.
  • Quarterly: revisit goals and measures, usually alongside quarterly business or OKR planning.

Also review it whenever something important changes: a major customer insight, a competitor move, a failed experiment or a change in budget. Frequent small updates are a sign of learning; a roadmap that has not changed in six months is a warning sign.

Agile roadmaps across multiple teams

When several teams work on one product or portfolio, each team keeps its own roadmap and a combined view shows how they connect.

  • Shared goals: all team roadmaps trace back to the same product or portfolio goals.
  • Dependency view: a portfolio roadmap shows where one team's Next depends on another team's Now.
  • Joint planning: teams align roadmaps at a regular event; in SAFe this is PI planning.
  • One tool: linking team epics in one tool lets the combined roadmap update itself.

Portfolio-level roadmaps are usually maintained by portfolio leaders with support from an Agile PMO. See our guide to Agile portfolio management for how priorities and funding are set at that level.

Agile roadmap checklist

Before you share a roadmap, check that it passes these tests:

  • Every item traces back to the vision or a stated goal.
  • Each goal has a measure and a target, not just a name.
  • Now fits the team's real capacity, including support and maintenance work.
  • Detail and confidence fall from Now to Later.
  • Genuine deadlines and cross-team dependencies are marked.
  • Each Now item links to an epic in the backlog.
  • It fits on one page or screen for its main audience.
  • It shows an owner, a last-updated date and the next review date.

Agile roadmap tools and templates

A slide or whiteboard is enough to start. Once the roadmap needs to stay linked to the backlog, a work management tool saves a lot of manual updating. Options from our reviews:

  • Jira includes a timeline view on every plan that shows epics and their child work, which works well as a single-team Agile roadmap. Premium and Enterprise add Plans (previously Advanced Roadmaps) for cross-team roadmaps, dependencies and capacity.
  • Wrike, our top-rated tool (9.4/10), combines Gantt and board views, request intake and cross-project dashboards, which suits roadmaps that span product, marketing and operations teams.
  • Asana links projects to goals and portfolios on its Advanced plan, a natural fit for outcome-based roadmaps tied to OKRs.
  • monday.com sells monday dev, a separate product for sprints, roadmaps and bug tracking, alongside its work management boards.
  • ClickUp and Notion are flexible options for smaller product teams: ClickUp combines sprints, goals and timelines, and Notion's timeline view is often used as a lightweight product roadmap.

Frequently asked questions

What is an Agile roadmap?

A high-level, regularly updated plan showing the goals and outcomes a product or team is working towards, in priority order over broad time horizons rather than exact dates.

What should an Agile roadmap include?

The vision or Product Goal, three to five measurable goals, the initiatives that support them, time horizons, confidence levels, genuine deadlines and dependencies, and an owner and last-updated date.

What is a Now-Next-Later roadmap?

A format with three columns: what the team is working on now, what comes next and what it plans to explore later. It replaces dates with priority and is honest about uncertainty further out.

What is an outcome-based roadmap?

A roadmap where each item is a measurable goal, such as reducing onboarding time, rather than a feature. It leaves the team free to find the best solution.

What is the difference between a roadmap and a release plan?

A roadmap shows goals and priorities over six to eighteen months. A release plan forecasts which backlog items will ship in the next few releases, usually one to six months ahead.

What is the difference between a roadmap and a product backlog?

The roadmap is a high-level view of goals and initiatives. The product backlog is the detailed, ordered list of items, such as user stories, that the team will work on.

Is a roadmap part of Scrum?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide has three artifacts and no roadmap, but it includes the Product Goal. Many Scrum Teams use a roadmap to show the steps towards that goal.

Who owns the Agile roadmap?

Usually the Product Owner or product manager, with input from leadership, customers, stakeholders and the development team.

Can an Agile roadmap have dates?

Yes, for near-term items and genuine external deadlines. Further out, use quarters, ranges or no dates at all, and label the confidence of each item.

What is a theme in an Agile roadmap?

A broad area of focus, such as self-service onboarding, that groups related initiatives. Themes give a sense of direction and timing without committing to specific features.

How far ahead should an Agile roadmap go?

Commonly six to eighteen months, with detail and confidence decreasing the further out it goes.

How often should you update an Agile roadmap?

Check Now items every sprint, review the wider roadmap monthly and revisit goals quarterly, plus after any major learning or change in strategy.

What is the difference between an Agile roadmap and a project roadmap?

A project roadmap usually shows phases and milestones for a project with defined scope and end date. An Agile roadmap focuses on outcomes for an ongoing product and changes as the team learns.

What tools can I use for an Agile roadmap?

A slide, whiteboard or spreadsheet template is enough to start. Tools such as Jira, Wrike, Asana, monday dev, ClickUp and Notion offer roadmap or timeline views linked to the backlog.
