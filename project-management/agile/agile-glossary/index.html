Agile Glossary: 120+ Agile Terms Defined A to Z

This Agile glossary defines more than 120 terms from Agile, Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming (XP), Lean and scaled Agile, in plain English and A to Z. Where it matters, each definition says where the term comes from, so you can tell official Scrum Guide language from common practice. Find a term in the letter sections, then use the commonly confused terms and the worked Sprint example to see how the vocabulary fits together.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • Last updated 3 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • Agile vocabulary comes from several sources: the Agile Manifesto, the 2020 Scrum Guide, Kanban, XP, Lean and scaling frameworks such as SAFe.
  • Story points, velocity, epics, user stories and the Definition of Ready are widely used practices, not part of the Scrum Guide.
  • Several terms have been renamed: "Development Team" is now Developers, "self-organising" is now self-managing, and since SAFe 6.0 PI means Planning Interval.
  • The most commonly confused pairs are burndown and burnup, cycle time and lead time, velocity and throughput, and the Definition of Done and acceptance criteria.
  • Agree a short team glossary, especially for "done", "ready" and when the clock starts for cycle time.

What is an Agile glossary?

An Agile glossary is a reference list of the terms used in Agile ways of working, each with a short, plain definition. It covers values and principles, frameworks such as Scrum and Kanban, roles, events, artifacts, estimation, flow metrics, engineering practices and scaling, so that everyone on a team uses the same words in the same way.

Shared vocabulary supports transparency: it breaks down quickly when one person's "done" means coded and another's means released. Official Scrum terms below follow the 2020 Scrum Guide, still the current version. For official Scrum vocabulary only, use our Scrum glossary. Agile terms A to B

A

TermDefinition
Acceptance criteriaConditions one specific backlog item must meet to be accepted. Unlike the Definition of Done, they differ for each item. See user stories.
Acceptance test-driven development (ATDD)Writing acceptance tests with business representatives before building a feature.
AgileA way of working that delivers in short cycles, seeks frequent feedback and adapts plans, guided by the Agile Manifesto.
Agile coachSomeone who helps teams and leaders adopt and improve Agile ways of working through coaching, teaching and facilitation.
Agile ManifestoFour values and twelve principles written by seventeen practitioners at Snowbird, Utah, in February 2001. See the Agile Manifesto.
Agile mindsetThe attitudes behind Agile practice: curiosity, learning from feedback, collaboration and comfort with change.
Agile Release Train (ART)In SAFe, a long-lived team of Agile teams, typically 50 to 125 people, that plans and delivers together.
Artifact (Scrum)The Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Increment. Each has a commitment that gives it focus.

B

TermDefinition
BacklogAn ordered list of work still to be done, usually the Product Backlog. Try our product backlog template.
Backlog refinementOngoing work to split, clarify, order and size backlog items for future Sprints. Formerly called grooming. See backlog refinement.
Behaviour-driven development (BDD)Describing behaviour through plain-language examples, often Given–When–Then, that become automated tests.
BlockerAnything that stops a work item progressing, such as a missing decision or another team's dependency.
Burndown chartA chart of work remaining against time, sloping down towards zero as work is finished.
Burnup chartA chart of work completed rising over time, with a separate total-scope line so scope changes stay visible.

Agile terms C

C

TermDefinition
CadenceA regular, predictable rhythm of events, such as two-week Sprints.
CapacityHow much work a team can realistically take on in a period after leave, support and other commitments.
CeremonyInformal name for a recurring Agile meeting; the Scrum Guide says event. See Agile meetings and ceremonies.
Class of serviceIn Kanban, a policy for handling a type of work, such as expedite, fixed date or standard.
Commitment (Scrum)Product Goal for the Product Backlog, Sprint Goal for the Sprint Backlog, Definition of Done for the Increment.
Continuous delivery (CD)Keeping software releasable at all times so any change can go live quickly and safely.
Continuous deploymentAutomatically releasing every change that passes the pipeline to production.
Continuous integration (CI)Merging code into a shared mainline several times a day, with automated build and tests.
Cost of delayThe value lost by delivering something later rather than sooner; used for prioritisation.
Cross-functional teamA team with all the skills needed to turn backlog items into finished work without hand-offs.
Cumulative flow diagram (CFD)A stacked area chart of items in each workflow stage over time; widening bands show bottlenecks. See the cumulative flow diagram.
Cycle timeElapsed time from starting work on an item to finishing it.

Agile terms D to E

D

TermDefinition
Daily ScrumA 15-minute event in which the Developers inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan.
Daily stand-upGeneral name for a short daily team check-in; Scrum's version is the Daily Scrum.
Definition of Done (DoD)A formal description of the quality the Increment must meet; anything short of it is not Done. See the Definition of Done.
Definition of Ready (DoR)An optional checklist for when an item is clear enough to enter a Sprint. Not part of the Scrum Guide.
Developers (Scrum)Scrum Team members who create the usable Increment each Sprint. Replaced "Development Team" in 2020.
DevOpsCulture and practices joining development and operations to deliver changes quickly and reliably.
Disciplined Agile (DA)A toolkit for choosing and tailoring your way of working, owned by PMI since 2019.
DORA metricsFive software delivery measures since 2024: change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate and deployment rework rate.
DSDMDynamic Systems Development Method: an Agile framework that fixes time and cost and varies scope. It popularised MoSCoW.

E

TermDefinition
EmpiricismDeciding based on observation. Scrum's three pillars are transparency, inspection and adaptation.
EpicA body of work too big for one Sprint, split into user stories. See epics vs stories vs tasks.
Escaped defectA defect found after work was considered Done, often by users.
EstimationForecasting the size or effort of work, usually relatively in Agile teams.
Extreme Programming (XP)Kent Beck's Agile method from the late 1990s, known for pair programming, TDD, continuous integration and refactoring.

Agile terms F to I

F

TermDefinition
FeatureFunctionality that delivers value to users. In SAFe, it sits between epic and story and fits in one Planning Interval.
Feature toggleA switch (feature flag) that turns functionality on or off without redeploying.
Fibonacci scaleStory point scale such as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20; widening gaps reflect uncertainty.
FlowThe smooth, steady movement of work from start to finish.
Flow efficiencyActive work time ÷ total elapsed time × 100. Low values mean items mostly wait.

G to H

TermDefinition
Given–When–ThenFormat for acceptance criteria: given a context, when an action happens, then an outcome follows.
Goodhart's lawUsually summarised as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure".
Hardening sprintA Sprint reserved for testing and fixing before release; usually a sign the Definition of Done is too weak.

I

TermDefinition
ImpedimentAnything slowing the team. The Scrum Master is accountable for causing impediments to be removed.
IncrementA usable stepping stone towards the Product Goal that meets the Definition of Done. A Sprint can produce several.
Information radiatorA large, visible display of team information, such as a board or chart.
INVESTBill Wake's checklist for user stories: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
IterationA fixed time-box in which a team builds and reviews working results; a Sprint in Scrum.
Iterative and incrementalImproving work through repeated cycles (iterative) and delivering it in usable pieces (incremental).

Agile terms K to M

K

TermDefinition
KaizenJapanese for "change for the better": continuous small improvements, central to Lean.
KanbanA strategy for improving flow by visualising work, limiting WIP and managing flow. David J. Anderson's Kanban Method applies it to evolutionary change. See what is Kanban.
Kanban boardA board with a column per workflow stage and a card per item. See Kanban boards.

L

TermDefinition
Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS)Framework for two to eight teams on one product with one Product Owner; LeSS Huge covers more.
Lead timeElapsed time from a request being made to its delivery. See cycle time vs lead time.
LeanThinking from the Toyota Production System focused on customer value, flow and removing waste.
Little's LawIn a stable system, average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput.

M

TermDefinition
Minimum marketable feature (MMF)The smallest piece of functionality worth releasing to all customers on its own.
Minimum viable product (MVP)The smallest version of a product that tests a key assumption with real users; popularised by Eric Ries's The Lean Startup.
Mob programmingThe whole team working on one task at one computer; also called ensemble programming.
Monte Carlo forecastingSimulating many futures from past throughput to give probabilistic forecasts, such as "85% likely within 9 weeks".
MoSCoWPrioritisation into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have (this time).

Agile terms N to P

N to O

TermDefinition
NexusScrum.org's framework for three to nine Scrum Teams on one product, with a Nexus Integration Team.
Non-functional requirementA quality such as performance, security or accessibility, often built into the Definition of Done.
OKRsObjectives and key results: a qualitative objective paired with a few measurable results.
OutcomeThe change in user behaviour or business results that work produces, as opposed to output.

P

TermDefinition
Pair programmingTwo developers working on the same code at one workstation, swapping roles often. An XP practice.
PersonaA research-based profile representing a group of users.
PI PlanningIn SAFe, a cadence-based event, typically two days, in which the whole ART plans the next Planning Interval. See PI Planning.
Planning Interval (PI)In SAFe, a time-box of typically 8 to 12 weeks. Called Program Increment before SAFe 6.0 (2023).
Planning pokerConsensus estimation: everyone reveals a card at once, then the highest and lowest explain their reasoning.
Product BacklogThe single ordered list of what is needed to improve the product; its commitment is the Product Goal.
Product GoalThe long-term objective for the Scrum Team, added in the 2020 Scrum Guide.
Product OwnerThe one person accountable for maximising product value, including ordering the Product Backlog. See Scrum team roles.
Psychological safetyA shared belief that people can raise problems and admit mistakes without fear of punishment.
Pull systemNew work starts only when there is capacity, rather than being pushed onto the team.

Agile terms R to S

R

TermDefinition
RefactoringImproving the internal structure of code without changing its behaviour.
Relative estimationSizing items by comparing them with each other rather than in hours.
ReleaseMaking an Increment available to users, at any point in a Sprint.
Release planningForecasting which features are likely to be ready for a release and when, as a range.
RetrospectiveA regular meeting to inspect how the team works and agree improvements. See the Sprint Retrospective.
RoadmapA high-level, regularly updated plan of goals and themes over time.

S

TermDefinition
Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe)A framework for applying Lean and Agile practices across many teams and the enterprise. See SAFe.
ScrumA lightweight framework in which a Scrum Team delivers value in Sprints of one month or less. See what is Scrum.
Scrum MasterAccountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness.
Scrum of ScrumsA coordination meeting for representatives of several Scrum Teams.
Scrum TeamOne Scrum Master, one Product Owner and Developers; typically 10 or fewer people.
Scrum valuesCommitment, focus, openness, respect and courage.
ScrumbanA blend of Scrum and Kanban, often Scrum's events with Kanban's WIP limits and flow metrics.
Self-managing teamA team that decides who does what, when and how. Replaced "self-organising" in the 2020 Scrum Guide.
Service level expectation (SLE)In Kanban, a forecast such as "85% of items finish within 8 days".
SpikeA time-boxed piece of research to reduce uncertainty before building. Originally an XP term.
SprintA fixed time-box of one month or less in which a Scrum Team turns ideas into value.
Sprint BacklogThe Sprint Goal, the selected Product Backlog items and the Developers' plan.
Sprint GoalThe single objective for the Sprint, created in Sprint Planning.
Sprint PlanningThe event that starts a Sprint: why it is valuable, what can be done and how.
Sprint ReviewThe event in which the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the Sprint's outcome and adapt the plan.
Sprint zeroAn informal set-up period before the first Sprint; not a Scrum term.
StakeholderAnyone with an interest in, or affected by, the product.
Story mappingArranging stories along a user journey to plan releases; associated with Jeff Patton.
Story pointsRelative units for sizing backlog items. See story points.
Sustainable paceA rhythm the team can keep up indefinitely; one of the Manifesto principles.
SwarmingSeveral team members working together to finish one item.
SwimlaneA horizontal band on a board separating types of work.

Agile terms T to Z

T

TermDefinition
T-shaped skillsDeep expertise in one area plus working knowledge of others.
T-shirt sizingRough relative sizing in XS, S, M, L and XL, useful for epics.
TaskA technical step needed to complete a user story.
Technical debtThe future cost of shortcuts, a metaphor coined by Ward Cunningham. See technical debt in Scrum.
Test-driven development (TDD)Write a failing test, write code to pass it, then refactor. An XP practice.
ThemeA broad area of focus that groups several epics.
Three AmigosBusiness, development and testing perspectives agreeing what a story means before work starts.
Three CsRon Jeffries' view of a story: Card, Conversation and Confirmation.
ThroughputThe number of items finished per period; needs no estimates.
Time-boxA fixed maximum duration, such as the 15-minute Daily Scrum.
Trunk-based developmentIntegrating small changes into one main branch at least daily.

U to V

TermDefinition
User storyA need described from the user's view: "As a [user], I want [goal] so that [benefit]". See our user story template.
Value streamAll the steps, including waiting, that deliver value from request to customer.
VelocityStory points finished to the Definition of Done per Sprint, for the team's own forecasting. See the velocity chart.
Vertical sliceAn item that cuts through every layer to deliver a small piece of working functionality.

W to Z

TermDefinition
WaterfallA sequential, plan-driven approach; also called predictive.
Weighted shortest job first (WSJF)In SAFe, cost of delay ÷ job size, so short, valuable items rise to the top.
Work in progress (WIP)Items started but not finished.
WIP limitA cap on items in a stage or the whole system. See WIP limits.
Work item ageHow long an unfinished item has been in progress; a Kanban Guide flow metric.
Working agreementRules a team agrees for how it works together.

Tip

If a term is not in the Scrum Guide, your team is free to use, adapt or drop it. Story points and the Definition of Ready help many teams, but nobody is doing Scrum "wrong" without them.

Commonly confused Agile terms

These pairs cause the most misunderstandings. The numbers are illustrative.

Burndown vs burnup

A release starts at 100 points. After two Sprints, 40 are finished, then 20 points of new scope are added. The burndown jumps from 60 remaining to 80, as if the team went backwards. The burnup shows completed work still at 40 and the scope line stepping up from 100 to 120.

Cycle time vs lead time

A request is logged on 1 March, started on 10 March and delivered on 14 March. Lead time is 13 days; cycle time is 4 days. The customer feels the lead time; the team mostly controls the cycle time.

Velocity vs throughput

A team finishes six items sized 3, 5, 2, 8, 3 and 5 points. Velocity is 26 points; throughput is 6 items. Both can drive forecasts, but throughput needs no estimating.

Done, acceptance criteria and ready

TermApplies toExampleIn the Scrum Guide?
Definition of DoneEvery itemCode reviewed, tests automated, deployed to stagingYes
Acceptance criteriaOne itemA user can cancel up to 24 hours before the appointmentNo
Definition of ReadyItems before Sprint PlanningHas acceptance criteria and fits in one SprintNo

Epic vs user story vs task

The epic "Online appointment booking" contains the story "As a patient, I want to cancel my booking online so that I do not have to phone", which contains tasks such as "Add cancel button" and "Send cancellation email".

Worked example: Agile terms in a two-week Sprint

Follow one illustrative piece of work through a Sprint for a six-person Scrum Team building an appointment booking service.

  1. Goal. The roadmap shows a self-service theme; the Product Owner sets the Product Goal: patients manage their own appointments online.
  2. Refinement. The epic is split into user stories that meet INVEST, with acceptance criteria agreed in a Three Amigos conversation.
  3. Estimation. The Developers use planning poker. One story has too many unknowns, so they add a two-day spike.
  4. Sprint Planning. Using a recent velocity of about 24 points and reduced capacity, the team agrees the Sprint Goal, "Patients can cancel a booking online", and forms the Sprint Backlog.
  5. During the Sprint. A WIP limit of four applies on the Kanban board. At the Daily Scrum, one item's work item age is unusually high, so two Developers swarm on it. The Scrum Master helps remove an impediment.
  6. Done. Stories meet the Definition of Done, including TDD and continuous integration, and form a usable Increment released behind a feature toggle.
  7. Review and Retrospective. At the Sprint Review, stakeholder feedback adds items to the Product Backlog. At the Retrospective, the team agrees to tackle technical debt in the booking module.

How to create an Agile glossary for your team

  1. Collect the terms people use, including local jargon and abbreviations from your board and meetings.
  2. Choose sources of truth, such as the 2020 Scrum Guide and the Kanban Guide, so debates have a reference point.
  3. Agree high-risk definitions first: what "done" and "ready" mean, and when the clock starts for cycle time.
  4. Mark local terms so nobody mistakes team conventions for framework rules.
  5. Publish it beside your working agreement and link it from onboarding notes.
  6. Review it at a Retrospective every few months, or whenever a misunderstanding causes rework.

Common mistakes with Agile terminology

  • Using retired terms such as "Development Team", "grooming" or "self-organising".
  • Treating practices as rules. Story points, velocity and the Definition of Ready are optional.
  • Calling any deadline a sprint. A Sprint has a goal, a plan and a usable Increment, not just a rush.
  • Comparing velocity between teams. Points are relative to each team; see Agile metrics and KPIs.
  • Mixing up done and released. Work can be Done without being released yet.

Frequently asked questions

What are the basic Agile terms every beginner should know?

Start with Agile, Sprint, Product Backlog, user story, Definition of Done, Daily Scrum, retrospective, Kanban board, WIP limit and velocity.

What is the difference between Agile and Scrum?

Agile is a broad approach defined by the Agile Manifesto. Scrum is one specific framework that applies it, with defined accountabilities, events and artifacts.

What is a sprint in Agile?

A fixed time-box of one month or less, often two weeks, in which a Scrum Team works towards a Sprint Goal and produces at least one usable Increment.

What is the difference between an epic, a user story and a task?

An epic is a large body of work, a user story is a slice of it finishable in one Sprint, and a task is a technical step towards a story.

What does WIP mean in Agile?

Work in progress: items started but not finished. WIP limits reduce multitasking and shorten cycle time.

What is velocity in Agile?

The story points a team finishes to the Definition of Done per Sprint. Use it for the team's own forecasts, never to compare teams.

What is a spike in Agile?

A short, time-boxed piece of research or prototyping that answers a question before the team commits to building something.

What is the difference between a burndown and a burnup chart?

A burndown shows work remaining. A burnup shows work completed against a separate scope line, so scope changes are visible.

Are story points part of Scrum?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide mentions neither story points nor velocity; they are common practices.

What is the Definition of Ready?

An optional checklist for when a backlog item is clear and small enough to enter a Sprint. It is not part of the Scrum Guide.

What does MVP mean in Agile?

Minimum viable product: the smallest version of a product that lets you test a key assumption with real users.

Is "ceremony" an official Scrum term?

No. The Scrum Guide calls the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective events.
