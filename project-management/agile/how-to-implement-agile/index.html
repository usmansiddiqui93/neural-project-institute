How to Implement Agile: A 10-Step Plan for Organisations

To implement Agile, define the problem it should solve, secure real leadership sponsorship, start with one or two willing pilot teams, change the governance around them, measure against a baseline and then spread the approach team by team. This guide gives you a 10-step plan for adopting Agile across a department or organisation, with a readiness check, a pilot scoring matrix and an illustrative 90-day example. To run a single project the Agile way, use our companion guide instead.

  • Last updated 3 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • Implementing Agile is an organisational change, not a training course. The hard part is changing how leaders set priorities, fund work and measure progress.
  • Start with a clear problem and a measured baseline so you can tell whether Agile has helped.
  • Choose one or two pilot teams with explicit criteria: willing people, an available Product Owner, visible value and few dependencies.
  • Start with one framework as written, usually Scrum or Kanban, and adapt it once the team understands why each part exists.
  • Adapt governance, funding and reporting around the pilots, or teams will run Agile meetings inside an unchanged system.
  • Judge pilots after five or six iterations, then scale in waves, using pilot members as coaches.

What does it mean to implement Agile?

Implementing Agile means changing how a team, department or organisation plans, delivers and improves its work so that it follows Agile values and principles in practice. Teams deliver in short cycles, one person owns priorities, work is visible and decisions move closer to the people doing it. It changes roles, governance and leadership behaviour, not only meetings.

People use the phrase for three different jobs, and mixing them up is a common reason plans go wrong.

Running an Agile projectImplementing Agile (this guide)Agile transformation
ScopeOne project or productSeveral teams, a department or the organisationThe whole organisation and its operating model
Led byProduct Owner, Scrum Master or Agile project managerSponsor, change lead and coachesExecutive team
What changesHow this project is planned, delivered and reportedTeam practices, roles, training, local governance and metricsStructure, funding, HR, portfolio management and culture
Typical timescaleThe life of the projectMonths for the first teams, longer to spreadTypically several years

Most organisations begin with the middle column: get a few teams working well, prove the value, then decide how far to go. Read how to run an Agile project if you only need to run one project.

Agile readiness check

Before you commit to a timeline, test how ready the organisation is. Answer each question honestly with the people involved. Warning signs do not mean you should not start; they tell you what to fix first.

QuestionReady signalWarning signal
Can you name the problem Agile should solve?A specific problem with a measure, such as lead time"Everyone else is doing it"
Will a senior sponsor give time, not just approval?Sponsor attends reviews and removes blockersSponsor delegates everything
Can someone own priorities with real authority?A named person can say yes and no to scopeEvery priority goes to a committee
Can teams stay stable for six months or more?People mostly work on one teamPeople are split across four or five projects
Can work be delivered in small pieces?Increments can be released or demonstratedNothing is usable until everything is finished
Will governance accept a forecast range?Stage gates can be adapted for a pilotFixed scope, date and cost are non-negotiable
Is there budget for training and coaching?Training and a coach are funded"Read a book and start on Monday"

If you have three or more warning signals, spend extra time on steps 2 and 8 before choosing a pilot. A gap analysis template is a simple way to record the current state, the target state and the actions in between.

The 10-step Agile implementation plan at a glance

The steps overlap in practice, but this order keeps the most common failures, such as starting without a sponsor or judging results too early, out of your way.

StepMain outputTypical timing
1. Define the problem and success measuresProblem statement and baselineWeeks 1–2
2. Secure sponsorshipNamed sponsor and leadership commitmentsWeeks 1–3
3. Choose the pilot teamsOne or two pilots chosen with explicit criteriaWeeks 2–4
4. Choose a starting frameworkScrum, Kanban or Scrumban for each pilotWeek 3
5. Map roles and form teamsProduct Owner, Scrum Master or flow lead, teamWeeks 3–5
6. Train and coachTraining delivered; coach in placeWeeks 4–6
7. Set up the team basicsBacklog, board, Definition of Done, cadenceWeek 6

Timings are illustrative for a department of 50–100 people; regulated organisations usually need longer for steps 2 and 8.

Step 1: Define the problem and how you will measure success

Agile is a means, not a goal. Write down the specific problem it should solve and how you will know it has improved. A clear reason guides later decisions, from pilot choice to metrics, and gives sceptics something concrete to test.

  • Weak: "We want to be more Agile."
  • Better: "Requests take too long to reach customers."
  • Best: "The median time from an approved request to release is about three months, and stakeholders only see results at the end. We want to release at least monthly and get feedback every two weeks."

Then capture a baseline before anything changes. The figures below are illustrative; use measures that match your problem.

ProblemBaseline measureIllustrative baselineTarget after 6 months
Slow deliveryMedian lead time from approval to release90 daysUnder 30 days
Infrequent feedbackTimes per quarter users see working output16 or more
Unclear prioritiesItems in progress per team145 or fewer
Quality problemsDefects found after release per month20Under 10

A problem statement template helps you agree the wording with leaders.

Step 2: Secure sponsorship and agree what leaders will change

Agile asks leaders to accept flexible scope, stable teams and decisions made closer to the work. Without that, teams run stand-ups and retrospectives inside a system that still demands fixed plans, moves people between projects and escalates every decision. The sponsor's job is to change that system, starting with the pilot.

Brief leaders on what will change for them, not just for teams, and agree the commitments in writing.

Today (typical)What leaders agree to during the pilot
Approve a detailed plan up front, then track varianceApprove a goal, budget and time window; review working results every iteration
Assign people to several projects at onceKeep pilot team members on one team for at least six months
Escalate priority decisions to a committeeGive the Product Owner authority to order the backlog
Ask for percentage-complete status reportsAttend Sprint Reviews and accept burnup charts and forecast ranges
Add urgent work directly to individualsRoute new requests through the Product Owner

Also name a change lead who plans the rollout, arranges training, removes organisational blockers and reports to the sponsor. In a large organisation this is usually a dedicated role supported by coaches.

Tip

Ask the sponsor to attend the first three Sprint Reviews of each pilot team. Nothing signals more clearly that the change is real, and it shows leaders what Agile progress looks like.

Step 3: Choose the pilot teams

Start with one or two teams, not the whole department. A pilot lets you learn in a contained way, build internal examples and find organisational blockers before they affect everyone.

What makes a good pilot

  • Meaningful work that stakeholders care about, so success is visible.
  • Willing volunteers, not reluctant conscripts.
  • An available Product Owner with authority to make priority decisions.
  • Few dependencies on other teams or suppliers.
  • Not the most critical, highest-pressure project. An immovable deadline leaves no room to learn.
  • Results visible within about three months.

Worked example: a pilot scoring matrix

Score each candidate from 1 (poor) to 5 (strong) against weighted criteria, multiply and add. The candidates and scores are illustrative.

Criterion (weight)A: Customer portalB: Core platform migrationC: Internal reportsD: Partner onboarding
Visible value (3)4524

Product Owner availability (3)4325
Independence from other teams (2)4153

For candidate A: (4×3) + (5×3) + (4×3) + (4×2) + (4×2) + (4×2) = 12 + 15 + 12 + 8 + 8 + 8 = 63.

A and D become the pilots. The core platform migration (B) has the most value but scores badly on willingness, dependencies and deadline pressure, so it waits for a later wave. Internal reports (C) is easy but would convince few people.

Step 4: Choose a starting framework

Start with one framework as written. Each part of Scrum or Kanban exists for a reason, and teams that customise on day one usually remove the parts that would have exposed their problems. Adapt it through retrospectives once the team understands why each element exists. Our guide to types of Agile methodologies covers the wider options.

ScrumKanbanScrumban
Best forPlanned product or project work delivered in incrementsContinuous requests, support and operationsMixed planned and reactive work
CadenceSprints of one month or less, usually two weeksContinuous flow with regular replenishmentPlanning cadence with flow inside it
RolesProduct Owner, Scrum Master, DevelopersNone required; many add a flow leadUsually a Product Owner and facilitator
Size of changeLarger: new roles and eventsSmaller: improve the current processMedium

Choose Scrum when the team builds something new and needs a rhythm of planning and feedback; choose Kanban when work arrives unpredictably or the team wants less disruption. Non-software teams, such as marketing or operations, often start with Kanban. See Kanban vs Scrum and what is Scrum for the framework as defined in the 2020 Scrum Guide.

Step 5: Map roles and form the teams

Agile teams are small, cross-functional and stable. The Scrum Guide describes a Scrum Team of 10 or fewer people with all the skills needed to create value each Sprint. Forming those teams changes some existing roles, and people will want to know early what happens to theirs.

Existing roleCommon Agile destinationWhat changes
Project managerScrum Master, Product Owner or Agile project managerFrom directing tasks to enabling teams and managing stakeholders, budget and cross-team risk
Business analystTeam member supporting the Product OwnerRequirements refined continuously as user stories
Product or service managerProduct OwnerOwns and orders the backlog; available every Sprint
Team leaderDeveloper on the team, or Scrum MasterThe team plans its own work
Line managerPeople and capability managerHiring, development and removing blockers, not daily task allocation
Steering committeeSprint Review attendees and quarterly portfolio reviewDecisions based on working results and forecasts
PMOAgile PMOFrom enforcing stage gates to supporting cadence, metrics and portfolio flow

The three roles to fill first

  • Product Owner: the most important appointment. Choose someone with authority over priorities and time to work with the team every week.
  • Scrum Master or flow lead: coaches the team on the framework and removes impediments. See the Scrum Master role.
  • Team members: a stable group with the skills to finish work without waiting on others.
Tip

Tell people about role changes before training starts. Uncertainty about their own job is the most common reason capable people resist a rollout.

Step 6: Train and coach

Train the team, the Product Owner and their managers together where you can, so everyone shares the same vocabulary and expectations. Then support teams with coaching, because most learning happens during the first iterations, not in the classroom.

AudienceWhat they needFormat (illustrative)
Sponsor and senior leadersWhat changes for them; how to read Agile progressHalf-day workshop
Pilot teamsValues, principles, the chosen framework, estimation, the boardTwo days, with the Product Owner
Product OwnersBacklog ordering, slicing stories, stakeholder managementTwo days plus mentoring
Scrum Masters or flow leadsFacilitation, coaching, metrics, removing impedimentsTwo days plus mentoring
Line managers and PMOTheir new role and the governance changesHalf-day workshop

Consider an experienced Scrum Master or Agile coach for the first few months to speed up learning, spot bad habits early and train internal people to take over. Record the plan in a training plan template.

Step 7: Set up the team basics

Before the first iteration, each pilot team needs a small set of basics. Keep them simple; the team will improve them through retrospectives.

  1. A single, ordered backlog owned by the Product Owner.
  2. A visible board showing work from to-do to done. A Kanban board template is enough to start.
  3. A Definition of Done so everyone agrees what finished means.
  4. A fixed cadence for planning, daily coordination, reviews and retrospectives.
  5. A team working agreement covering hours, channels and how decisions are made.
  6. A short list of metrics linked to the problem from step 1.

The day-to-day detail of running the work, including kickoff, the first backlog, release forecasts and the sprint cycle, is covered in our guide to running an Agile project. This guide stays focused on what the organisation must put in place around the teams.

Step 8: Adapt governance, funding and reporting

This is the step most implementations skip, and the main reason pilots stall. If the pilot team still has to pass the same stage gates, produce the same documents and report percentage complete, it spends its energy satisfying two systems at once. Agree temporary exceptions for the pilots, then make the successful ones permanent.

Traditional controlAgile equivalent for the pilot
Detailed requirements sign-off before work startsAgreed product vision, initial backlog and Definition of Done
Stage-gate approval at each phaseSprint Review every iteration plus a quarterly continue or stop decision
Project budget for fixed scopeFunding for a stable team for a time window, scope ordered by value
Change control board for scope changesProduct Owner reorders the backlog; large changes discussed at Sprint Review
Percentage-complete status reportBurnup chart, forecast range, demo and top risks
Individual utilisation targetsTeam-level outcomes and flow measures

Keep the controls that matter, such as financial approval limits, regulatory requirements and security reviews, but build them into the Definition of Done or the team's cadence rather than adding them at the end. Our guide to Agile portfolio management shows how funding and prioritisation work once Agile spreads beyond the pilots.

Step 9: Run, measure and improve

Run at least five or six iterations before judging results. The first two are usually messy as the team learns the framework, estimates poorly and discovers hidden dependencies. Use retrospectives to improve each time, and keep tracking the measures from step 1.

If the problem was…TrackAvoid
Slow deliveryLead time, cycle time, release frequencyComparing velocity between teams
Unclear prioritiesWork in progress, share of work that was plannedCounting tickets closed
Poor qualityEscaped defects, reworkHours spent testing
Low stakeholder confidenceSprint Goal success rate, forecast accuracyCeremony attendance

Questions for the 90-day review

  • Has the baseline measure moved in the right direction, and by how much?
  • Which organisational blockers did the pilot hit, and which were removed?
  • Did leaders keep their commitments from step 2?
  • Would the pilot team members choose to keep working this way?
  • What would we change before the next teams start?

Step 10: Scale team by team

Once the pilots show results, expand in waves rather than switching everyone at once. Each wave should be small enough for your coaches to support properly.

  1. Share the pilot results openly, including what did not work. Honest results convince more people than a polished success story.
  2. Plan waves of two to four teams, starting with teams whose work is similar to the pilots.
  3. Seed pilot team members into new teams so experience spreads.
  4. Set up a community of practice where Scrum Masters, Product Owners and team members share problems and solutions.
  5. Fix the shared blockers every team hits, such as release approvals, procurement and HR policies.
  6. Add a scaling approach only when teams must coordinate closely on one product, for example a Scrum of Scrums or the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe).

When the change starts to involve funding models, organisational structure and HR policy everywhere, you have moved from implementation into transformation, which our Agile transformation roadmap covers.

Worked example: a 90-day Agile implementation

This example is illustrative. The organisation, figures and results are invented to show how the steps fit together.

A mid-sized insurer's claims systems department has about 70 people working on 12 projects at once. Approved changes take around 90 days to reach users, releases are quarterly, and stakeholders only see results when it is too late to change them. The head of department wants monthly releases and earlier feedback.

WeeksActivityOutput
1–2Baseline measured: 90-day median lead time, quarterly releases, 14 items in progress per teamProblem statement and baseline
1–3Claims director named as sponsor; leadership commitments agreedCommitments signed; change lead appointed
2–4Four candidates scored; customer portal (63) and partner onboarding (56) chosenTwo pilots
3–5Both choose Scrum with two-week Sprints; two project managers become Scrum Masters, two service managers become Product OwnersTeams of 7 and 8
4–6Team training, leaders' workshop, external coach three days a weekTraining complete
4–8Stage gates replaced by Sprint Reviews and a monthly sponsor review; status reports replaced by burnup chartsGovernance exceptions
6–18Six Sprints per team; coach steps back from Sprint 4Working increments every Sprint
1390-day review with sponsor, teams and stakeholdersDecision to expand

What happened

  • Sprints 1 and 2 were rough: both teams over-committed, and the partner onboarding team found a dependency on a supplier's API.
  • By Sprint 4 the customer portal team was releasing every two weeks, and median lead time for its changes had fallen to about 25 days.
  • The biggest blocker was the monthly change advisory board. The sponsor agreed a standard-change route for low-risk releases.

At day 90 the department agreed a second wave of three teams, each seeded with a pilot team member, and asked the change lead to fix release approvals for every team before wave three. The core platform migration stayed on its existing plan until the organisation had more experience.

Managing the people side of the change

Most resistance to Agile is reasonable from the person's point of view. Treat it as information about what the plan has missed.

What you hearWhat it often meansWhat helps
"We tried Agile before and it failed."A previous rollout changed meetings but not the systemAsk what went wrong; show which leadership commitments are different
"What happens to my job?"Role uncertaintyPublish the role mapping early; talk to each affected person
"We can't commit without a full plan."Governance or contract pressureShow forecast ranges and burnup charts; agree governance exceptions
"Our work is too regulated for Agile."Fear of losing controlBuild controls into the Definition of Done; start with a lower-risk pilot

Communicate the plan, the reasons and the results regularly. A communication plan template helps you decide who hears what and when.

Common mistakes when implementing Agile

  • Mandating Agile without explaining why. People attend the meetings and keep the old behaviour.
  • Renaming roles without changing them. A Scrum Master who still assigns tasks changes nothing.
  • Leaving governance untouched. Teams produce Agile and traditional reporting at the same time.
  • Choosing the most critical project as the pilot. Pressure removes the room to learn.
  • Customising the framework on day one. Teams drop the parts that would expose their problems.
  • Splitting people across many projects. It defeats stable teams and short cycles.
  • Measuring velocity or attendance instead of outcomes. Measure the problem you set out to solve.
  • Judging after one or two iterations, or scaling before the pilots work. Both spread problems rather than benefits.

Tools that support an Agile rollout

Tools do not make an organisation Agile, but the right one makes work visible across teams and saves the change lead from building reports by hand. Start with the simplest tool that gives each team a board and backlog and gives leaders a shared view of progress.

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, suits organisations rolling Agile out across mixed teams and portfolios, with boards, request forms, dashboards and cross-project reporting in one place.
  • Jira offers mature Scrum and Kanban boards and is a strong choice for software development teams.
  • monday.com and Asana are easy for non-technical teams to adopt, which helps when rolling out beyond IT.

Frequently asked questions

What are the steps to implement Agile?

Define the problem and success measures, secure sponsorship, choose pilot teams, choose a starting framework, map roles and form teams, train and coach, set up the team basics, adapt governance and reporting, run and measure five or six iterations, then scale team by team.

How long does it take to implement Agile?

A pilot team can work well within about three months. Spreading Agile across a department usually takes many months, and changing an organisation's structure, funding and culture typically takes years.

Do we need an Agile coach to implement Agile?

Not always, but an experienced coach or Scrum Master speeds up learning and helps avoid common mistakes. Many organisations use a coach for the first few months and train internal people to take over.

Should we start with Scrum or Kanban?

Choose Scrum for planned work that can be delivered in increments, and Kanban for continuous requests or teams that want to evolve their current process with less disruption.

How do you choose a pilot team for Agile?

Look for meaningful work, willing volunteers, an available Product Owner, few dependencies and results visible within about three months. Avoid the highest-pressure project. A weighted scoring matrix makes the choice transparent.

What is the biggest obstacle to implementing Agile?

Usually the organisation around the teams: fixed-scope approvals, people split across many projects, stage gates that do not fit iterative delivery, and leaders who keep decisions away from the team.

What happens to project managers when an organisation implements Agile?

Many become Scrum Masters, Product Owners or Agile project managers who coordinate several teams and manage stakeholders, budgets and risks. The role shifts from directing tasks to enabling teams.

How do you measure whether an Agile implementation is working?

Compare against the baseline captured at the start, using measures linked to the original problem, such as lead time, release frequency, escaped defects or stakeholder satisfaction.

What is the difference between implementing Agile and an Agile transformation?

Implementing Agile usually means adopting Agile practices in a set of teams or a department. A transformation changes the whole organisation's structure, funding, governance and culture.

Can you implement Agile in a non-software team?

Yes. Marketing, HR, operations and construction teams use Agile, often starting with Kanban. The steps are the same; what counts as a finished increment changes.

Can you implement Agile without changing the organisation's structure?

You can start without restructuring, which is why pilots work. But if governance, funding and reporting never change, teams eventually hit limits, so plan to adapt them as the pilots show what gets in the way.
