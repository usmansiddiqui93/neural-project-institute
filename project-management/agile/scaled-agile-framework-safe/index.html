What Is SAFe? The Scaled Agile Framework Explained

The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) is a knowledge base published by Scaled Agile, Inc. for running Agile and Lean practices across many teams, from groups of Agile teams called Agile Release Trains up to portfolio strategy and funding. Since 2026 the framework site offers two operating models: Core SAFe, whose Big Picture is still labelled SAFe 6.0, and the newer AI-Native SAFe. This guide explains how SAFe is structured, which configuration fits which organisation, how its cadence works, and where its critics have a point.

Key takeaways

  • SAFe coordinates many Agile teams through long-lived Agile Release Trains of about 50 to 125 people that plan together every Planning Interval of typically 8 to 12 weeks.
  • Core SAFe (Big Picture 6.0) remains the established model; AI-Native SAFe, announced in June 2026 and presented in full at the SAFe Summit in September 2026, sits alongside it rather than replacing it.
  • Four core values and ten Lean-Agile principles underpin the framework; the knowledge base now groups its competencies into five disciplines.
  • Choose a configuration by structure: Essential for one or a few trains, Large Solution when one solution needs several trains, Portfolio when strategy and funding must connect to delivery, Full for all of these.
  • SAFe adds roles, events and planning layers. That structure helps large organisations align, but it costs time and can preserve old hierarchies if adopted mechanically.
  • If one or two teams can deliver the product, a scaling framework is probably unnecessary; lighter options such as LeSS or Scrum@Scale also exist.

What is the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe)?

The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) is a set of roles, events, practices and principles for applying Agile and Lean ways of working across an enterprise. It organises Agile teams into long-lived Agile Release Trains that plan and deliver on a shared cadence, and it connects those trains to portfolio strategy, funding and governance.

SAFe was created by Dean Leffingwell, first released in 2011 and is maintained by Scaled Agile, Inc., which publishes the framework articles at framework.scaledagile.com and runs the associated training and certification. It draws on Agile development, Lean product development, systems thinking and DevOps. Its teams use Scrum or Kanban, which SAFe calls SAFe Scrum and SAFe Team Kanban.

SAFe is a framework, not a methodology you execute line by line. Most organisations adopt part of it, and the framework itself is designed to be configured. That flexibility matters for the debate about SAFe: many problems attributed to it come from how it was implemented, though critics argue that its size makes heavy implementations likely.

Which version of SAFe is current?

This question has become less straightforward. As of November 2026, Scaled Agile's framework site presents two operating models side by side:

Operating modelStatusWhat it contains
Core SAFeThe established model; Big Picture posters still labelled SAFe 6.0 (released March 2023)The four configurations, Agile Release Trains, two-day PI planning, Inspect and Adapt, Lean Portfolio Management
AI-Native SAFeUnveiled in June 2026 (Scaled Agile's release announcement is dated 23 June); presented in full in the keynote at the SAFe Summit, San Diego, 14–18 September 2026Smaller AI-augmented teams, AI-Native ARTs, a new AI Value Architect role, AI governance and ethics, and redesigned ART events

Scaled Agile states that AI-Native SAFe does not replace Core SAFe, and that it will continue to support and evolve Core SAFe. Because AI-Native SAFe is so new, existing SAFe implementations, role certifications such as SAFe Agilist and most tool templates are built around Core SAFe, so the rest of this guide describes Core SAFe unless it says otherwise.

What AI-Native SAFe changes

AI-Native SAFe redesigns teams and events for organisations where AI agents do a substantial share of the work. Its framework articles describe AI-Native Teams of typically three to seven people combining product, builder, domain expert and AI capabilities, with humans reviewing AI output and remaining accountable. At train level, the two-day PI Planning is replaced by a one-day PI Outcome Planning event, and a two-hour Sense and Respond event held at least once per iteration replaces Inspect and Adapt and absorbs System Demo activities. A dedicated AI Value Architect role helps teams adopt AI responsibly and link it to business outcomes. The model is new, so expect its guidance to change and evidence from real adoptions to be thin for some time.

SAFe core values and Lean-Agile principles

SAFe's four core values describe the behaviour the framework expects from leaders and teams:

  • Alignment: everyone understands the strategy and how their work contributes to it.
  • Transparency: plans, progress, problems and decisions are visible, which requires trust.
  • Respect for people: the people doing the work are trusted, developed and listened to.
  • Relentless improvement: the organisation keeps learning, through events such as Inspect and Adapt.

Beneath the values sit ten Lean-Agile principles. Scaled Agile calls them immutable; the practices are meant to be adapted, the principles are not.

#PrincipleWhat it means in practice
1Take an economic viewDecide using cost of delay and economics, not only dates and utilisation
2Apply systems thinkingOptimise the whole value stream, not one team or department
3Assume variability; preserve optionsKeep design options open until learning reduces uncertainty
4Build incrementally with fast, integrated learning cyclesIntegrate frequently so that feedback arrives early
5Base milestones on objective evaluation of working systemsJudge progress by working solutions, not documents
6Make value flow without interruptionsLimit WIP, reduce batch sizes and queues
7Apply cadence, synchronize with cross-domain planningPlan together on a fixed rhythm
8Unlock the intrinsic motivation of knowledge workersAutonomy, mastery and purpose rather than individual incentives
9Decentralize decision-makingCentralise only strategic, infrequent decisions
10Organize around valueBuild teams and trains around value streams, not functions

Principle 10 is the one most often skipped. Renaming departments as trains without reorganising around a flow of value leaves the hand-offs that SAFe is meant to remove.

How SAFe is organised: five disciplines

Earlier descriptions of SAFe, including many training materials, list seven core competencies. Scaled Agile's knowledge base now groups its guidance into five disciplines, each containing several competencies:

If you are studying for an exam, check which model your course material uses. The underlying ideas map across, but question wording may follow the version the course was written for.

How SAFe works, level by level

SAFe stacks structures so that the same rhythm of planning, delivery and review runs from teams to the portfolio.

Team level

Agile teams of ten or fewer people, each with a Product Owner and a Scrum Master/Team Coach, plan and deliver in iterations, usually of two weeks. They use Scrum or Kanban practices and share a Definition of Done with the other teams on their train.

Agile Release Train (ART)

An ART is a long-lived team of Agile teams, generally 50 to 125 people, organised around a value stream and able to define, build, test and release its solution. All teams on a train share one ART backlog, one calendar and one set of events, led by the Release Train Engineer, Product Management, the System Architect and Business Owners. The ART is the core building block of every configuration.

Solution Train

When a single solution, such as an aircraft system or a national tax platform, is too large for one ART, several trains and suppliers coordinate through a Solution Train, led by a Solution Train Engineer, Solution Management and a Solution Architect. Trains on a Solution Train align their Planning Intervals and add solution-level events such as Pre-Plan and Coordinate and Deliver.

Portfolio

A portfolio contains one or more value streams. Lean Portfolio Management connects strategy to execution through value stream budgets with guardrails, a portfolio Kanban for large initiatives called epics, and Lean governance. Roles include Epic Owners, Enterprise Architects and the Value Management Office. Our Agile portfolio management guide works through WSJF prioritisation and Lean budgeting in detail, and Agile epics explains how epics are written and split.

The four configurations and how to choose

Core SAFe's Big Picture comes in four configurations. Each adds structure to the one before, so start with the smallest that fits.

ConfigurationAddsChoose it whenAvoid it when
Essential SAFeTeams and ARTs with their roles, events and artefactsOne or a few trains each deliver a largely independent solutionOne or two teams can deliver the product without a train
Large Solution SAFeSolution Train, supplier coordination, solution roles and eventsOne very large solution needs several ARTs and suppliers to integrateTrains deliver separate solutions with few integration points
Portfolio SAFeLean Portfolio Management, value stream budgets, portfolio KanbanSeveral value streams compete for funding and strategy must steer themFunding and strategy are already set elsewhere and cannot change
Full SAFeEverything aboveA very large enterprise has multiple large solutions plus portfolio governanceA smaller configuration covers your actual problems
Tip

Many organisations start with Essential SAFe on one train and add the portfolio layer after two or three Planning Intervals, once the first train has shown where funding and prioritisation get in its way.

None of these roles is a traditional project manager. Former project and programme managers often become RTEs or work in the VMO, but the RTE coaches and facilitates rather than owning scope or assigning tasks. The Agile Release Train guide describes the train-level roles in depth.

The SAFe cadence: iterations, Planning Intervals and events

A Planning Interval (PI) is a fixed timebox, typically 8 to 12 weeks, in which an ART plans, builds, demonstrates and reviews a set of objectives. Scaled Agile notes a bias towards shorter PIs. The most common pattern is four development iterations followed by one Innovation and Planning (IP) iteration, which provides time for innovation, training, final integration, Inspect and Adapt and planning the next PI. There is no fixed rule on the number of iterations.

EventLevelWhenPurpose
Iteration Planning, Team Sync, Team Backlog Refinement, Iteration Review, Iteration RetrospectiveTeamEvery iterationPlan, coordinate daily, refine, review and improve team work
PI PlanningARTOnce per PI, typically two days, in the IP iterationCommitted PI objectives and an ART planning board of features and dependencies
ART SyncARTWeekly or more oftenTrack progress, dependencies, risks and scope
System DemoARTEnd of each iterationShow the integrated increment working
Inspect and AdaptARTEnd of each PIPI system demo, measures review and problem-solving workshop
Pre-Plan; Coordinate and DeliverSolution TrainAround each PIAlign several ARTs and suppliers on one solution

SAFe's phrase for releases is develop on cadence, release on demand: planning and integration follow the calendar, but features can be released whenever they are ready and the business wants them. See our PI planning guide for the two-day agenda, ROAM risk handling and the confidence vote.

Implementing SAFe: the Implementation Roadmap

Scaled Agile publishes an Implementation Roadmap that sequences a SAFe adoption. In outline it moves through the following stages; the exact step names have changed between versions, so treat this as a summary rather than the official list.

  1. Reach a tipping point. Leaders agree that the current way of working must change and why.
  2. Train change agents and leaders. Internal coaches, often SAFe Practice Consultants, and executives learn the framework first.
  3. Create a Lean-Agile Center of Excellence (LACE). A small team owns the transformation backlog.
  4. Organise around value. Map value streams and design the trains; value stream mapping is the usual starting tool.
  5. Prepare, train and launch the first ART. Fill roles, publish the PI calendar, train the teams together and run the first PI planning.
  6. Coach ART execution. Support the first PIs closely and act on Inspect and Adapt findings.
  7. Launch more ARTs and value streams, then enhance the portfolio. Extend the pattern and introduce Lean Portfolio Management.
  8. Accelerate. Measure, learn and improve continuously.

Change-management issues such as sponsorship, resistance and measuring progress apply to any large Agile adoption, not only SAFe. Read our Agile transformation guide for how to handle them.

Worked example: sizing and configuring SAFe for a 420-person organisation

This example is illustrative; the organisation and numbers are invented. A bank's technology division has 420 people and wants to decide how many trains to form and which configuration to adopt.

Step 1: map value streams and size the trains

The division maps four development value streams and counts the people needed to build and run each solution.

Value streamPeopleWithin 50–125?Teams of about ten
Retail digital banking120YesAbout 12
Business banking105YesAbout 10 to 11
Payments110YesAbout 11
Data platform85YesAbout 8 to 9

The total is 120 + 105 + 110 + 85 = 420 people. An earlier proposal merged retail and business digital banking into one train of 120 + 105 = 225 people, well above the 125 guide figure, so the division keeps them as two trains split by customer segment. That gives four ARTs.

Step 2: choose the configuration

Each train delivers a largely independent solution, and integration between them happens through stable APIs rather than joint releases, so no single solution needs a Solution Train. Large Solution SAFe would add roles and events with little benefit. However, the four value streams compete for one annual technology budget, and the board wants funding to follow strategy. The division therefore chooses Portfolio SAFe: Essential SAFe for each train plus Lean Portfolio Management.

Step 3: check the cadence cost

The division picks a 10-week PI: four two-week iterations plus a two-week IP iteration. A full-time team member has about 10 × 40 = 400 working hours per PI. Attending PI planning (two days, about 16 hours) and Inspect and Adapt (about half a day, 4 hours) takes 16 + 4 = 20 hours, or 20 ÷ 400 = 5.0% of the PI. With a 12-week PI the same events take 20 ÷ 480 = 4.2%. Team-level events, ART Sync for coaches and Product Owners, and System Demos come on top and are not included here.

The IP iteration is 2 ÷ 10 = 20% of the PI calendar, but it is not overhead: it holds integration, innovation, training and buffer for estimates that prove wrong. A 10-week cadence gives 52 ÷ 10 = 5.2 PIs a year, so in practice the calendar needs an adjustment, such as one longer PI over the year-end holidays; a 12-week cadence gives about 4.3.

Step 4: interpret and decide

The numbers support four trains in Portfolio SAFe, but they do not prove SAFe is the right choice. Before committing, the division should test whether a lighter approach would solve its actual problems; if the main pain is funding rather than cross-team delivery, Lean Portfolio Management practices alone might be enough. If it proceeds, it launches the payments train first, because that value stream has the most cross-team dependencies today and a sponsor willing to attend PI planning. Each train can record its first PI's features and capacity check in the release plan template, and portfolio objectives can be tracked with the OKR template, which holds objectives, key results, owners, baselines, targets and confidence.

Benefits, limitations and criticisms of SAFe

What SAFe does well

  • It gives large organisations a documented, shared vocabulary and a defined starting structure, which reduces design effort.
  • PI planning makes cross-team dependencies and risks visible early, in one room or one virtual board.
  • It addresses portfolio funding and governance, which team-level frameworks leave out.
  • Its training and certification ecosystem makes it easy to find people with a common baseline of knowledge.

Limitations and criticisms

SAFe is one of the most debated frameworks in Agile. Ken Schwaber, a co-creator of Scrum, criticised it in 2013 as a return to hierarchical control, and Thoughtworks' Technology Radar has warned that top-down control generates waste in the value stream. Common concerns are:

  • Weight: many roles, events and artefacts, which smaller organisations may not need.
  • Preserved hierarchy: existing managers and approval chains can survive under new names, so behaviour does not change.
  • Reduced team autonomy: synchronised cadences and PI commitments constrain how teams plan.
  • Planning theatre: PI planning becomes a ritual when scope is fixed in advance and teams cannot negotiate.
  • Commercial ecosystem: the framework is tied to paid training and certification, which critics say encourages adoption by default.

Supporters reply that SAFe is meant to be configured and that most failures come from adopting its structures without its principles. Both views have merit, which is why the decision to adopt SAFe should rest on your organisation's actual coordination problems rather than on the framework's popularity.

When SAFe is unsuitable and what to use instead

SAFe is designed for coordination problems that appear at scale. It is usually a poor fit when:

  • A single team, or a pair of closely working teams, can build the product; a shared backlog and a Scrum of Scrums may be enough.
  • The organisation cannot or will not reorganise around value streams, so trains would be departments in disguise.
  • Leaders want visibility but not decentralised decision-making; SAFe's events will then turn into status reporting.
  • Work is mostly continuous operational flow rather than product development; Kanban at team and service level may suit better.

Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS)One product built by up to eight teams (more with LeSS Huge)One Product Owner and one Product Backlog, very few added roles

Frequently asked questions

What is SAFe in simple terms?

SAFe is a framework for coordinating many Agile teams working on the same products or solutions. Teams are grouped into Agile Release Trains that plan together every 8 to 12 weeks, and the trains are linked to portfolio strategy and funding.

Is SAFe 6.0 still the current version?

SAFe 6.0 remains the basis of Core SAFe, whose Big Picture posters are still labelled 6.0. In 2026 Scaled Agile also released AI-Native SAFe as a second operating model alongside Core SAFe, not as a replacement.

What is AI-Native SAFe?

An operating model Scaled Agile released in 2026 for organisations where AI agents do a large share of the work. It uses smaller AI-augmented teams, a one-day PI Outcome Planning event, a recurring Sense and Respond event and a new AI Value Architect role.

What does PI stand for in SAFe?

Planning Interval. Before SAFe 6.0 it stood for Program Increment. It is a timebox of typically 8 to 12 weeks, commonly four development iterations and one Innovation and Planning iteration.

How many people do you need for SAFe?

SAFe's basic unit, the Agile Release Train, is generally 50 to 125 people. Organisations with fewer people working on one product rarely need SAFe.

What are the four SAFe configurations?

Essential SAFe, Large Solution SAFe, Portfolio SAFe and Full SAFe. Choose the smallest that covers your coordination and governance needs.

Is SAFe really Agile?

SAFe is built on Agile and Lean principles, and its teams use Scrum and Kanban. Critics argue that its structure can preserve top-down control, so whether an implementation is Agile depends on how much decision-making it actually decentralises.

What is the difference between SAFe and Scrum?

Scrum is a framework for one team of ten or fewer people. SAFe coordinates many such teams through trains, Planning Intervals and portfolio management; its teams often use Scrum internally.

What is the difference between SAFe and LeSS?

SAFe adds roles, events and portfolio layers for large enterprises. LeSS keeps Scrum's roles and scales one product with one Product Owner and one Product Backlog, with far less added structure but deeper organisational change.

Who created SAFe?

Dean Leffingwell, who first released it in 2011. It is maintained by Scaled Agile, Inc.

What does a Release Train Engineer do?

The RTE is the servant leader and coach of an Agile Release Train. They facilitate PI planning, ART Sync, System Demos and Inspect and Adapt, manage risks and impediments, and help the train improve its flow.

Which SAFe certification should I take first?

Most people start with SAFe Agilist through the Leading SAFe course, then take a role certification such as SAFe Scrum Master or POPM if their job requires it. Certification is not needed to work on a train.
