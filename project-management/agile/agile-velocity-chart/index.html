Agile Velocity Chart: How to Read, Build and Use It

An Agile velocity chart is a bar chart that shows how much work a team planned and actually finished in each recent sprint, usually measured in story points. Read across several sprints, it tells you how much the team can realistically take on next and roughly when a release is likely to be ready. This guide explains how the chart works, how to calculate velocity, how to build and read one, and how to forecast with it, using a worked eight-sprint example you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • Velocity is the total estimate of the backlog items that met the Definition of Done in a sprint; partly finished work counts as zero.
  • A velocity chart normally shows two bars per sprint, committed and completed, often with a rolling average line.
  • Plan the next sprint from the average of the last three to five sprints, adjusted for the team's actual availability.
  • Forecast releases as a range (for example 6 to 8 sprints), not a single date, and update the forecast every sprint.
  • Look for stability and trends rather than growth. A steady velocity makes forecasting reliable; a rising one is often a sign of estimate inflation.
  • Never use velocity to compare teams or judge individuals. It is a planning aid for the team, not a productivity score.

What is an Agile velocity chart?

An Agile velocity chart is a bar chart that plots, for each completed sprint, the amount of work the team committed to at Sprint Planning and the amount it actually completed to its Definition of Done. Teams use the trend across several sprints to plan how much to take on next and to forecast when larger pieces of work will be finished.

Velocity itself is the sum of the estimates, most often story points, of every backlog item that met the Definition of Done during a sprint. An item that is 90% finished counts as zero in that sprint and is counted in full in the sprint in which it is actually done. This all-or-nothing rule keeps the measure honest: it records finished, usable work rather than effort.

The term comes from Extreme Programming (XP). According to the Agile Alliance's practices timeline, "velocity" was added to XP around 2000, replacing an earlier idea called the load factor, and Scrum teams widely adopted it soon afterwards. It is worth knowing that the 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention velocity at all. It says that the more the Developers know about their past performance, upcoming capacity and Definition of Done, the more confident they will be in their Sprint forecasts. Velocity is simply the most common way teams record that past performance. For the Scrum-specific view, see velocity in Scrum.

Anatomy of a velocity chart

Most velocity charts, whether drawn in a spreadsheet or generated by a tool, share the same elements.

ElementWhat it showsWhy it matters
Horizontal axisSprints in order (Sprint 1, 2, 3…), usually the last 6 to 10Lets you see the trend rather than a single result
Vertical axisStory points (or another unit such as item count)The unit must stay the same across sprints for the chart to mean anything
Committed barPoints in the Sprint Backlog when the sprint startedShows what the team forecast it could finish

Average line (optional)Rolling average of completed points, often over the last three sprintsSmooths out noise and is the usual planning figure
Range band (optional)Lowest and highest recent velocitiesSupports forecasting with a range instead of one number

In Jira, for example, the velocity report on a Scrum board shows a commitment bar and a completed bar for each recent sprint, based on the estimation statistic the board uses. Other tools and spreadsheets follow the same idea, even if the labels differ.

How to calculate velocity

The formulas

  • Sprint velocity = sum of the estimates of all items that met the Definition of Done in the sprint.
  • Average velocity = total completed points over the last *n* sprints ÷ *n* (most teams use the last three to five sprints).
  • Say/do ratio (also called commitment reliability) = completed points ÷ committed points × 100%.

For example, if a team completed 25, 26 and 24 points in its last three sprints, its average velocity is (25 + 26 + 24) ÷ 3 = 25 points per sprint.

What counts and what does not

Teams get inconsistent charts mainly because they count different things from sprint to sprint. Agree the rules once, write them down and apply them every time.

How to build a velocity chart step by step

You do not need special software. A spreadsheet produces a perfectly good velocity chart in about ten minutes, and it is a useful exercise even if your tool draws one automatically, because it forces you to agree what counts.

  1. Agree the counting rules. Confirm the unit (story points or item count), the Definition of Done and how you treat bugs, spikes and items added mid-sprint.
  2. Set up the columns. In a sheet, create columns for Sprint, Committed, Completed, Rolling average and Say/do ratio.
  3. Record committed points at Sprint Planning. Enter the total estimate of the Sprint Backlog as soon as planning ends. Do not change it later, even if items are added or removed.
  4. Record completed points at the end of the sprint. Add up only the items that met the Definition of Done before the Sprint Review.
  5. Add the formulas. For the rolling average in row 4, use =AVERAGE(C2:C4) and fill down. For the say/do ratio use =C2/B2 formatted as a percentage.
  6. Insert a clustered column chart using the Sprint, Committed and Completed columns, so each sprint has two bars side by side.
  7. Add the average as a line. Add the rolling average column as a second series and change its chart type to a line.
  8. Label it clearly. Title the chart, label the axes, and add short notes for unusual sprints (holidays, incidents, team changes).
  9. Review it every sprint. Look at it briefly at Sprint Planning (to set capacity) and at the Sprint Retrospective (to discuss trends).
Tip

Keep the chart next to your planning data. Our free sprint planning template gives you a place to record capacity, committed items and completed points sprint by sprint.

How to read a velocity chart

Four questions to ask

  1. Is it stable? Look at the spread of completed points over the last five or so sprints. A narrow spread means forecasts will be reliable.
  2. Which way is the trend going? A gradual rise or fall over several sprints matters more than any single bar.
  3. How big is the gap between committed and completed? A consistent small gap is healthy. A large or growing gap suggests over-commitment, interruptions or poor refinement.
  4. Can we explain the outliers? Mark known causes (leave, incidents, team changes) so nobody over-reacts to them.

Common patterns and what they usually mean

PatternLikely causesWhat to do
Stable bars within about ±15% of the averageSettled team, consistent estimating, well-refined backlogKeep going; use the average with confidence
Steady rise over many sprintsGenuine learning and better tooling, or estimate inflationCheck whether similar items now get bigger estimates; compare with item counts
Steady fallGrowing technical debt, more support work, unclear requirements, team changesRaise it at the retrospective; make the hidden work visible
Saw-tooth (high, low, high, low)Large items finishing in alternate sprints, or work carried overSplit stories smaller so most finish within one sprint
Completed always well below committedOver-commitment, optimism, frequent interruptionsPlan to the recent average, protect the sprint from unplanned work
Completed often above committedUnder-commitment or work pulled in mid-sprintCommit a little more, or check that pulled-in work is genuinely priority
One sudden dipHoliday, illness, incident, release freezeAnnotate it and exclude it from range calculations if it is clearly a one-off
Completed exactly equals committed every sprintScope being trimmed or estimates adjusted to matchCheck that the counting rules are being followed honestly

Applied to our example, the chart shows a team that over-committed early, settled at around 24 to 27 points, and had one explained dip. The say/do ratio has stayed at or above 86% for the last four sprints, which suggests planning is now realistic.

Using the velocity chart for sprint planning

Velocity tells you what the team has historically finished. Capacity tells you how much time it has available next sprint. Good sprint planning combines the two.

  1. Start with the recent average. Use the last three sprints (25 points in our example).
  2. Work out normal capacity. Six people × 10 working days = 60 person-days in a typical sprint.
  3. Subtract known absences. One person is on leave for 5 days and there is a public holiday for everyone (6 person-days). Available capacity is 60 − 5 − 6 = 49 person-days, or about 82% of normal.
  4. Scale the velocity. 25 × 0.82 ≈ 20 points.
  5. Select items up to that level. Take the top items from the ordered Product Backlog until you reach roughly 20 points, and agree a Sprint Goal that those items support.
  6. Sense-check the plan. Ask the Developers whether it feels achievable. The number is a guide; the team's judgement decides.
Tip

Plan to the recent average, not the best sprint ever. Planning to the peak sets the team up to miss, and repeated misses push people to inflate estimates.

Forecasting a release with velocity

The most valuable use of a velocity chart is answering the question "When will it be ready?" with evidence. The method is simple, but it only works if you forecast a range.

  1. Size the remaining work. Make sure everything in scope for the release is in the Product Backlog and roughly estimated.
  2. Calculate a slow and a fast velocity. A common approach is to average the three lowest and the three highest of the last eight or so sprints.
  3. Divide and round up. Remaining points ÷ each velocity gives a range of sprints.
  4. Allow for scope growth. If scope has been growing, subtract the average growth per sprint from each velocity before dividing.
  5. Convert to dates. Multiply by sprint length and map onto the calendar, allowing for holidays.
  6. Update every sprint. Recalculate after each Sprint Review and show progress on a burnup chart.

Worked example

The team has 150 points left in the release. From the eight sprints above, the three lowest velocities are 18, 22 and 24 (average 21.3) and the three highest are 25, 26 and 27 (average 26).

ScenarioVelocity usedCalculationSprints neededWeeks (2-week sprints)
Fast26150 ÷ 26 = 5.8612
Typical23.9 (8-sprint average)150 ÷ 23.9 = 6.3714

The honest message to stakeholders is: "Most likely in 7 sprints (about 14 weeks), and very probably within 6 to 8 sprints, provided scope stays roughly where it is." If the date is fixed, the same numbers tell you how much scope is realistic: 6 sprints × 21.3 points ≈ 128 points in a pessimistic case, so the lowest-priority 22 or so points are at risk.

For larger portfolios or higher-stakes dates, teams sometimes go further with probabilistic techniques such as Monte Carlo simulation, which resample past sprint results thousands of times to give likelihoods for each date.

Tip

Fix either the date or the scope, not both. Velocity forecasting works best when lower-priority scope is allowed to flex.

Velocity chart vs burndown, burnup and other Agile charts

Velocity is one of several charts Agile teams use. Each answers a different question, and most teams use two or three together.

ChartWhat it showsTime frameBest for
Velocity chartPoints committed and completed per sprintAcross many sprintsPlanning capacity and long-range forecasts
Sprint burndownWork remaining in the current sprint, day by dayOne sprintSeeing whether the Sprint Goal is on track
Release burnupCompleted work and total scope over timeSeveral sprintsShowing progress when scope changes
Cumulative flow diagramItems in each workflow state over timeContinuousSpotting bottlenecks and growing work in progress
Throughput chartNumber of items finished per periodContinuousForecasting without story points
Cycle time scatterplotHow long each item took from start to finishContinuousPredicting how long a single item will take

A velocity chart tells you how much the team gets done per sprint. A burndown tells you whether this sprint is on track. A burnup tells you whether the release is on track and whether scope is growing.

Velocity vs capacity

The two terms are often confused, but they look in different directions.

VelocityCapacity
LooksBackwards at what was completedForwards at time available
UnitStory points (or items) per sprintPerson-days or hours
Changes whenThe team's way of working, item size or estimating changesPeople take leave, join, leave or split their time
Used forForecasting how much work fitsAdjusting that forecast for the coming sprint

In practice: velocity × (available capacity ÷ normal capacity) gives a sensible starting point for the next sprint, as in the planning example above.

How to stabilise and genuinely improve velocity

People often ask how to increase velocity. The honest answer is that you cannot improve it directly without distorting it. What you can do is remove the things that slow the team down, and a steadier, sometimes higher, velocity follows.

  • Split stories smaller. Items that each fit comfortably in a few days finish within the sprint, which removes the saw-tooth pattern. Our guide to user stories covers splitting techniques.
  • Refine the backlog regularly. Ongoing backlog refinement means items arrive at planning clear, estimated and ready.
  • Stabilise the Definition of Done. If "done" means different things in different sprints, velocity will wobble for reasons that have nothing to do with the work.
  • Limit work in progress. Finishing a few things beats starting many. WIP limits apply inside a sprint too.
  • Protect the sprint. Route unplanned requests through the Product Owner, and make support or incident work visible rather than hidden.
  • Pay down technical debt. Fragile code and manual testing slow every item a little; together they slow the team a lot.
  • Keep the team stable. Frequent changes of membership reset velocity, because the team's shared sense of what a point means changes.
  • Use the retrospective. Look at the velocity chart at each retrospective and pick one improvement to try.

Common velocity chart mistakes

  • Comparing teams. Each team calibrates story points differently, so 30 points for one team and 30 for another mean different things. Comparisons encourage point inflation.
  • Setting velocity targets. Goodhart's law, usually paraphrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", applies directly: targets make estimates grow, not output.
  • Measuring individuals. Velocity is a team measure. Individual velocity discourages pairing, helping and swarming.
  • Counting partly finished work. It flatters the current sprint and creates a hidden debt of unfinished work.
  • Re-estimating finished items. Changing an estimate after the work is known makes the chart self-fulfilling and useless for forecasting.
  • Mixing units. Switching between hours, points and item counts breaks the trend.
  • Treating the forecast as a promise. A forecast is a range with uncertainty, and stakeholders should hear it that way.
  • Valuing output over outcomes. A high velocity on low-value work is not success. Pair velocity with measures of customer value and quality.
  • Over-reacting to one sprint. One low bar with a known cause is noise, not a trend.

Velocity charts in project management tools

Most Agile project management tools can draw a velocity chart automatically from sprint data, which saves time and removes counting errors, provided the team closes sprints and records estimates consistently.

  • Jira includes a velocity report on Scrum boards showing commitment and completed work for recent sprints, and uses average velocity in its planning features.
  • ClickUp offers sprint features with velocity, burndown and burnup reporting for teams that run sprints.
  • Wrike, our top-rated tool overall, suits organisations where Agile teams work alongside marketing, operations or PMO teams and need shared dashboards, boards and cross-project reporting in one place.
  • Spreadsheets remain a good option for small teams or for agreeing the counting rules before choosing a tool.

Whichever tool you use, the chart is only as good as the data: close sprints on time, keep estimates unchanged once work starts, and record items added mid-sprint.

Velocity without story points

Story points are not the only option. Some teams find that counting items works just as well, especially once stories are consistently small.

  • Item count velocity. Count finished backlog items per sprint instead of points. It works when items are of broadly similar size.
  • Throughput in Kanban. Kanban teams do not usually use sprints or velocity; they track throughput (items finished per week) and cycle time instead, and forecast from those.
  • #NoEstimates. An approach in which teams split work into small, similar-sized items and forecast by counting them rather than estimating each one.

If you switch units, start a new chart rather than mixing the old and new data. To compare velocity with other forecasting approaches, see our guide to project forecasting.

Frequently asked questions

What is a velocity chart in Agile?

A bar chart that shows, for each recent sprint, the story points a team committed to and the points it actually completed to its Definition of Done. Teams use the trend to plan future sprints and forecast releases.

How do you calculate velocity in Agile?

Add up the estimates of every item that met the Definition of Done in the sprint. For planning, average the last three to five sprints: for example, 25, 26 and 24 points give an average velocity of 25.

What is a good velocity for a Scrum team?

There is no universally good number, because each team sizes story points differently. A good velocity is a stable one, because stability makes forecasts reliable.

How many sprints does it take for velocity to become reliable?

Usually three to five sprints with a stable team and consistent counting rules. New teams often over-commit in the first one or two sprints.

Should velocity increase over time?

Not necessarily. Velocity may rise a little as a team matures, but a steady rise is often a sign of estimate inflation. Aim for stability and improve the conditions for flow instead.

Do unfinished stories count towards velocity?

No. Partly finished items count as zero and go back to the Product Backlog. Their full estimate is counted in the sprint in which they meet the Definition of Done.

What is the difference between a velocity chart and a burndown chart?

A velocity chart compares committed and completed work across many sprints for planning. A sprint burndown tracks the work remaining day by day within one sprint.

Can you compare velocity between teams?

No. Story points are relative to each team, so the same number means different amounts of work for different teams. Comparing velocities encourages teams to inflate estimates.

Is velocity part of the Scrum Guide?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention velocity. It refers to the Developers' past performance and capacity when forecasting. Velocity is a common complementary practice that came from Extreme Programming. See velocity in Scrum.

What should we do when velocity drops suddenly?

First check for a simple cause such as leave, an incident or a team change. If there is none, raise it at the retrospective and look for growing technical debt, unclear requirements, dependencies or items that are too large.

Does Kanban use velocity?

Not usually. Kanban teams work in a continuous flow without sprints, so they track throughput (items finished per period) and cycle time instead.

Should bugs and technical debt count towards velocity?

Either approach works if you are consistent. Counting them shows the true workload; leaving them unestimated means velocity reflects only new features. Agree the rule and keep to it.

What does the gap between committed and completed mean?

It shows how realistic the team's planning is. A small, steady gap is healthy. A large or growing gap suggests over-commitment, interruptions, poor refinement or items that are too big.

How do you forecast a release date with velocity?

Divide the remaining points by a slow and a fast recent velocity to get a range of sprints, allow for any scope growth, and convert the range to dates. Update the forecast after every sprint.
