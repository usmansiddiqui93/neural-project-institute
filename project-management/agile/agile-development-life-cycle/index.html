Agile Development Life Cycle: 6 Phases With Examples

The Agile development life cycle is the path a product follows when it is built with Agile methods: concept, inception, repeated iterations, release, maintenance and, eventually, retirement. Unlike a linear life cycle, the build stage loops many times, and each loop delivers a working, tested piece of the product that real feedback can shape. This guide explains every phase, what it produces and how to run it, with a worked example and a comparison with the traditional SDLC.

Key takeaways

  • A common Agile development life cycle has six phases: concept, inception, iteration (construction), release, maintenance (production) and retirement.
  • Most of the effort sits in the iteration phase, which repeats in short cycles of one to four weeks and continues after the first release.
  • Planning, design, building and testing happen together inside every iteration, not in separate phases as in Waterfall.
  • There is no single official model: five-phase, six-phase and Disciplined Agile's three-phase versions describe the same idea with different names.
  • Release early increments to real users; feedback from production is what makes the loop worth running.
  • Plan capacity for maintenance from the start and give every product an owner until it is formally retired.

What is the Agile development life cycle?

The Agile development life cycle is the sequence of stages a product moves through when it is built using Agile methods. It starts with an idea, moves through short, repeated iterations that each produce a working and tested increment, and ends with release, ongoing support and retirement. Feedback from each iteration shapes the next.

A life cycle answers the question "what stages does the work pass through?". It is different from a methodology or framework, which answers "how do we work within those stages?". Scrum and Kanban, for example, are frameworks for running the iteration phase; neither tells you how to decide whether a product is worth starting or how to retire it.

It helps to picture two loops:

  • The product loop. The whole journey from concept to retirement, which may last months or many years.
  • The iteration loop. The short cycle of plan, build, test, review and adapt that repeats inside the iteration phase, usually every one to four weeks.

Both loops follow the values in the Agile Manifesto: working results over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration and responding to change. The underlying technique is iterative and incremental development: build the product in small slices and improve it each cycle.

Tip

There is no single official Agile development life cycle. The Agile Manifesto and the Scrum Guide do not define one. Treat the six phases as a practical map and rename them to fit your organisation.

Phase-by-phase guide

Each phase has a clear goal and a recognisable point at which you are ready to move on. The exit criteria below are our recommendations, not a formal standard.

Phase 1: Concept

The concept phase checks whether an idea deserves investment. A product manager or sponsor describes the problem, who has it and what outcome would count as success. The aim is a quick, cheap decision, not a detailed plan.

  • Activities: customer interviews, a lightweight feasibility study, rough cost and benefit estimates, a check against strategy.
  • Who: sponsor, product manager or Product Owner, a senior technical person, key stakeholders.
  • Outputs: a problem statement, a target outcome, a rough budget and timeframe, and a go/no-go decision.
  • Ready to move on when: a sponsor has agreed to fund inception and the desired outcome is written down in one or two sentences.

Phase 2: Inception

Inception gets the team ready to deliver. Many teams call this "Sprint 0", although the Scrum Guide does not use that term. Keep it short: the goal is just enough shared understanding to start building, not a full specification.

  • Activities: form the team, agree a product vision, map the main user journeys, write an initial set of epics and user stories, agree a Definition of Done, set up code repositories, environments and the team board, and sketch the architecture.
  • Who: the whole team, the Product Owner and a facilitator (Scrum Master or Agile project manager).
  • Outputs: product vision, initial product backlog, team working agreement, a first release plan, and often a lightweight project charter.
  • Ready to move on when: the top of the backlog holds enough refined items for the first one or two iterations and the team can build, test and deploy a trivial change end to end.

Phase 3: Iteration (construction)

This is where the product is built, and where most of the time and money go. The team repeats a short cycle, often a two-week sprint, in which it plans, designs, builds, tests and reviews a small set of backlog items. Kanban teams run the same activities as a continuous flow rather than in fixed cycles.

  • Activities: iteration planning, daily coordination, building and testing each item together, backlog refinement, review with stakeholders, retrospective.
  • Who: the delivery team (developers, testers, designers, analysts), the Product Owner, the Scrum Master, stakeholders at each review.
  • Outputs: working, tested increments that meet the Definition of Done; stakeholder feedback; an updated backlog; improvement actions.
  • Ready to move on when: an increment delivers enough value to release. In Agile this happens early and often, and iterations continue after the release.

Phase 4: Release (transition)

Release puts one or more increments into the hands of real users. With mature automation this can be a routine, low-risk step that happens many times per iteration. In more regulated or hardware-dependent settings it may involve formal acceptance, user training and a cut-over plan.

  • Activities: final regression and acceptance testing, release notes, user documentation and training, deployment, monitoring, handover to support.
  • Who: the team, the Product Owner, operations or DevOps, support staff, and sometimes compliance or security reviewers.
  • Outputs: a released product or feature set, release notes, a support handover.
  • Ready to move on when: the release is live, stable and supported, and its usage is being measured.

Phase 5: Maintenance (production)

Once a product is live, the team supports it, fixes defects and keeps improving it. Agile maintenance is not a separate team quietly patching code: new features and fixes go into the same backlog and are prioritised against each other.

  • Activities: incident handling, defect fixing, performance monitoring, small enhancements, further iterations driven by usage data.
  • Who: the product team (often with reduced capacity), operations, support.
  • Outputs: fixes, enhancements, operational metrics, an updated roadmap.
  • Ready to move on when: the product no longer delivers enough value to justify its cost, or a replacement is ready.

Phase 6: Retirement

Retirement is the phase most teams forget. A product with no owner and no plan carries security, cost and data-protection risks. Retirement is a small project in its own right and is usually run through the same backlog.

  • Activities: announce the end of life, migrate users and data, archive records, switch off infrastructure, close contracts and licences, capture lessons learned.
  • Who: Product Owner, team, operations, legal or data protection, communications.
  • Outputs: decommissioning plan, migrated or archived data, closure report.
  • Ready to close when: users have moved, data is handled according to policy and costs have stopped.

How one iteration works, step by step

The iteration loop is the engine of the Agile life cycle. Here is a typical two-week iteration for a Scrum team, with the Scrum Guide's maximum time-boxes scaled down for a two-week sprint. Teams using other frameworks follow the same rhythm with different names.

  1. Plan. In Sprint Planning the Product Owner explains the most valuable items, the team selects what it can finish and agrees one Sprint Goal, then breaks items into tasks.
  2. Coordinate daily. In a 15-minute Daily Scrum the developers check progress towards the goal and adjust the plan for the day.
  3. Design, build and test together. Designers, developers and testers work on the same item at the same time rather than passing it along a chain. Automated tests run on every change.
  4. Refine upcoming work. Part of the team's time goes on backlog refinement: clarifying, splitting and estimating items for the next iteration.
  5. Finish to Done. An item counts only when it meets the Definition of Done, including testing, review and documentation where required.
  6. Review. In the Sprint Review the team shows working results to stakeholders and agrees what to change in the backlog.
  7. Reflect. In the Sprint Retrospective the team chooses one or two concrete improvements to how it works.
  8. Adapt and repeat. Feedback updates the backlog, and the next iteration starts immediately.
DayMain activityTypical time
Day 1 (morning)Sprint PlanningUp to 4 hours
Days 1 to 10Daily Scrum15 minutes each day
Days 2 to 9Design, build, test; release when readyMost of the sprint
Around days 5 to 7Backlog refinement for the next sprintUsually no more than 10% of capacity (a common guideline, not a Scrum rule)
Day 10Sprint ReviewUp to 2 hours
Day 10Sprint RetrospectiveUp to 1.5 hours

Releasing does not have to wait for the end of the iteration. Many teams deploy each item as soon as it is Done.

5-phase, 6-phase and Disciplined Agile models compared

If you have seen different numbers of phases in different sources, you have not misunderstood anything. Authors slice the same journey in different ways. The table maps the most common versions to each other.

6-phase model (this guide)Common 5-stage modelAlternative 6-phase modelDisciplined Agile (PMI)
ConceptIdeation / requirementsConcept(Before the delivery life cycle)
InceptionIdeation / requirementsInceptionInception
Iteration (construction)Development, then testingIteration, then testingConstruction
Release (transition)DeploymentReleaseTransition
Maintenance (production)MaintenanceReview(Operations, outside delivery)
RetirementNot usually shownNot usually shown(Outside delivery)

Disciplined Agile, created by Scott Ambler and Mark Lines and acquired by PMI in 2019, describes its delivery life cycles in three phases: Inception, Construction and Transition. It treats concept, operations and retirement as part of the wider product or enterprise life cycle rather than the delivery project.

Two practical points follow:

  • Do not split testing into its own phase. Models that show "testing" after "development" are describing what happens inside each iteration, not a separate stage. A testing phase at the end turns Agile back into Waterfall.
  • Pick one model and use it consistently. It matters far more that your team and stakeholders share the same words than which model you choose.

Worked example: a gym class-booking app

A regional gym chain with 12 sites wants members to book classes online. Staff currently take bookings by phone. Here is how the product moves through the life cycle. The figures are illustrative.

Concept (1 week)

The product manager confirms that phone bookings take reception staff around 20 hours a week across the chain and that members often find classes already full. The outcome is set as "most class bookings made online within six months of launch". The operations director approves a budget for inception.

Inception (2 weeks)

A team of six forms: a Product Owner, three developers, a tester and a designer, with a part-time Scrum Master. They write a one-page vision, map the member journey and create 40 user stories grouped into four epics: timetable, booking and cancellation, waiting lists and reminders, and payments. The team sizes the stories at 130 story points in total and agrees two-week sprints.

Iterations (6 two-week sprints)

SprintSprint GoalPoints completedReleased?
1Members can view the live class timetable for their gym18Internal only
2Members can book and cancel a class22No
3Pilot-ready: booking confirmation emails and staff view23Yes, at 2 pilot gyms
4Waiting lists with automatic promotion21Yes, pilot gyms
5Reminders and no-show tracking20Yes, pilot gyms
6Card payments for paid classes; roll-out readiness20Yes, all 12 gyms

After sprint 3 the team has completed 63 points, so 67 remain. Its average velocity is 21 points per sprint, which forecasts a little over three more sprints. The Product Owner moves a low-value "share on social media" story (6 points) out of the first release, which brings the remaining scope to 61 points, about three sprints. The plan for six sprints holds.

Release

The pilot release after sprint 3 surfaces a problem nobody predicted: members book several classes and cancel late. The team adds a cancellation cut-off story to sprint 4. After sprint 6 the app is rolled out to all 12 gyms with a short training session for reception staff.

Maintenance and retirement

The team drops to three people and spends roughly half its capacity on fixes and half on improvements chosen from usage data. Years later the chain moves to a new member platform; the old app is retired, booking history is migrated and the hosting contract is closed.

Agile life cycle vs traditional SDLC

The software development life cycle (SDLC) is a general term for the stages of building software. A traditional, or Waterfall, SDLC runs each stage once and in order. The Agile life cycle runs the same activities in short repeated loops.

AspectTraditional (Waterfall) SDLCAgile development life cycle
Order of workRequirements, design, build, test and deploy happen once, in sequencePlan, design, build and test repeat in every iteration
RequirementsFixed and signed off up frontEvolve in a prioritised backlog
TestingA separate phase near the endContinuous, inside each iteration
DeliveryOne main release at the endFrequent increments, often released early
ChangeHandled through formal change controlExpected and absorbed through backlog reordering
Customer involvementMainly at the start and at acceptanceAt every review
Progress measurePercentage of phases or tasks completeWorking, accepted increments
Risk discoveryLate, often in testingEarly, through working results and feedback
Best suited toStable, well-understood requirements; heavy regulationUncertain or changing requirements; need for early value

Many organisations use a hybrid: a predictive approach for funding, contracts and hardware, and Agile iterations for the software. That can work well if each iteration still produces tested, working results.

How Scrum and Kanban fit the life cycle

Agile frameworks focus mainly on the iteration phase. Knowing which phases a framework covers helps you fill the gaps with your own practices.

Scrum suits product development with clear goals for each cycle; Kanban suits continuous streams such as maintenance and support. Many teams switch from Scrum to Kanban when a product moves into maintenance.

How to set up an Agile life cycle for your project

Use these steps to tailor the life cycle to a new product or project. They reflect our own recommendations rather than any single standard.

  1. Name your phases. Choose the six-phase model or a variant your organisation already uses, and write down what each phase means for you.
  2. Agree entry and exit criteria. For each phase, list what must be true before you move on, such as "top of backlog refined for two sprints" before leaving inception.
  3. Keep concept and inception short. Time-box them, for example two weeks each, and accept that you will learn more once you start building.
  4. Choose your iteration model. Fixed sprints (Scrum) for goal-driven development, continuous flow (Kanban) for unpredictable work, or a mix.
  5. Plan the first release early. Identify the smallest increment that real users could benefit from and aim to release it within the first few iterations.
  6. Build in maintenance capacity. Reserve a share of each iteration, for example 10 to 20 per cent, for defects and support once the product is live, and adjust it based on actual demand.
  7. Decide how you will measure success. Pick outcome measures from the concept phase and track them after every release.
  8. Write down the retirement trigger. Agree in advance what would cause the product to be retired and who would decide.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Mini-Waterfall sprints. Doing design in one sprint, build in the next and testing after that. Fix: finish each item, including testing, within one iteration.
  • An endless inception. Spending months on a detailed specification before building anything. Fix: time-box inception and start with the highest-risk items.
  • Skipping inception entirely. Starting sprints without a shared vision, so the backlog drifts. Fix: hold at least a short vision and backlog workshop before sprint 1.
  • Never releasing. Building many increments but holding them back for one big launch. Fix: release to a pilot group as soon as there is something useful.
  • A separate testing phase. Leaving testing to a hardening phase at the end. Fix: include testing in the Definition of Done.
  • Forgetting maintenance capacity. Planning every iteration as if there were no support work. Fix: reserve capacity and track unplanned work.
  • Ignoring retirement. Leaving old products running with no owner. Fix: name an owner for every live product and review its value at least yearly.
  • Treating phases as approval gates. Adding heavy sign-offs between iterations. Fix: use reviews with stakeholders instead of document approvals.

Frequently asked questions

What are the phases of the Agile development life cycle?

A common model has six phases: concept, inception, iteration (construction), release, maintenance (production) and retirement. The iteration phase repeats many times.

What are the 5 stages of Agile development?

Five-stage models usually combine concept and inception into one ideation or requirements stage and drop retirement, giving ideation, development, testing, deployment and maintenance. They describe the same journey as the six-phase model with different boundaries.

Is Agile an SDLC?

Agile is an approach to running the software development life cycle. The Agile development life cycle is one way of organising the SDLC, with the build stages repeated in short iterations instead of running once in sequence.

What is the difference between the SDLC and the Agile life cycle?

SDLC is the general term for the stages of building software. A traditional SDLC runs each stage once, in order. The Agile life cycle repeats planning, design, build and test in short loops and releases working increments early.

Which phase of the Agile development life cycle takes the longest?

The iteration phase, because the team repeats it many times and usually continues iterating after the first release, during maintenance.

How long is an iteration in the Agile development life cycle?

Usually one to four weeks. Scrum limits a sprint to one month or less, and two weeks is the most common choice. Kanban teams work in a continuous flow instead of fixed iterations.

Does the Agile development life cycle have a design phase?

Not a separate one. High-level design and architecture are sketched during inception, and detailed design happens continuously inside each iteration.

Where does testing happen in the Agile life cycle?

Inside every iteration. Work is not counted as finished until it is tested and meets the Definition of Done. Some models show testing as a phase, but that describes an activity within each iteration.

Is Scrum an Agile development life cycle?

Scrum is a framework for running the iteration phase. It does not define concept, release management or retirement, so teams add their own practices for those phases.

What happens in the inception phase of the Agile life cycle?

The team forms, agrees a product vision, creates an initial backlog of epics and user stories, sets up tools and environments, agrees a Definition of Done and drafts a first release plan.

Can you release before the end of the Agile life cycle?

Yes, and it is encouraged. Releasing early increments brings real user feedback sooner and starts delivering value before the whole product is complete.

Is there documentation in the Agile life cycle?

Yes. Agile values working results over comprehensive documentation, not instead of it. Teams produce the documentation users, support and regulators need, usually as part of the Definition of Done.

What is the Disciplined Agile life cycle?

Disciplined Agile, now owned by PMI, describes delivery in three phases: Inception, Construction and Transition. It offers several life cycle variants, including Agile, Lean, continuous delivery and exploratory versions.

Can the Agile development life cycle be used outside software?

Yes. Marketing, product design, construction and service teams use the same phases, replacing software deployment with launches, hand-overs or go-lives. The key ideas, short loops and early feedback, still apply.
