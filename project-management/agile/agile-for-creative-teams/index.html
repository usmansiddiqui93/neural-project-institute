Agile for Creative Teams: Kanban, Sprints and Feedback

Agile for creative teams means managing design, content, video and brand work in small, visible pieces, with limits on how much is in progress and short, regular feedback loops. It suits creative work because good ideas already emerge by making something, showing it and refining it. This guide shows how to choose between Kanban and sprints, set up a creative board, control review rounds and measure flow, with two illustrative worked examples.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • Last updated 4 November 2026

Key takeaways

  • Agile helps creative teams handle a constant stream of requests, shifting priorities and long feedback chains without sacrificing time for good work.
  • Kanban is the usual starting point for in-house studios with continuous requests; sprints suit larger, goal-driven work such as a website, campaign or content hub.
  • Limit work in progress: fewer jobs in parallel means each one gets real attention and finishes sooner.
  • Most creative delay sits in review, so agree reviewers, consolidate feedback through one person and set the number of rounds in the brief.
  • Track cycle time, throughput and review rounds per job rather than hours or story points.
  • Start with one team, one board and one intake route, then improve the process at a short retrospective every few weeks.

What is Agile for creative teams?

Agile for creative teams is the use of Agile values and practices, such as visual boards, limits on work in progress, short iterations and regular reviews, to manage creative work like design, copywriting, video and content. It replaces long, hidden production cycles with small, visible steps, so stakeholders give feedback early and the team works on the most important jobs first.

Agile did not start in creative work, but it fits well. Concepts, sketches, storyboards and rough cuts are already iterative: you make something, show it and refine it. What creative teams usually lack is not iteration but control over how much arrives, in what order it is done and how feedback flows. That is the part Agile supplies.

Translating Agile for creative work

Agile vocabulary comes from software, so translate it into creative terms before you introduce it. The table shows the equivalents most teams use.

Agile conceptCreative equivalentExample
Product backlogOrdered list of creative requests and projectsSpring campaign assets, new sales deck, recruitment video
User storyA brief written from the audience's point of viewAs a first-time visitor, I want a homepage banner that explains the offer in five seconds
IncrementA reviewable piece of workThree logo routes, a storyboard, a first draft, a rough cut
Definition of DoneA shared checklist for "finished"Proofread, brand-checked, correct sizes exported, files named and archived
Sprint ReviewA regular show-and-tell with stakeholdersFortnightly 30-minute review of work in progress
RetrospectiveA short session on how the team worksWhy did the brochure take four rounds? What will we change?
WIP limitA cap on jobs in each stageNo more than four jobs in design at once

The Definition of Done is particularly useful for creative teams, because many reworks happen after approval when sizes, formats or legal copy turn out to be wrong.

What does not translate well

  • Precise estimates for exploratory work. Concept work is hard to estimate in hours. Use rough sizes and timeboxes instead.
  • Velocity comparisons between people. Creative jobs vary too much in nature; compare the team with itself over time, never designer against designer.
  • Rigid sprint scope. Creative teams usually need room for urgent work, so plan a capacity buffer rather than pretending interruptions will not happen.

Kanban, Scrum, Scrumban or design sprint?

There is no single Agile framework for creative teams. Choose by how work arrives and how much of it is goal-driven.

ApproachHow it worksBest forWatch out for
KanbanContinuous flow on a board with WIP limits and explicit policiesIn-house studios and content teams with a steady stream of requests of different sizesWithout WIP limits it becomes just a to-do board
ScrumFixed sprints of one month or less with a Sprint Goal, planning, review and retrospectiveLarger, goal-driven work: a website, a design system, a content hub, a campaign launchUrgent requests breaking the sprint; too many meetings for a small team
ScrumbanKanban flow plus a regular planning and review cadenceTeams with both continuous requests and a few larger projectsDrifting back into an unlimited to-do list
Design sprintA five-day process to map a problem, sketch, decide, prototype and test with customersAnswering one big question quickly, such as a new product concept or campaign ideaIt is a one-off workshop, not a way to run the team

Do not confuse a design sprint with a Scrum Sprint. The design sprint was developed by Jake Knapp, who ran it at Google from 2010 and brought it to GV in 2012, and it is described in the book *Sprint* (2016). It compresses discovery into five days and ends with customer testing of a prototype. A Scrum Sprint is a repeating cycle for delivering work.

Most in-house creative teams start with Kanban and add a fortnightly review and planning slot, which is effectively Scrumban. Our comparison of Kanban vs Scrumban explains the difference in more detail.

How to set up a creative Kanban board, step by step

  1. Map how work really flows today. Follow three or four recent jobs from request to delivery and list every stage they passed through, including waiting for feedback.
  2. Turn the stages into columns. Keep it to six to eight columns. Split review into internal and stakeholder review if they are handled by different people.
  3. Write an entry policy for each column: what must be true before a card can move there. This is what stops half-briefed jobs entering production.
  4. Set WIP limits. A simple starting point is one to one and a half jobs per person in active stages, then adjust after two or three weeks.
  5. Design the card. Include requester, deliverable, deadline, size, class of service and a link to the brief and files.
  6. Add an expedite lane with a limit of one card, for genuinely urgent work.
  7. Agree a short daily check at the board, focused on blocked and ageing cards rather than a status round.
  8. Review the board every two to four weeks in a retrospective and adjust columns, policies and limits.

Intake, prioritisation and classes of service

A creative team cannot control its flow if work enters through ten different doors. Set up one intake route, usually a request form, and make a complete brief the price of entry. A work request template covers the basics for small requests, and the creative brief template covers larger projects: objective, audience, key message, deliverables, mandatories, deadline and approvers.

Who decides the order?

Name one person who orders the backlog, usually the creative director, head of content or studio manager, working from priorities agreed with marketing or business leaders. Stakeholders can argue for their requests, but only one person sets the order, otherwise the team falls back into "loudest wins".

Sizing creative work

Rough sizing is enough for creative capacity planning. Many teams use t-shirt sizes, for example S (up to a day), M (two to three days), L (about a week) and XL (more than a week, so split it). If you want relative points instead, our guide to Agile estimation explains how.

Classes of service

Classes of service, a practice from the Kanban Method, give different types of work different rules, so urgency is handled openly rather than by interruption.

ClassExampleRule
ExpediteFixing an error in a live advertStarts immediately; limit of one at a time; can break WIP limits
Fixed dateEvent signage, a print deadline, a launchPulled early enough to meet the date, based on cycle time
StandardMost requestsFirst in, first out within the agreed priority order
IntangibleTemplate library, brand guidelines refreshReserved share of capacity, such as 10%, so it does progress
Tip

Agree with stakeholders in writing what qualifies as expedite. If more than one or two jobs a fortnight are expedited, the real problem is planning upstream, and that is worth raising at a leadership level.

Running creative work in sprints

Sprints work well when a creative team has a clear goal for a few weeks, such as launching a campaign or building a set of templates. Two weeks is the most common length for creative teams; the 2020 Scrum Guide allows up to one month.

Write outcome-focused Sprint Goals

  • Weak: design 12 banners, 3 emails and a landing page.
  • Better: launch the spring offer across web and email so that customers understand the offer and how to claim it.

An outcome-focused goal lets the team trade one deliverable for another when feedback shows a better route, without breaking the sprint.

Adapted events for a two-week creative sprint

EventCreative adaptationTypical length
Sprint PlanningAgree the goal, pick backlog items by size and capacity, confirm briefs are ready1 to 2 hours
Daily check-in15 minutes at the board: what is blocked, what is waiting for feedback15 minutes
Mid-sprint review slotStakeholders see concepts and drafts while change is still cheap30 minutes
Sprint ReviewShow finished and in-progress work against the goal; gather feedback for the backlog45 to 60 minutes
RetrospectiveDiscuss what helped and hindered, such as review rounds or unclear briefs45 to 60 minutes

Plan only 70 to 80% of capacity for sprint work and keep the rest for urgent requests and small fixes. Use our sprint retrospective template to run the retrospective, and see our guide to the Sprint Retrospective for formats.

Managing feedback and review rounds

In many creative teams, more time is spent waiting for or reacting to feedback than making the work. Agile helps by making review visible, early and bounded.

A simple review process

  1. Name reviewers in the brief, including one approver who has the final say.
  2. Share early. Show rough concepts, wireframes, outlines or storyboards before investing in polish.
  3. Ask for feedback against the brief: does this meet the objective for this audience? Personal taste comes second.
  4. Set a feedback deadline, such as two working days, and put the card in a stakeholder review column so the wait is visible.
  5. Consolidate. One person, usually the requester, resolves conflicting comments before they reach the creative.
  6. Count the round on the card, and record the reason when a job needs an extra round.
  7. Treat extra rounds beyond the agreed number as change, to be prioritised against other work.

How many review rounds?

Two rounds after the concept stage is a common agreement for standard work, with one more for complex or high-profile pieces. The number matters less than agreeing it in advance and tracking it. Rounds that keep exceeding the agreement usually point to weak briefs or reviewers joining late, both of which you can fix.

Worked example 1: an in-house design studio on Kanban

This example is illustrative; the organisation and figures are invented to show the method.

A retail company's in-house studio has six people: four designers, a motion designer and a studio manager. It serves marketing, e-commerce and HR. Requests arrive by email and chat, every designer is juggling seven or eight jobs, and stakeholders complain that small jobs take weeks.

What the team changed

  1. Week 1: mapped five recent jobs and found most of their elapsed time was spent waiting in review or for missing information.
  2. Week 2: launched one request form, built the eight-column board above and wrote entry policies. Jobs without a complete brief stayed in Requests.
  3. Week 3: set WIP limits of three in Concept and five in Production, which meant finishing jobs before starting new ones, and added an expedite lane with a limit of one.
  4. Week 4: introduced a two-day feedback deadline and a single consolidator per request.
  5. Week 6: held the first retrospective and split Stakeholder review into two lanes, because legal review needed its own policy.

Before and after (illustrative)

MeasureBeforeAfter 8 weeks
Jobs in progress at once4116
Average cycle time, all jobsAbout 19 working daysAbout 6 working days
Average review rounds per job3.62.1
Jobs delivered per week1113
Expedited jobs per fortnightNot tracked2

The team did not work harder; it worked on fewer things at once and removed waiting. The figures follow Little's Law, which links the three averages: cycle time equals work in progress divided by throughput (41 jobs at 11 a week is about 3.7 weeks; 16 jobs at 13 a week is about 1.2 weeks). Cut WIP without cutting throughput and cycle time falls.

Worked example 2: a content team on two-week sprints

This example is also illustrative. A B2B software company's content team of five (a managing editor acting as product owner, two writers, a designer and an SEO specialist) needs to launch a new pricing resource hub in six weeks.

Capacity

Two weeks contain 10 working days, so the team has 50 person-days. After holidays (3 days) and a 20% buffer for urgent requests (about 9 days), it plans around 38 person-days of sprint work.

Sprint 1 plan

Sprint Goal: publish the hub's core page and two cornerstone guides so prospects can compare plans without contacting sales.

Mid-sprint, the sales director reviews the outline of Guide 2 and asks for a worked cost example. The editor swaps the second social cut-down for the extra work, so the Sprint Goal still holds. At the Sprint Review, sales asks for a calculator; the editor adds it to the backlog for Sprint 2 rather than squeezing it in. The retrospective finds that legal review took four days, so the team books a standing legal slot for Sprint 2.

Agile for design, content and video

The principles stay the same across creative disciplines, but the unit of work and the early artefact you share differ.

DisciplineTypical unit of workEarly artefact to shareUsual framework
Graphic and brand designAn asset or asset setSketches, mood boards, two or three routesKanban
UX and product designA user journey or screen setWireframes, clickable prototypeScrum alongside the product team
Copy and contentAn article, page or emailOutline and headline optionsKanban or Scrumban
Video and motionA video or series of editsScript, storyboard, animatic, rough cutPhased plan for the shoot, Kanban or sprints for edits
CampaignsA launch across channelsKey visual and message testSprints

Video shows why hybrids are common: pre-production and shoot days are fixed and need a predictive plan, while editing benefits from short review cycles on rough cuts. Agile for agencies and client work

Agencies face an extra constraint: the client. A fixed-scope statement of work that lists every deliverable sits awkwardly with Agile. Three adjustments help:

  • Retainers with a capacity model. The client buys a team's time each month, and a client-side owner prioritises the backlog with the account lead.
  • Phased scopes. Fix the scope and price of discovery and concept work, then agree production scope once the direction is approved.
  • Rounds and change in the contract. State the number of review rounds and how extra rounds or new deliverables are handled.

Invite the client to regular reviews rather than sending finished work for approval. Our guide to agency project management covers pricing models, scoping and resourcing in detail.

Metrics for Agile creative teams

Measure the flow of work, not hours or individual output. These measures are easy to collect from a board and lead to useful conversations.

MetricHow to calculateWhat it tells you
Cycle timeDelivery date minus the date work started, per jobHow long a job takes once started; use it to promise dates
ThroughputJobs delivered per week or sprintTeam capacity and trend
Review rounds per jobRounds recorded on each card, averaged by job typeBrief quality and review discipline
First-time-right rateJobs approved at first review divided by all jobs reviewedAlignment between brief, creative and stakeholders
WIP ageDays each open job has been in progressStalled work before it becomes late
On-time deliveryJobs delivered by the agreed date divided by all jobs deliveredReliability as seen by stakeholders

How to introduce Agile to a creative team

  1. Explain the why in creative terms. Start from the team's frustrations, such as interruptions and endless rounds, not from Agile jargon.
  2. Pick a pilot team of up to ten people who share work and a manager willing to protect the experiment.
  3. Agree the order-setter and the single intake route with the stakeholders who send most requests.
  4. Build the board together from a map of real recent jobs, with entry policies and WIP limits.
  5. Choose a cadence: daily 15-minute board check, a fortnightly stakeholder review and a retrospective every two to four weeks.
  6. Collect a baseline of cycle time, throughput and review rounds in the first two weeks.
  7. Run for eight weeks before judging. Adjust columns, limits and policies at each retrospective.
  8. Share results with stakeholders and extend to other teams only once the pilot works.

Agile works outside software when the work can be shown in pieces and feedback is available; our guide to whether Agile is only for software covers other non-software examples.

Common mistakes when applying Agile to creative work

  • Copying software Scrum wholesale. Five events, story points and burndown charts can overwhelm a four-person studio. Start with a board, WIP limits and a retrospective.
  • A board without WIP limits. It shows the overload but does nothing to reduce it.
  • Letting work bypass intake. One senior exception soon becomes everyone's habit.
  • Sharing work only when polished. Late feedback on finished work causes the most expensive rework.
  • Unlimited reviewers. Every additional reviewer adds a round; name them in the brief.
  • Measuring individuals. Comparing designers' output damages trust and encourages rushing; measure the team's flow.
  • No time for exploration. Timebox concept work rather than squeezing it out; creative quality depends on it.
  • Skipping retrospectives when busy. That is when the team most needs to fix the process.

Tools for Agile creative teams

Creative teams need more than a board: request forms, proofing on the asset, approvals and workload views make the biggest difference.

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool (9.4/10), is a strong choice for marketing and creative teams that need structured request forms, proofing and approvals, plus dashboards across many projects. It rewards time spent on setup.
  • Asana (9.0/10) is easy to adopt and offers approvals and proofing on images and creative assets on its Advanced plan.
  • ClickUp (8.9/10) combines forms, content calendars, proofing, docs for briefs and whiteboards at a competitive price.
  • monday.com (9.1/10) offers highly visual boards that creative teams adopt quickly, and Trello suits small teams that want a simple Kanban board.

Frequently asked questions

Does Agile limit creativity?

Done well, it protects creativity. Limiting work in progress reduces interruptions, clear priorities stop constant re-planning, and early feedback means less effort is wasted on directions that will be rejected. Timebox exploratory work rather than squeezing it out.

What is the best Agile framework for creative teams?

Kanban is usually the best start for in-house studios, because creative requests arrive continuously and vary in size. Scrum suits goal-driven projects such as a website or campaign launch. Many teams combine them as Scrumban.

Is Scrum or Kanban better for designers?

Kanban suits designers handling a stream of varied requests; Scrum suits designers embedded in a product or campaign team with a shared goal for each sprint. UX designers working with software teams often follow the team's Scrum cadence.

How long should a creative sprint be?

Two weeks is the most common length for creative teams. The 2020 Scrum Guide allows up to one month. Shorter sprints give faster feedback but more planning overhead; longer ones risk late surprises.

Should creative teams estimate their work?

Rough sizing, such as small, medium and large, is usually enough to plan capacity. Precise hour estimates are rarely worth the effort for exploratory creative work. Use cycle time data to forecast dates instead.

What is a design sprint?

A design sprint is a five-day process, developed by Jake Knapp at Google and GV, in which a small team maps a problem, sketches solutions, decides on one, builds a prototype and tests it with customers. It answers one big question quickly and is different from a Scrum Sprint.

How do you manage feedback in Agile creative teams?

Name reviewers and one approver in the brief, share rough work early, ask for feedback against the brief's objective, set a feedback deadline and have one person consolidate conflicting comments before they reach the creative.

How many review rounds should creative work have?

Two rounds after the concept stage is a common agreement for standard work, with one more for complex pieces. Agree the number in the brief, track rounds on each job and treat extra rounds as change.

How do Agile creative teams handle urgent requests?

Use an expedite lane on the board with a limit of one job, agree in writing what qualifies as urgent, and keep 20 to 30% of sprint capacity free for unplanned work.

Can video production use Agile?

Yes, usually as a hybrid. Pre-production and shoot days need a fixed plan, while scripting and editing benefit from short review cycles on storyboards, animatics and rough cuts.

How do agencies use Agile with fixed-scope clients?

They use phased scopes, retainers based on team capacity, and contracts that state the number of review rounds and how changes are handled. Clients are invited to regular reviews instead of seeing only finished work.

What columns should a creative Kanban board have?

A typical board has Requests, Ready, Concept, Production, Internal review, Stakeholder review, Final artwork and Delivered, each with a simple entry policy and WIP limits on the active stages.
