Is Agile Only for Software? How Agile Works Beyond IT

Is Agile only for software? No. Agile was named and popularised by software developers, but its core idea, delivering value in small steps and adjusting based on feedback, works for most knowledge work where the answer is not fully known at the start. This guide explains where the myth comes from, when Agile fits outside software and when it does not, what changes in practice, and how to start, with two worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • Agile is not only for software: marketing, HR, finance, government, hardware and construction teams all use Agile practices.
  • Agile's roots were never purely software: Scrum drew on studies of copiers, cameras and cars, and Kanban comes from manufacturing.
  • Agile fits best where the work is uncertain, can be delivered in usable pieces and feedback is available quickly.
  • Outside software, translate the terms: an increment might be a campaign, a policy pilot or a prototype, and the Definition of Done covers checks such as legal or brand approval.
  • Kanban is often the easiest starting point for non-software teams; many projects end up with a hybrid of predictive and Agile planning.

Is Agile only for software? The short answer

No. Agile is a way of working that delivers results in small, usable increments, gets feedback early and adapts the plan as the team learns. It started in software development, but any team facing uncertainty, changing priorities and work that can be split into smaller pieces can use it, from marketing and HR to hardware engineering.

What matters is not the industry but the nature of the work. Agile helps most when you cannot fully specify the result in advance, when customer or stakeholder feedback will change what you build, and when delivering something partial early is better than delivering everything late. It helps least when the work is well understood, cannot be split, or is very expensive to change once started.

Our guide to Agile project management explains the approach in general; this guide focuses on using it beyond software.

Why people think Agile is only for software

The myth has understandable causes:

  • The language. The Agile Manifesto, written at Snowbird, Utah in February 2001, values "working software over comprehensive documentation" and says "working software is the primary measure of progress". Read literally, it is about software.
  • The authors. The seventeen people who wrote the Manifesto were mostly software practitioners, and the early frameworks, such as Extreme Programming, include practices that only make sense in code.
  • The tools and job titles. Agile tools such as Jira grew up in software teams, and roles such as "developer" sound technical.
  • Training and certification. Much Agile training uses software examples, so people outside IT rarely see their own work reflected.
  • Early success stories. The most visible Agile transformations happened in technology companies and IT departments.

None of these makes the ideas themselves software-specific. Replace "working software" with "working results" and most of the Agile Manifesto values and principles apply directly.

Agile's roots were never purely software

Many of the ideas behind Agile came from outside software:

  • Scrum's inspiration came from hardware. Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka's 1986 Harvard Business Review article, "The New New Product Development Game", described a "rugby" approach in which a cross-functional team moves forward together. The products they studied included the Fuji-Xerox FX-3500 copier, the Canon PC-10 personal copier and AE-1 camera, the Honda City car and the NEC PC 8000 personal computer.
  • Lean and Kanban came from manufacturing. Flow, waste reduction, pull systems and visual boards grew out of the Toyota Production System. See our guide to Lean manufacturing.
  • Iterative development predates the Manifesto. Iterative and incremental approaches were used on engineering programmes from the late 1950s, decades before the word Agile was adopted. Our history of Agile gives the full timeline.

The 2020 Scrum Guide is explicit about this. Its authors write that Scrum is being adopted "in many domains holding essentially complex work, beyond software product development where Scrum has its roots", and that "we use the word 'developers' in Scrum not to exclude, but to simplify. If you get value from Scrum, consider yourself included." The 2020 revision also removed software-specific terminology. Our guide to what Scrum is explains the framework itself.

Translating the Agile Manifesto for non-software work

The four values and twelve principles translate with small changes of wording. This table shows the parts most often questioned by non-software teams.

Original wordingGeneral versionExample outside software

Working software is the primary measure of progressUsable outcomes are the primary measure of progressRoles filled, not CVs screened; a policy piloted, not drafted
Deliver working software frequentlyDeliver usable results frequentlyPublish one module of a new course every two weeks for learner feedback
Customer collaboration over contract negotiationUnchangedFinance business partners reviewing forecast drafts with budget holders
Responding to change over following a planUnchangedMoving campaign budget to the channel that is working this month
Continuous attention to technical excellenceContinuous attention to craft and qualityConsistent brand, legal and accessibility standards

When Agile works outside software, and when it does not

Agile is a tool, not a goal. Use this test to judge whether it suits a piece of work, whatever the industry.

QuestionPoints towards AgilePoints towards a predictive plan
How well is the result understood?Requirements will emerge or changeRequirements are known and stable
Can the work be split into usable pieces?Yes, each piece gives value or learningNo, value only exists when everything is finished
How quickly can you get feedback?Days or weeksOnly at the end
How expensive is a change after work starts?Low: content, process, design, prototypesHigh: concrete, tooling, regulated submissions
Is there a fixed external sequence?Little mandated sequenceRegulatory or physical steps must happen in order
Is the team stable and able to decide?Dedicated, cross-functional teamMany part-time contributors and sign-off layers

Most real projects mix both columns. Building a new office is predictive at its core, but the workplace design, move communications and IT set-up can be run iteratively. Launching a regulated product has fixed submission steps, but marketing content and training can be built in sprints. A hybrid plan, with a predictive backbone and Agile delivery inside it, is often the right answer. Our Agile vs Waterfall guide compares the two approaches in detail.

Tip

A useful rule: use Agile where learning is the main risk, and predictive planning where coordination and sequence are the main risk.

Government

In the UK, the government's Service Standard, which public services are assessed against, includes point 7, "Use agile ways of working", and point 8, "Iterate and improve frequently". In the US, the Digital Services Playbook's fourth play is "Build the service using agile and iterative practices". These standards are written for digital services, but the teams applying them include policy specialists, content designers, user researchers and operations staff, not only developers. Our guide to government project management covers the wider context.

Hardware and manufacturing

Physical products have long lead times, so teams iterate on what can change quickly: designs, simulations, prototypes and test plans. One frequently cited example is Saab's Gripen fighter programme. According to the book Doing Agile Right (Rigby, Elk and Berez, 2020), as reported by Acquisition Talk, Saab's aeronautics business created more than 100 Agile teams across software, hardware and fuselage, coordinated through a common rhythm of three-week sprints.

Marketing

Marketing is one of the most established non-software uses. The current Agile Marketing Manifesto, finalised in 2021, adapts the Agile values to campaigns, with five values and ten principles. See our guide to Agile marketing for a two-week sprint example.

What changes when you use Agile outside software

The principles stay the same, but the vocabulary and some practices need translating. This table maps common software terms to non-software equivalents.

Software termNon-software equivalentExample
Working software / incrementA usable result that can get feedbackA published article, a piloted process, a prototype, interim research findings
User storyA need from the customer's point of view"As a new starter, I want my laptop ready on day one so I can start training"
Definition of DoneThe quality checks every item must passBrand approved, legal reviewed, accessibility checked, published and tracked
DevelopersThe people doing the workWriters, recruiters, analysts, designers, engineers
ReleaseMaking results available to customersLaunching a campaign, rolling out a policy, opening a course
BugA defect or error in delivered workA wrong price in a brochure, a broken form, an unclear policy clause
Technical debtShortcuts that slow future workOutdated templates, undocumented processes, messy shared drives
  • Roles may be part-time. A small marketing or HR team rarely has a full-time Scrum Master; one team member or a shared coach often takes the role. See the Scrum Master role.
  • Cadence follows the work. Two-week sprints suit campaign or content work; a continuous flow with weekly replenishment suits request-driven teams.
  • Metrics change. Velocity in story points matters less; lead time, throughput and outcome measures such as conversion, time to hire or forecast accuracy matter more.
  • Some work stays predictive. Contracts, procurement, regulatory steps and physical lead times still need dates and dependencies.

KanbanScrumHybrid (predictive plan with Agile delivery)

CadenceContinuous, with regular review meetingsFixed sprints of one month or lessMilestones plus sprints or a Kanban flow
RolesNo new roles requiredProduct Owner, Scrum Master, DevelopersProject manager plus Agile roles inside teams
ChangeNew work can enter whenever capacity frees upChanges wait for the next sprint unless urgentChange control for the backbone; backlog for the rest

Kanban is usually the easiest start because you begin with the process you already have and improve it. Scrum works well when a team does project-like work with a clear owner who can set priorities. Worked example: an HR recruitment team on Kanban

This illustrative example shows how a three-person recruitment team supporting a 400-person organisation might move to Kanban. Before the change, the team had 18 open vacancies, all nominally "in progress", and hiring managers chased individual recruiters for updates.

Step 1: Map the workflow onto a board

Each card is one vacancy. The columns follow the real process, with explicit entry criteria so everyone knows when a card can move.

ColumnA card can enter whenWIP limit
ApprovedBudget and headcount are approvedNone
Role brief agreedHiring manager has signed off the job description and selection criteria3
AdvertisingThe advert is live on the agreed channels6
ShortlistingThe advert has closed or enough applicants have been received4
InterviewingInterview panel and dates are confirmed4
OfferA preferred candidate has been selected3
HiredOffer accepted and start date agreedNone

Step 2: Limit work in progress

With WIP limits in place, the team stops starting new vacancies when a column is full and helps move existing ones forward instead, for example by chasing interview feedback. Lower-priority vacancies wait in Approved, visibly, so hiring managers can see the queue and the team can agree priorities with them. Our guide to WIP limits explains how to set them.

Step 3: Agree a review rhythm

  • Twice-weekly 15-minute stand-up at the board, focused on blocked and ageing cards.
  • Fortnightly replenishment with HR business partners to agree which approved vacancies start next.
  • Monthly retrospective on the process, such as how to shorten the time between interview and offer.

Step 4: Measure flow and outcomes

The team tracks time to hire (from Approved to Hired), how many vacancies are filled each month, and how long cards sit in each column. If cards wait longest in Interviewing, the bottleneck is panel availability, not recruiter effort, and the fix is to book interview slots in advance. These measures replace the old status spreadsheet and give hiring managers a realistic forecast. A free Kanban board template is enough to start.

Worked example: a hybrid plan for a product launch event

This illustrative example shows a hybrid approach for a product launch event 14 weeks away. Some parts are fixed and must be planned predictively; the rest is uncertain and benefits from sprints.

The predictive backbone

WeekFixed milestoneWhy it is fixed
1Venue contract signedAvailability and cancellation terms
4Registration opensAnnounced publicly with the product teaser
6Speakers confirmedNeeded for the agenda and travel bookings
11Print and signage deadlineSupplier lead time
14Event dayFixed date

Agile delivery inside it

Content, the registration campaign and the attendee experience are run in seven two-week sprints by a small cross-functional team: a marketer, a content writer, a designer and an events coordinator, with the marketing lead as product owner.

  • Sprint 2 goal: registration page and first email live, with tracking in place to see which messages drive sign-ups.
  • Sprint 3 goal: double down on the best-performing channel and test two new agenda descriptions.
  • Sprint 5 goal: final agenda published and the attendee app content ready for review.

Backlog items are written as needs, for example "As a prospective attendee, I want to see who is speaking so I can decide whether to attend". The team's Definition of Done for any public item is: copy proofread, brand approved, accessibility checked, links tested, published and tracked. Each sprint review is held with the sales and product leads, who see real registration data and can redirect effort.

The project manager tracks the five milestones in a simple plan and checks at each sprint review that the Agile work is on course to meet them. If a sprint threatens the print deadline, scope is cut (fewer printed items, more digital) rather than moving the date. Our sprint planning template and user story template support this kind of plan.

How to start using Agile outside software

  1. Pick one team and one type of work where uncertainty or frequent change is causing pain. Avoid starting with work that is fixed and well understood.
  2. Make the current work visible on a simple board with columns that match your real process.
  3. Agree what "done" means for your work: the checks and approvals every item must pass.
  4. Limit work in progress so the team finishes before starting more.
  5. Find the smallest useful result you can deliver in one to two weeks, and show it to the customer or stakeholder.
  6. Set a rhythm: a short planning meeting, brief check-ins and a regular review with stakeholders.
  7. Hold a retrospective every two to four weeks and change one thing at a time.
  8. Measure outcomes, not only activity, and share what you learn with other teams before scaling up.

A six-week pilot might look like this:

WeeksFocusOutput
1Map the workflow and build the boardBoard with all current work on it
2Agree the Definition of Done and WIP limitsWritten team policies
3First review with stakeholdersFeedback on delivered items
4First sprint retrospectiveOne or two process changes
5Start measuring lead time and throughputBaseline flow measures
6Pilot review with leadershipDecision to continue, adjust or extend

Common mistakes when applying Agile beyond software

  • Copying software practices literally, such as story points and two-week sprints for a team that handles a continuous stream of requests.
  • Renaming without changing: calling the weekly meeting a stand-up and the to-do list a backlog, while work is still planned and approved the old way.
  • No real customer feedback, so increments are reviewed only by the team and the benefit of iteration is lost.
  • Forcing Agile onto fixed work, such as statutory reporting with set content and dates, where a checklist and a plan work better.
  • Ignoring sign-off layers: if every item needs three approvals, the approvals are the bottleneck and must be part of the redesign.
  • Starting too big, with an organisation-wide transformation before one team has shown what works. See Agile transformation.
  • Measuring busyness (tasks completed) instead of outcomes for the customer.

Tools for non-software Agile teams

Non-software teams usually need visual boards, simple forms for incoming requests, approvals and reporting more than developer features. Wrike, our top-rated tool, combines boards, request forms, Gantt charts and dashboards, which suits marketing, operations and PMO teams running hybrid work across departments. monday.com has colour-coded boards that non-technical teams adopt quickly. Asana is easy to roll out across departments and supports boards and sprint-style projects. Trello is a simple way to start with Kanban.

Jira supports almost every Agile software practice, but for a team whose work is mainly non-technical it is usually more tool than it needs. Whatever you choose, start with a board that matches your process and add features only when the team needs them.

Frequently asked questions

Is Agile only for software development?

No. Agile started in software, but marketing, HR, finance, legal, operations, education, government, hardware and construction teams all use Agile practices. It suits any work that is uncertain, can be split into usable pieces and benefits from early feedback.

Can Scrum be used for non-software projects?

Yes. The 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum is used in many domains of complex work beyond software, and that the term developers is meant to include anyone doing the work. It suits project-like work with a clear product owner.

Which Agile framework is best for non-software teams?

Kanban is often the easiest to start with, especially for teams handling a continuous flow of requests. Scrum works well for goal-driven project work. Many teams use a hybrid of a predictive plan with Agile delivery.

What are examples of Agile outside software?

Marketing campaigns run as experiments, recruitment pipelines on Kanban boards, rolling financial forecasts, government services built with users, hardware prototypes in short iterations and Last Planner weekly planning in construction.

When should you not use Agile?

When the work is well understood and stable, cannot be split into usable pieces, is very expensive to change once started, or must follow a fixed regulatory or physical sequence. A predictive or hybrid plan usually fits better there.

What does an increment look like outside software?

Anything usable that generates feedback: a published article, a tested campaign, a piloted process, a course module, a prototype or interim research findings.

How do you adapt the Definition of Done for non-software work?

Replace software checks with the quality steps your work needs, such as proofreading, legal review, brand approval, accessibility or safety checks. See our Definition of Done guide.

Do non-software Agile teams need a Scrum Master?

Not always. Kanban requires no new roles. Small teams using Scrum often give the Scrum Master accountability to a team member or share a coach across several teams.

Can HR and finance teams use Agile?

Yes. HR teams often run recruitment and onboarding on Kanban boards and pilot new policies with one department. Finance teams use rolling forecasts and fund persistent teams rather than individual projects.

Is Agile used in government?

Yes. The UK government's Service Standard asks teams to use agile ways of working and iterate frequently, and the US Digital Services Playbook includes building services using agile and iterative practices.

Can Agile work in manufacturing and hardware development?

Yes. Teams iterate on designs, simulations and prototypes, while Lean and Kanban are long established on the shop floor. Saab's Gripen programme is a frequently cited example of Agile at scale in hardware.

What is business agility?

The ability of a whole organisation, not just its delivery teams, to sense change and respond quickly with new products, services or ways of working, supported by funding, governance and leadership that allow it.

Do you need special software to use Agile outside software?

No. Many teams start with a whiteboard, sticky notes or a shared spreadsheet. Project management tools help once work is shared across locations or teams, because they keep the board, requests and reporting in one place.

Is Agile only for small teams?

No. Agile teams are usually small (the Scrum Guide suggests 10 or fewer people), but larger organisations coordinate many such teams, as Saab does with a common sprint rhythm. Scaling frameworks exist for this, though most non-software teams should start with one team.

How long does it take a non-software team to adopt Agile?

A single team can see whether basic practices help within a six- to eight-week pilot. Changing approvals, funding and culture across an organisation takes much longer and works best when built on early team results.
