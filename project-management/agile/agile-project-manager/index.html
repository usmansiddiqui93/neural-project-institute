The Agile Project Manager: Role, Skills and Career Path

An Agile project manager coordinates the delivery of work done by Agile teams: aligning it with business goals, managing budget, risk and dependencies, and reporting progress to sponsors, while the teams themselves plan and organise their own work. Scrum has no project manager role, yet many organisations still employ Agile project managers, especially where several teams, suppliers or governance requirements are involved. This guide explains what the role involves, how it differs from the Scrum Master and a traditional project manager, and how to move into it, with worked examples.

Key takeaways

  • An Agile project manager coordinates delivery around self-managing teams: funding, budget, dependencies, risk, governance and reporting.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide has no project manager. In Scrum, project management work is shared between the Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers, so the role usually sits outside or across Scrum Teams.
  • The role is most useful with several teams, external suppliers, fixed budgets or dates, regulation, or hybrid projects that mix predictive and Agile work.
  • Agile project managers lead through facilitation and service. They forecast from real team data and report working increments and trends, not percentage complete.
  • PMI-ACP, PMP, PRINCE2 Agile and Scrum certifications are all relevant; hands-on experience on an Agile team matters most.

What is an Agile project manager?

An Agile project manager is a project manager who delivers outcomes through Agile teams rather than by directing tasks. They create the conditions for teams to succeed: clear goals, secured funding, managed dependencies and risks, and transparent reporting. They plan in layers, forecast from the team's actual delivery data and adapt the plan as the work reveals new information.

Two things make the role confusing. First, the Scrum Guide does not include it: a Scrum Team consists of a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and Developers, and the project management work inside the team is shared between them. Second, organisations use the title for quite different jobs, from a delivery lead for one team to a programme manager coordinating many.

PMI's Agile Practice Guide describes the project manager in Agile settings as a servant leader whose emphasis shifts from directing work to coaching, removing obstacles and helping collaboration across the organisation. That is a useful summary of the mindset. In practice, Agile project managers are most common in:

  • organisations running several Agile teams that depend on each other;
  • projects with a fixed budget, a contractual date or external suppliers;
  • regulated sectors such as finance, healthcare and government, where governance and audit evidence are required;
  • hybrid projects that combine predictive work (procurement, construction, hardware) with Agile software delivery;
  • companies part-way through an Agile transformation, where existing project managers take on new responsibilities.

What does an Agile project manager do?

The responsibilities below are typical. A single-team role will lean towards the first few; a programme role will lean towards funding, governance and cross-team coordination.

1. Align the work with business goals and secure funding

The Agile project manager makes the case for the work, often through a business case or project charter, agrees the objectives with the sponsor and secures funding. In Agile organisations funding is increasingly allocated to long-lived teams or value streams rather than fixed-scope projects, and the project manager helps translate between the two models.

2. Coordinate teams, suppliers and dependencies

When several teams contribute to one release, someone has to make cross-team dependencies visible and resolve conflicts early. Agile project managers run or support dependency boards, Scrum of Scrums meetings and joint planning, and they manage supplier relationships and contracts.

3. Manage the budget

Agile teams usually have a stable size, so cost per Sprint is predictable. The project manager tracks spend against value delivered, forecasts the cost of remaining work and flags early when budget and scope no longer match.

4. Manage risks, issues and decisions

Team-level impediments belong to the team and the Scrum Master. The Agile project manager handles risks that cross teams or need management attention: supplier delays, regulatory change, funding decisions, organisational blockers. A RAID log keeps risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies visible.

5. Forecast and report progress

Instead of percentage complete, the Agile project manager reports working increments, burnup charts, forecast ranges and outcome measures, and explains what they mean for dates and budget.

6. Satisfy governance without slowing teams down

Stage gates, steering committees and audit requirements do not disappear in Agile organisations. A good Agile project manager meets them with evidence the teams already produce, such as Sprint Review outcomes, the Definition of Done and release records, rather than asking teams to create extra documents.

7. Support the team and the Product Owner

They help the Product Owner with stakeholder management, protect the team from disruption and remove organisational obstacles. They do not assign tasks or decide how the work is done.

Can a Scrum Master be the project manager?

It is possible but usually unwise. The Scrum Master's accountability is the effectiveness of the Scrum Team, and they need the team's trust to coach it. Combining that with budget control and reporting to sponsors can make the Scrum Master look like a supervisor, which discourages openness. If one person must do both, agree clearly which hat they are wearing in each meeting.

When does an organisation need an Agile project manager?

Not every Agile team needs one. A single, stable Scrum Team with an empowered Product Owner and an internal sponsor can usually manage itself. The role earns its place as complexity grows.

You probably need one if…You probably do not if…
Several teams must deliver one integrated releaseOne team owns the whole product
External suppliers or contracts are involvedAll work is done in-house
The budget or date is fixed by contract or regulationFunding is stable and dates are flexible
Governance requires stage gates or audit evidenceGovernance is light and trust-based
The project mixes predictive and Agile workAll work suits iterative delivery
The Product Owner is overloaded with stakeholder managementThe Product Owner has capacity and authority
Tip

If you are introducing the role, write down what it is accountable for and what it is not. Most conflict with Scrum Masters and Product Owners comes from unclear boundaries, not bad intentions.

How an Agile project manager plans and forecasts: a worked example

Agile projects still need answers to "when will it be ready?" and "how much will it cost?". The difference is that the answers come from the teams' real delivery data and are updated every Sprint. The example below is illustrative.

Scenario: two Scrum Teams of seven people are building a new customer portal. The sponsor needs a release in 12 weeks for a contract renewal and has approved a budget of £450,000. Sprints are two weeks long, so there are six Sprints left.

Step-by-step forecasting process

  1. Agree the release goal and order the scope. With the Product Owner, split the backlog into Must have, Should have and Could have. Here the Must haves total 260 story points and the full backlog is 330 points.
  2. Collect recent velocity. Combined velocity over the last six Sprints was 52, 61, 48, 57, 55 and 59 points. Use a range, not a single number: lowest 48, highest 61.
  3. Forecast a range. Multiply by the six Sprints remaining: a pessimistic 6 × 48 = 288 points and an optimistic 6 × 61 = 366 points.
  4. Compare with scope. All Must haves (260) fit even in the pessimistic case. The full backlog (330) is likely but not certain.
  5. Calculate the cost per Sprint. 14 people × an assumed blended rate of £500 a day × 10 working days = £70,000 per Sprint. Six Sprints cost £420,000; adding £15,000 for environments and licences gives £435,000, leaving £15,000 of contingency.
  6. Communicate the forecast honestly. Tell the sponsor that Must haves are expected by the date, Should haves are likely, and Could haves depend on the next few Sprints.
  7. Update every Sprint. Plot completed work and total scope on a burnup chart and refresh the forecast after each Sprint Review.

Pessimistic48288DeliveredNot all delivered

If scope grows during the project, the burnup chart shows the total-scope line rising, which makes the trade-off visible: add time, add budget or move items out. That conversation belongs with the Product Owner and sponsor, supported by the project manager's data.

Reporting progress the Agile way

Sponsors still want to know whether the project is on track. Agile project managers answer with evidence rather than estimates of percentage complete.

Instead of…Report…Why
% complete per taskIncrements accepted at the Sprint ReviewWorking product is the clearest measure of progress
A single forecast dateA forecast range with confidence levelsShows uncertainty honestly
Tasks completedBurnup of scope delivered against total scopeMakes scope change visible
Hours spentSpend against budget and value deliveredLinks cost to outcome
Red/amber/green opinionTrends in throughput, cycle time and escaped defectsData that teams already produce

Example of a one-page status update

  • Sprint 4 of 6 complete. Increment accepted: customer login and account dashboard.
  • Forecast: Must haves on track for the release date; two Should haves at risk.
  • Budget: £280,000 spent of £450,000; on plan.
  • Top risks: payment provider API change (mitigation agreed); security review booking (escalated to sponsor).
  • Decisions needed: confirm whether the reporting module can move to the next release.

Running hybrid projects

Many Agile project managers work on hybrid projects, where some parts of the work suit a predictive plan and others suit iterative delivery. The skill is deciding which approach fits each part and integrating them through shared milestones.

Example: opening a new customer contact centre.

WorkstreamApproachWhyHow it is managed
Building fit-outPredictiveFixed design, contractor dependencies, little benefit from iterationGantt chart, contractor milestones, change control
Telephony procurementPredictiveFormal tender and contractProcurement plan and supplier contract
CRM configurationAgile (Scrum)Requirements will emerge as agents test the systemTwo-week Sprints, Product Owner from operations
Staff trainingIterativeContent improves after each pilot groupShort cycles with feedback after each cohort

The Agile project manager sets integration milestones (for example, "CRM ready for first training cohort") that both the predictive plan and the Agile release plan work towards, and manages the dependencies between them.

How to become an Agile project manager

Most Agile project managers come from one of two directions: traditional project managers learning Agile, or Scrum Masters and team leads taking on broader delivery responsibility.

  1. Learn the foundations of both worlds. Understand predictive project management (scope, schedule, cost, risk, governance) and Agile values, principles and frameworks.
  2. Work on an Agile team. Join a Scrum or Kanban team in any role. Nothing replaces seeing how self-managing teams plan, estimate and improve.
  3. Practise facilitation. Run retrospectives, planning sessions and workshops. Ask for feedback on how you facilitate.
  4. Learn to forecast from data. Track velocity or throughput and build a burnup forecast for a real release.
  5. Take a recognised certification. Choose PMI-ACP, PRINCE2 Agile or a Scrum certification depending on your background and local job market.
  6. Take on cross-team work. Volunteer to coordinate a dependency, a supplier or a multi-team release.
  7. Build a record of outcomes. Describe projects by the value delivered and how you helped teams achieve it, not by the size of the plan you managed.

Frequently asked questions

What does an Agile project manager do?

Aligns work with business goals, secures funding, coordinates dependencies between teams and suppliers, manages budget and risk, forecasts and reports progress, meets governance needs and removes organisational obstacles.

Is there an Agile project manager in Scrum?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide defines the Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers only. Project management work inside a Scrum Team is shared between them, but many organisations still have Agile project managers working across or outside Scrum Teams.

Is an Agile project manager the same as a Scrum Master?

No. The Scrum Master is accountable for one Scrum Team's effectiveness and has no authority over the work. An Agile project manager usually coordinates delivery, budget, risk and stakeholders, often across several teams.

How is an Agile project manager different from a traditional one?

The Agile project manager facilitates layered, adaptive planning and supports a self-managing team, rather than creating a detailed plan upfront and assigning tasks. Progress is shown through working increments and forecasts instead of percentage complete.

What skills does an Agile project manager need?

Knowledge of Agile frameworks, forecasting from data, budgeting and contracts, risk and dependency management, facilitation, stakeholder management, servant leadership and comfort with changing plans.

Can a traditional project manager become an Agile project manager?

Yes. Learn the frameworks, work on an Agile team, practise facilitation, learn to forecast from velocity or throughput, and shift from directing tasks to setting goals and removing obstacles.

What is a hybrid project manager compared with an Agile project manager?

A hybrid project manager deliberately combines predictive planning for some workstreams with Agile delivery for others. Many Agile project managers work this way, particularly in regulated or large organisations.

How do Agile project managers report progress?

With increments accepted at the Sprint Review, burnup charts, forecast ranges, spend against budget, key risks and the decisions needed from sponsors.

What is PMI-ACP for an Agile project manager?

PMI's Agile Certified Practitioner credential, covering Scrum, Kanban, Lean and other approaches. PMI requires Agile experience (or an active PMP) and 21 hours of Agile training.

Does the PMP cover what an Agile project manager needs?

Partly. PMI's 2026 exam content outline says about 40% of questions represent predictive approaches and the rest Agile and hybrid. See PMP certification.

Do Agile project managers need certification?

Not always, but PMI-ACP, PRINCE2 Agile or a Scrum certification helps show your knowledge, especially when changing roles.

Does an Agile project manager manage the team's tasks?

No. Developers decide how to do the work and who does which task. The Agile project manager works on the conditions around the team: goals, budget, dependencies and risks.

What is the difference between an Agile project manager and an Agile product manager?

The product manager decides what to build and why and is measured on customer and business outcomes. The project manager makes sure agreed work is delivered within its constraints. See Agile product manager.
