Agile Marketing: What It Is, Examples and How to Start

Agile marketing is a way of running marketing in short cycles, using small experiments, a single prioritised backlog and frequent reviews of real results to decide what to do next. Instead of betting a quarter's budget on one big campaign, an Agile marketing team launches the smallest useful version, measures it and doubles down on what works. This guide covers the Agile Marketing Manifesto, the main frameworks, team set-up, a worked sprint example, metrics and a step-by-step plan to get started.

Key takeaways

  • Agile marketing applies Agile ways of working to marketing: short cycles, one ordered backlog, cross-functional teams and decisions based on data.
  • The current Agile Marketing Manifesto, finalised in 2021, has five values and ten principles, starting with customer value and business outcomes over activity and outputs.
  • Scrum suits goal-driven campaigns and launches; Kanban suits continuous request streams such as content, social and design; many teams combine them as Scrumban.
  • Good Sprint Goals describe a customer or business outcome, such as more trial sign-ups, not a list of assets.
  • Protect the team with a single intake route for requests and a reserved share of capacity for urgent work.
  • Start with one pilot team, a visible board and two-week cycles, then improve the process at every retrospective.

What is Agile marketing?

Agile marketing is an approach in which marketing teams plan and deliver work in short cycles, test ideas with small experiments, and use data and customer feedback to decide what to do next. Work is held in one prioritised backlog, delivered by a small cross-functional team, and reviewed with stakeholders every one to two weeks.

The idea comes from Agile software development, which favoured working results, customer collaboration and responding to change over rigid plans. Marketers saw the same problems in their own work: long planning cycles, campaigns launched with no chance to adjust, and teams overloaded with requests from every direction. Our guide to whether Agile is only for software shows how far these ideas have spread.

In practice, an Agile marketing team does five things differently:

  • Works in short cycles. Plans one or two weeks at a time, or manages a continuous flow of work with limits on how much is in progress.
  • Tests before scaling. Launches small versions of ideas to a slice of the audience, measures them, then scales what works.
  • Keeps one backlog. Collects every campaign idea, request and experiment in one ordered list instead of many inboxes.
  • Works cross-functionally. Content, design, channel and analytics specialists work on the same goal at the same time.
  • Reviews results often. Shows stakeholders real outcomes every cycle and changes the plan when the data says so.

Agile marketing does not mean having no plan. Strategy, brand guidelines, budgets and annual goals still matter. What changes is how far ahead you commit to detail: you plan the direction for the year, the priorities for the quarter and the detailed work for the next cycle only.

The Agile Marketing Manifesto

The Agile Marketing Manifesto adapts the software Agile Manifesto for marketing. According to the manifesto's official history, it developed in three stages:

EventDateWhat happened
SprintZero11 June 2012, San FranciscoAbout 35 marketers drafted a first version with seven values and ten principles, always intended as a draft
SprintOne12 May 2017, San FranciscoFocused on helping marketers adopt Agile; its main lasting result was the ICAgile Agility in Marketing certification
SprintTwo8 and 9 September 2021, virtualAttendees voted on and finalised five values; the ten principles were finalised in October 2021

Many articles still describe only the 2012 draft. The current version is the 2021 one below.

The five values

Each value is written as "X over Y": both sides matter, but the left side matters more. The wording below is quoted from the manifesto; the explanations are ours.

Value (verbatim)What it means in practice
Focusing on customer value and business outcomes over activity and outputsMeasure leads, revenue and retention, not how many posts or emails went out
Delivering value early and often over waiting for perfectionLaunch a simple version this week and improve it, rather than a polished one next quarter
Learning through experiments and data over opinions and conventionsSettle debates with a test, not with seniority or "we always do it this way"
Cross-functional collaboration over silos and hierarchiesWriters, designers and channel experts work on the same goal together
Responding to change over following a static planShift budget and effort when results, competitors or the market change

The ten principles

  1. Great marketing requires close alignment, transparency, and quality interactions with internal and external customers.
  2. Seek out different and diverse points of view.
  3. Embrace and respond to change to enhance customer value.
  4. Plan only to a level sufficient to ensure effective prioritization and execution.
  5. Take chances, and learn from your failures.
  6. Organize in small, cross-functional teams where possible.
  7. Build marketing programs around motivated individuals and trust them to get the job done.
  8. Long-term marketing success benefits from operating at a sustainable pace.
  9. Agile marketing isn't enough. Excellence in marketing requires continuous attention to marketing fundamentals as well.
  10. Strive for simplicity.

The ninth principle is worth highlighting: Agile is a way of organising the work, not a replacement for marketing skill, positioning or brand discipline. To see where these ideas came from, read the history of Agile.

Benefits and challenges of Agile marketing

Agile marketing brings real advantages, and it also asks teams and leaders to change habits. Knowing both sides helps you set realistic expectations.

Benefits

  • Faster learning. Small launches show what works within days, not at the end of a quarter.
  • Less wasted spend. Budget moves towards channels and messages that are proving themselves.
  • Clear priorities. One ordered backlog makes trade-offs visible, so the team stops trying to do everything at once.
  • Better collaboration. Cross-functional work cuts hand-offs and rework between content, design and channel teams.
  • Transparency for stakeholders. Sales and leadership can see what is being worked on and why.
  • A sustainable pace. Work-in-progress limits and capacity planning reduce last-minute fire-fighting.

Challenges

  • Stakeholder habits. Leaders used to fixed annual plans may find a changing plan uncomfortable. Show results every cycle to build trust.
  • Specialist bottlenecks. A single designer or video editor can limit the whole team. Make the bottleneck visible and plan around it.
  • Agency and approval lead times. Legal review, print and media buying do not always fit two-week cycles. Plan them earlier and track them as dependencies.
  • Measuring outcomes. Some outcomes, such as brand awareness, take longer to show. Pair them with faster leading indicators.
  • Process for its own sake. Copying every Scrum event without adapting it to marketing creates overhead with little benefit.

Agile marketing frameworks: Scrum, Kanban and Scrumban

Most Agile marketing teams use one of three frameworks, or a blend of them.

Scrum for marketing

Scrum organises work into fixed sprints of one month or less, most often two weeks, each with a Sprint Goal. The team holds Sprint Planning, short daily check-ins, a Sprint Review with stakeholders and a retrospective. Scrum works well for campaigns, launches and growth experiments where a clear goal for each cycle helps focus.

Kanban for marketing

Kanban visualises work on a board and limits how many items can be in progress at once, so work flows steadily from request to done. There are no fixed sprints; the team pulls new work when capacity frees up. Kanban suits teams that handle a continuous stream of requests, such as content production, social media, design services and marketing operations. Setting good WIP limits is the key skill.

Scrumban

Scrumban combines a Kanban board and WIP limits with some Scrum events, such as regular planning and retrospectives. Many marketing teams settle here because it gives them a planning rhythm without forcing every request into a sprint.

Tip

Not sure which to choose? If most of your work is planned campaigns with clear goals, start with Scrum. If most of it arrives as requests from other teams, start with Kanban. You can change once you have a few cycles of data.

Roles and team structure

Agile marketing works best with small, stable, cross-functional teams of around three to nine people. The roles below borrow from Scrum but can be adapted.

RoleWhat they do in Agile marketingOften filled by
Marketing owner (Product Owner)Owns the backlog and its order, sets goals with stakeholders, accepts finished workMarketing manager, campaign lead or head of a channel
Facilitator (Scrum Master or Agile coach)Runs the events, removes blockers, coaches the team and protects its focusTeam lead, marketing operations manager or a dedicated coach
Team membersPlan, create, launch and measure the workContent writers, designers, channel and performance specialists, analysts
StakeholdersBring requests and context, attend reviews, use the resultsSales, product, leadership, regional teams

Common team structures

  • One cross-functional pod. A single team with every skill needed to launch a campaign end to end. Good for small marketing departments.
  • Pods by customer journey or segment. For example, an acquisition pod, a customer-retention pod and a partner-marketing pod, each with its own backlog and goal.
  • A service team plus pods. Shared specialists such as video or brand design run a Kanban service that several pods pull from.

Building an Agile marketing backlog

The backlog is the single ordered list of everything the team might work on. A good marketing backlog is short at the top, detailed for the next cycle and rough further down.

What goes in it

  • Campaign work: landing pages, emails, ads, events, launch content.
  • Experiments: tests of new channels, offers, messages or audiences, each with a hypothesis.
  • Requests: work for sales, product or other teams, arriving through one intake route.
  • Always-on work: blog posts, social content, SEO updates, newsletters.
  • Improvements: fixing tracking, updating templates, automating reports.

Writing marketing user stories

Many teams write backlog items as user stories, describing who benefits and why. Two examples:

  • "As a trial user who has not logged in for a week, I want a short email showing one feature I have not tried, so that I see value before my trial ends."
  • "As a sales rep, I want a one-page comparison against our main competitor, so that I can answer the question in first calls."

For experiments, a hypothesis format works better: "We believe that a shorter sign-up form for visitors from paid search will increase trial sign-ups. We will know it worked when the conversion rate rises by at least 10 per cent over two weeks."

Prioritising the backlog

Order items by expected value against effort, with urgency and dependencies as tie-breakers. Simple scoring helps: rate impact and confidence from 1 to 5, divide by an effort estimate, and use the result to start a conversation, not to replace judgement. Spend a short session each week on backlog refinement so the next cycle's items are clear before planning.

How an Agile marketing sprint works

Here is a typical two-week marketing sprint for a team using Scrum or Scrumban. Kanban teams run the same activities without the fixed start and end.

  1. Sprint Planning (up to 2 hours). The marketing owner explains the priorities, the team agrees one Sprint Goal and selects the items it can finish, allowing for leave, meetings and reserved capacity.
  2. Daily stand-up (15 minutes). The team checks progress towards the goal, flags blockers such as late approvals, and adjusts the day's plan.
  3. Create and launch. The team builds the smallest version that can be tested, gets it reviewed and launches it within the sprint wherever possible.
  4. Measure. Results are tracked against the goal as soon as data arrives, not only at the end.
  5. Refine. A short mid-sprint session prepares items for the next sprint.
  6. Sprint Review (up to 1 hour). The team shows stakeholders what launched and what the data shows, and agrees what to keep, change or stop.
  7. Retrospective (up to 1 hour). The team picks one or two improvements to how it works.

Two weeks is a good default for marketing: long enough to launch and gather some data, short enough to react. Some social and performance teams use one-week cycles. Learn more about how sprints work.

Worked example: a two-week campaign sprint

A B2B software company has a five-person growth marketing team: a marketing owner, a content writer, a designer, a performance marketer and a marketing analyst who also facilitates. The figures below are illustrative.

Sprint Goal

"Increase free-trial sign-ups from paid search and the pricing page, and learn which message works best for operations managers."

Sprint Backlog

ItemTypeSize (points)Owner
Two landing-page variants for paid search: "save time" vs "reduce errors"Experiment5Writer and designer
Shorter sign-up form on the pricing page (5 fields to 3)Experiment3Designer and analyst
New ad set targeting operations managersCampaign3Performance marketer
Re-engagement email for inactive trial usersCampaign3Writer
Competitor comparison page for salesRequest5Writer and designer
Fix sign-up tracking on mobileImprovement2Analyst
Reserved for urgent requestsBufferAbout 15% of capacityWhole team

The team's recent average is around 21 points per sprint, so it commits to 21 points of planned work and keeps a small buffer.

During the sprint

  • Day 2: tracking fix goes live first, because every experiment depends on it.
  • Day 4: both landing-page variants and the new ad set launch, splitting paid-search traffic evenly.
  • Day 6: sales asks for an urgent webinar invitation; it fits within the buffer, so nothing else moves.
  • Day 8: the shorter form launches on the pricing page.
  • Day 9: the competitor page is approved by product marketing and published.

Sprint Review

The team shows the results. In this example the "reduce errors" variant converts at 3.1 per cent against 2.4 per cent for "save time", and the shorter form is trending upwards but has too little data to call. The stakeholders agree to:

  • Move most paid-search budget to the "reduce errors" message and test it in the re-engagement email next sprint.
  • Leave the form test running for another week before deciding.
  • Add a sales-team request for a second comparison page to the backlog, ordered below the current experiments.

Retrospective

The team notices that two items waited two days for legal review. Its improvement action: agree a standing 24-hour review slot with the legal team for campaign copy.

Handling ad-hoc requests and urgent work

Unplanned requests are the most common reason marketing teams say Agile "doesn't work for us". A few simple rules solve most of the problem:

  1. One front door. All requests come through one intake form or channel, never direct messages to individual team members.
  2. Triage on a schedule. The marketing owner reviews new requests once or twice a week and places them in the backlog by value.
  3. Reserve capacity. Hold back a share of each sprint, often 10 to 20 per cent, for genuine urgent work. Adjust it based on how much you actually use.
  4. Define "urgent". Agree with stakeholders what qualifies for the expedite lane, for example a legal requirement or a press issue, and limit it to one item at a time.
  5. Make the cost visible. When an urgent item displaces planned work, show stakeholders what moved out so the trade-off is clear.

Teams with a high share of unplanned work often do better with Kanban than with Scrum. Our guide to Agile for creative teams covers protecting creative time and managing feedback loops.

Metrics for Agile marketing

Measure outcomes first, then the health of the process. Track a small set and review it at every Sprint Review.

CategoryExample measuresWhy it matters
OutcomesQualified leads, trial sign-ups, conversion rate, pipeline or revenue influenced, retentionShows whether the work is creating customer and business value
ExperimentsNumber of tests run, share that produced a clear learning, time from idea to resultShows how fast the team is learning
FlowCycle time from start to launch, throughput per week, work in progress, items blockedShows how smoothly work moves and where it gets stuck
PredictabilitySprint Goal achieved (yes/no), planned vs completed workHelps stakeholders trust the plan
Team and stakeholdersTeam health check, stakeholder satisfaction, unplanned work shareWarns of overload before it hurts results

Avoid counting outputs, such as number of posts or emails, as success measures on their own. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs explains how to calculate flow measures such as cycle time.

How to start Agile marketing: step by step

These steps are our recommended way to introduce Agile marketing with low risk. Expect the first few cycles to feel awkward; that is normal.

  1. Pick a pilot team and goal. Choose one team of three to nine people and one meaningful goal, such as a product launch or lead-generation target.
  2. Get leadership support. Agree with your manager that the pilot will run for about three months and that stakeholders will attend reviews.
  3. Map all current work. List every campaign, request and recurring task the team is doing. Most teams find far more in progress than they expected.
  4. Build one visible board. Put all the work on a single Kanban board with simple columns: backlog, ready, doing, review, done.
  5. Name a priority owner. One person decides the order of the backlog after listening to stakeholders.
  6. Choose a framework. Two-week Scrum sprints for goal-driven campaigns, Kanban with WIP limits for request-heavy work.
  7. Set up intake. Create one route for requests and agree triage times and what counts as urgent.
  8. Run the first cycle. Hold planning, daily stand-ups, a review and a retrospective. Keep the meetings short and practical.
  9. Review results and adjust. Use each retrospective to change one thing about how you work.
  10. Expand gradually. After three to six cycles, share what worked with other marketing teams and help them start their own pilots.

Tools for Agile marketing

You can start Agile marketing with sticky notes on a wall or a shared spreadsheet. As the team grows, a work management tool helps with request intake, approvals and reporting.

  • Wrike is a strong fit for marketing and creative departments: request forms, proofing and approvals, Kanban boards and cross-team reporting in one platform. It is our top-rated tool overall.
  • Asana and monday.com are easy for marketing teams to adopt, with boards, timelines and campaign templates.
  • Trello is a simple way to start with Kanban for small teams.

Common Agile marketing mistakes

  • Renaming the old process. Calling weekly status meetings "stand-ups" without changing how work is prioritised. Fix: start with one backlog and one priority owner.
  • Output goals. Sprint Goals such as "publish 10 posts". Fix: write goals as outcomes, such as "increase newsletter sign-ups from the blog".
  • No WIP limits. Starting everything and finishing little. Fix: limit work in progress and finish before starting.
  • Letting requests bypass the backlog. Side requests sent to individuals undermine planning. Fix: one front door and reserved capacity.
  • Experiments without hypotheses. Testing without saying what success looks like. Fix: write the hypothesis and the measure before launch.
  • Skipping the review. Launching work but never looking at results with stakeholders. Fix: make the Sprint Review the main reporting meeting.
  • Forgetting fundamentals. Running fast experiments with a weak brand or unclear positioning. Fix: follow the manifesto's ninth principle and keep marketing fundamentals strong.
  • Copying software Scrum exactly. Adopting every rule without adapting. Fix: keep the principles, adjust the practices, and review them at each retrospective.

Frequently asked questions

What is Agile marketing?

Agile marketing is an approach in which marketing teams work in short cycles, keep one prioritised backlog, test ideas with small experiments and use data and feedback to decide what to do next.

What are the values of the Agile Marketing Manifesto?

The 2021 version has five values: customer value and business outcomes over activity and outputs; delivering value early and often over waiting for perfection; learning through experiments and data over opinions and conventions; cross-functional collaboration over silos and hierarchies; and responding to change over following a static plan.

When was the Agile Marketing Manifesto created?

A first draft was written at SprintZero in San Francisco on 11 June 2012. The current five values and ten principles were finalised in 2021 after the SprintTwo event.

What is an example of Agile marketing?

A growth team sets a two-week goal of increasing trial sign-ups, launches two landing-page variants and a shorter sign-up form, reviews the results with stakeholders at the end of the sprint, and moves budget to the message that performed best.

How is Agile marketing different from traditional marketing?

Traditional marketing fixes detailed plans months ahead and launches a few large campaigns. Agile marketing plans detail one cycle at a time, launches many small experiments and adjusts priorities based on results.

Is Agile marketing the same as Scrum?

No. Scrum is one framework teams use for Agile marketing. Many marketing teams use Kanban or Scrumban instead, especially when much of their work arrives as requests.

How long should an Agile marketing sprint be?

One or two weeks is typical, and two weeks is a good default. The aim is a regular point to review results and re-plan.

Should Agile marketing teams use Scrum or Kanban?

Scrum suits campaigns and launches with clear goals; Kanban suits continuous requests such as content, social and design. Many teams combine them as Scrumban.

Does Agile marketing need a Scrum Master?

Not necessarily. Many teams start with a team lead or marketing operations manager facilitating. A dedicated coach helps larger teams or those adopting Scrum in full.

What metrics do Agile marketing teams track?

Outcome measures such as leads, conversions and revenue influenced, experiment measures such as tests run and learnings, and flow measures such as cycle time, throughput and work in progress.

How does Agile marketing handle ad-hoc requests?

Requests arrive through one intake route and are prioritised in the backlog. Teams reserve a share of capacity, often 10 to 20 per cent, for genuinely urgent work, with an agreed definition of urgent.

Is Agile marketing only for digital marketing?

It is most common in digital channels, where feedback is fast, but brand, events, content, product marketing and communications teams use it too, sometimes with longer cycles.

Is there an Agile marketing certification?

Yes. ICAgile offers the ICAgile Certified Professional in Agility in Marketing (ICP-MKG), which grew out of the 2017 SprintOne event. Training providers run courses leading to it.

How do you start Agile marketing?

Pick a pilot team and goal, put all work on one visible board, name a priority owner, set up a single intake route, run two-week cycles or a Kanban flow, and improve the process at every retrospective.
