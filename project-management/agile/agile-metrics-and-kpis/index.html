Agile Metrics and KPIs: What to Track, Formulas, Examples

Agile metrics and KPIs are the measures teams and leaders use to see whether Agile work is delivering value predictably, at good quality and at a sustainable pace. The best set is small and balanced: a few flow or delivery measures, one or two quality measures, an outcome measure and a team health check, reviewed as trends rather than targets. This guide explains each metric with formulas and worked examples, shows how to measure across several teams and a portfolio, and gives you a step-by-step way to choose your own.

Key takeaways

  • Cover five areas: delivery and predictability, flow, quality and stability, value and outcomes, and team health. Each area balances the others.
  • Flow metrics (WIP, cycle time, throughput, work item age) work in any Agile method and support forecasts without story points.
  • DORA now has five software delivery metrics: change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate and deployment rework rate.
  • Report outcomes, forecast ranges and quality trends to leaders; keep velocity and story points inside the team.
  • At scale, add measures for the whole system, such as flow distribution and predictability, instead of adding up team velocities.
  • Metrics are for learning, not for ranking teams or rating individuals. When a measure becomes a target, people optimise the number instead of the result.

What are Agile metrics and KPIs?

Agile metrics are quantitative measures that Agile teams and organisations use to make their work visible and improve it. They show how much work is delivered, how smoothly it flows, how good it is, what value it creates and whether the team can sustain its pace. Agile KPIs are the few metrics an organisation chooses to track progress towards its goals.

The difference matters. A metric can be anything the team finds useful, such as a cumulative flow diagram it looks at in a Retrospective. A key performance indicator (KPI) is a measure leadership has chosen as a critical signal of progress, usually reviewed monthly or quarterly and often with a target. Good KPIs tend to be outcome measures, such as customer satisfaction or lead time, rather than output measures, such as velocity, which are easy to inflate.

Most Agile metrics work in Scrum, Kanban, Scrumban or hybrid teams alike. The table shows where each approach puts its emphasis.

ApproachTypical metricsWhere to read more
ScrumSprint Goal success rate, velocity, burndown, burnupScrum metrics and KPIs
KanbanWIP, cycle time, throughput, work item age, service level expectationKanban metrics
XP and DevOpsDORA metrics, test coverage trend, build healthDevOps
Scaled AgileFlow distribution, flow predictability, portfolio outcomesSAFe

Our Scrum guide covers Scrum-specific measures, Evidence-Based Management and metrics for each Scrum event. This page focuses on metrics that apply across methods, flow-based forecasting and measuring several teams. Terms are defined in our Agile glossary.

The five categories of Agile metrics

A balanced set covers five areas. Pick one or two measures per area that answer questions your team or stakeholders actually ask.

CategoryQuestion it answersExample metricsMain level
Delivery and predictabilityDo we deliver what we plan, and when will it be ready?Say/do ratio, Sprint Goal success, velocity, burnupTeam, programme
FlowHow smoothly and quickly does work move?WIP, cycle time, lead time, throughput, work item age, flow efficiencyTeam, programme
Quality and stabilityIs what we deliver reliable?Escaped defects, change fail rate, deployment rework rateTeam, product
Value and outcomesAre we building the right thing?Adoption, NPS or CSAT, time to value, OKR progressProduct, portfolio
Team healthCan we keep working this way?Happiness score, unplanned work, retro actions completedTeam

The categories check each other. Throughput can rise because the team skips testing, which shows up in escaped defects. Cycle time can fall because only easy items are picked, which shows up in outcomes. Never read one category alone.

Delivery and predictability metrics

These metrics answer the stakeholder question "can we rely on your plans?"

Velocity

Velocity is the amount of work, usually in story points, that a team finishes to the Definition of Done in an iteration. It is a planning aid for that team only. Because points are relative, velocities cannot be compared across teams, and a velocity target simply encourages inflated estimates. Our velocity chart guide shows how to chart it and forecast from it.

Burndown and burnup charts

A burndown shows work remaining in an iteration; a burnup shows work completed against a separate scope line, so it reveals scope changes. Use burnups for release and roadmap conversations.

Say/do ratio (planned vs completed)

The say/do ratio compares what a team planned with what it finished. It works with points or item counts.

Formula: say/do ratio = work completed that was planned ÷ work planned × 100.

Worked example (illustrative): over six iterations a team planned 30, 28, 32, 30, 26 and 30 points and completed 24, 27, 22, 28, 25 and 29 of those planned points. Totals: 155 completed ÷ 176 planned = 88%. The one low iteration (22 of 32) followed an over-ambitious plan, so the team now plans at its recent average rather than its best.

Use it to improve planning, not to push the team. Always 100% usually means plans are too cautious; wide swings point to unplanned work or large items. Scrum teams often prefer the Sprint Goal success rate, covered in the Scrum guide.

Release forecasts as ranges

Whatever delivery metric you use, forecast with a range from your actual history, such as "6 to 8 iterations", and update it as scope and pace change. Our release plan template records the forecast and its assumptions.

Flow metrics

Flow metrics measure how work moves through your process, regardless of how it was estimated. The Kanban Guide names four: WIP, cycle time, work item age and throughput. Lead time and flow efficiency are widely used additions.

MetricDefinitionHow to calculateWhat it tells you
Work in progress (WIP)Items started but not finishedCount items in active workflow stagesOverload; high WIP lengthens cycle time
Cycle timeTime from start to finish of an itemFinish date − start date; report percentilesHow long items take once started
Lead timeTime from request to deliveryDelivery date − request dateHow long customers wait
ThroughputItems finished per periodCount items done per week or iterationDelivery rate, without estimates
Work item ageTime an unfinished item has been in progressToday − start dateItems at risk, while you can still act
Flow efficiencyShare of elapsed time spent actively workingActive time ÷ total time × 100How much time items spend waiting

For definitions in more depth, read our guides to cycle time, cycle time vs lead time, throughput, flow efficiency and the cumulative flow diagram.

Worked example: cycle time percentiles

Cycle times are rarely evenly distributed: most items are quick and a few take much longer. That is why percentiles are more useful than averages. Take ten finished items (illustrative) with cycle times, sorted, of 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 and 14 days.

  1. Average: 57 ÷ 10 = 5.7 days. The single 14-day item pulls it up.
  2. Median (50th percentile): the midpoint between the 5th and 6th values, (4 + 5) ÷ 2 = 4.5 days. Half of items finish within 4.5 days.
  3. 85th percentile: 0.85 × 10 = 8.5, rounded up to the 9th value, which is 9 days. Using this simple nearest-rank method, 85% of items finished within 9 days.
  4. Service level expectation: the team can tell stakeholders "once we start an item, 85% are done within 9 days" and use 9 days as the alarm level for work item age.

Percentiles become more reliable with more data, so collect 20 to 30 items before publishing a service level expectation.

Worked example: Little's Law

Little's Law links the three core flow metrics for a stable system: average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput. Suppose a team averages 12 items in progress and finishes 4 items a week. Average cycle time is 12 ÷ 4 = 3 weeks. If the team lowers WIP to 8 items and throughput holds at 4 a week, average cycle time falls to 8 ÷ 4 = 2 weeks, with no one working harder. This is the reasoning behind WIP limits. Use it to understand your system, not to forecast single items: it assumes a roughly stable system.

Worked example: forecasting with throughput

Throughput lets you forecast without estimating every item. Over the last eight weeks a team finished 5, 7, 4, 6, 8, 5, 6 and 7 items: an average of 6 a week, with a low of 4 and a high of 8. With 60 items left in the backlog:

  • Likely: 60 ÷ 6 = 10 weeks.
  • Cautious: 60 ÷ 4 = 15 weeks.
  • Optimistic: 60 ÷ 8 = 7.5, so about 8 weeks.

A Monte Carlo forecast improves on this by simulating thousands of possible futures: each simulated week draws a random throughput value from your history until the 60 items are done, and the results are reported as probabilities, such as "85% of simulations finished within N weeks". Our guide to Monte Carlo simulation explains the technique. Allow for backlog growth from new and split items.

Quality and delivery stability metrics

Quality metrics check that faster delivery is not being bought with problems later.

  • Escaped defects: defects found after work met the Definition of Done, counted per iteration or month. The Scrum guide gives the defect escape rate formula and an example.
  • Definition of Done adherence: the share of finished items that genuinely met every criterion, checked on a sample.
  • Technical debt trend: known shortcuts logged in the backlog, test coverage or static analysis trends.

DORA software delivery metrics

For software teams, the most widely used delivery measures come from the DORA research programme. Since 2024 there are five, in two groups. Mean time to restore was renamed failed deployment recovery time in 2023, and deployment rework rate was added in 2024, so older articles listing four metrics are out of date.

MetricGroupWhat it measures
Change lead timeThroughputTime from a code change being committed to running in production
Deployment frequencyThroughputHow often changes are deployed to production
Failed deployment recovery timeThroughputTime to recover when a deployment causes a failure
Change fail rateInstabilityShare of deployments that cause a failure needing immediate fix or rollback
Deployment rework rateInstabilityShare of deployments that are unplanned and made to fix a user-facing problem

Worked example (illustrative): a team made 40 production deployments last month. Three needed a rollback or hotfix, so change fail rate = 3 ÷ 40 × 100 = 7.5%. Two further deployments were unplanned fixes for user-reported bugs, so deployment rework rate = 2 ÷ 40 × 100 = 5%. The team tracks both monthly, alongside deployment frequency, so that releasing more often does not quietly increase failures.

DORA metrics rely on DevOps practices such as automated pipelines. Non-software teams can borrow the idea: how often you deliver, how long it takes and how often deliveries need rework.

Value and outcome metrics

Delivery, flow and quality metrics say how well you build; value metrics say whether you are building the right thing. They are the strongest candidates for KPIs.

Output (what we produced)Outcome (what changed)
Features releasedShare of active users who use the new feature
Campaign pages launchedVisit-to-sign-up conversion rate
Reports automatedHours saved per user per week

Net Promoter Score (NPS)

NPS asks customers how likely they are to recommend you on a 0 to 10 scale. NPS = % promoters (9 or 10) − % detractors (0 to 6), giving a score from −100 to +100. Example: of 200 responses, 110 are promoters (55%), 50 passives and 40 detractors (20%). NPS = 55 − 20 = +35. Watch the trend after each release, not a single survey.

Feature adoption rate

Adoption rate = users who used the feature in a period ÷ active users in that period × 100. Example: 1,200 of 8,000 monthly active users used a new booking feature in its first 30 days, an adoption rate of 15%. If the Product Owner expected 30%, that is evidence to investigate discoverability or usefulness before building more on top of it.

Time to value and OKRs

Time to value measures how long it takes from starting an initiative, or a user signing up, to the first real benefit. Many organisations frame outcome KPIs as objectives and key results; our OKR template helps you set and score them. Scrum.org's Evidence-Based Management offers a fuller value framework, summarised in our Scrum metrics guide.

Outcome data lags, so review value metrics over several iterations.

Team health metrics

Sustainable pace is an Agile Manifesto principle. Team health metrics warn you before burnout appears in delivery.

  • Happiness or engagement score: a quick anonymous 1 to 5 rating at each Sprint Retrospective, with a reason.
  • Unplanned work: the share of capacity spent on work not in the plan, such as urgent support.
  • Retrospective actions completed: the share of agreed improvements that were actually done.

Keep these inside the team. Publishing individual scores or ranking teams by happiness destroys the honesty the metric needs.

Agile metrics at scale

When several teams work on one product, adding up team metrics misleads: summed velocities mean nothing, and every team can look fast while the system waits on integration. Measure the system as well as the teams.

LevelTypical questionsUseful metrics
TeamAre we improving how we work?Cycle time, throughput, WIP, escaped defects, happiness
Team of teams (programme or ART)Are we delivering together, predictably?Feature lead time, cross-team dependencies open, predictability, release frequency
PortfolioAre we investing in the right things?Outcome KPIs, flow distribution, epic lead time, value delivered per initiative

SAFe flow metrics

SAFe groups its measures into three domains: outcomes, flow and competency. Its six flow metrics apply at team, Agile Release Train and portfolio level:

SAFe flow metricMeaningClosest team-level equivalent
Flow distributionShare of completed work by type, such as features, enablers, defects and risksWork type mix
Flow velocityNumber of backlog items completed per periodThroughput
Flow timeTime from an item entering the workflow to releaseLead time
Flow loadTotal number of items in progressWIP
Flow efficiencyShare of elapsed time items are actively worked onFlow efficiency
Flow predictabilityHow closely delivered business value matches what was plannedSay/do ratio

Flow distribution example (illustrative): an Agile Release Train completed 40 items in a Planning Interval: 24 features (60%), 8 enablers (20%), 6 defects (15%) and 2 risk items (5%). If leaders expected 30% of capacity for architecture enablers, the 20% figure prompts a capacity conversation at the next PI Planning.

Predictability example (illustrative): teams' committed PI objectives were given 50 planned business value points by business owners; at the end of the PI, business owners scored the actual value achieved at 42. Predictability = 42 ÷ 50 × 100 = 84%. SAFe guidance commonly treats 80% to 100% as a predictable range.

Measuring an Agile transformation

Leaders often want to know whether a move to Agile is working. Counting teams trained or ceremonies held measures activity, not results. Compare a baseline from before the change with the same measures afterwards.

Instead of measuringMeasure
Number of teams "doing Agile"Lead time from idea to customer, before and after
Ceremony attendanceRelease frequency and change fail rate
Maturity questionnaire scores aloneCustomer satisfaction and adoption of what was delivered
Velocity across teamsPredictability of commitments and forecasts
Training completionEmployee engagement and voluntary attrition in delivery teams

Maturity assessments can still help a team reflect, but they should not be the headline result. Our Agile transformation guide covers how to plan the change itself.

Agile KPIs to report to leadership

Leaders need answers to a few questions, not a wall of charts. Map each question to one KPI and an agreed cadence.

Leadership questionSuggested KPICadence
When will it be ready?Forecast range from throughput or velocity, with confidence levelEvery iteration or month
Are we delivering what we said?Say/do ratio, Sprint Goal success or PI predictabilityMonthly or per PI
How quickly do we respond?Lead time (85th percentile) or change lead timeMonthly
Is quality holding up?Escaped defects trend, change fail rateMonthly
Is it making a difference?Adoption, NPS or CSAT, OKR key resultsMonthly or quarterly

Share ranges and trends with a short commentary, and keep story points, burndowns and individual data inside the team. A one-page project status report is a good format.

How to choose and introduce Agile metrics

This step-by-step process, based on the Goal–Question–Metric approach, keeps your set small and useful.

  1. Start with goals. Write down what the team or programme is trying to achieve this quarter, for example "reduce the time customers wait for fixes".
  2. Ask the questions that goal raises. For example: how long do customers wait now, where does work wait, and are fixes sticking?
  3. Choose one metric per question. Lead time, flow efficiency and deployment rework rate answer those three. Aim for four to six metrics in total, across at least three of the five categories.
  4. Define each metric precisely. Agree when the clock starts and stops, which items count and which percentile you report. Write the definitions into your working agreement.
  5. Automate collection. Let your tool produce the data, and make sure board states match reality, or the metric will be wrong.
  6. Collect a baseline. Gather four to six iterations or eight weeks of data before drawing conclusions.
  7. Review at a fixed point. Look at team metrics in Retrospectives and outcome KPIs in reviews with stakeholders, and ask "what will we do differently?"
  8. Retire metrics nobody uses. Every quarter, drop any metric that has not changed a decision.
Tip

Pair every speed metric with a quality metric: throughput with escaped defects, deployment frequency with change fail rate. It stops one improving at the expense of the other.

Worked example: a three-team metrics review

This illustrative example shows three teams working on one customer platform, reviewed at the end of a quarter. Each team uses its own method, so the programme compares flow and outcome measures, not velocity.

TeamMethodThroughput (items/week)Cycle time, 85th pct (days)WIP (avg)Change fail rateHappiness (1–5)
PaymentsScrum7684%3.9
OnboardingKanban9576%4.1
Mobile appScrumban5141615%3.0

How the programme lead and teams read it together:

  1. Mobile app stands out on flow. Its 85th percentile cycle time is more than twice the others, and its WIP is twice as high. By Little's Law, high WIP with lower throughput means long waits.
  2. Quality confirms the pattern. A 15% change fail rate suggests work is rushed at the end to clear the queue.
  3. Health completes the picture. A happiness score of 3.0 with comments about context switching fits the same story.
  4. Root cause. The team found it was waiting on another team's API changes, so items sat half-finished while new ones were started.
  5. Actions. A WIP limit of 8, a weekly dependency check with the Payments team and a shared definition of an API change being done.

Throughput alone would have invited pressure on Mobile app to work harder. Reading flow, quality and health together revealed a system problem that more effort could not fix.

Common mistakes with Agile metrics

  • Setting velocity or throughput targets. Teams respond by inflating estimates or splitting items, and the number rises while value does not.
  • Comparing or ranking teams. Teams differ in context, work type and definitions.
  • Using team metrics in individual reviews. People stop surfacing problems, which ruins the data.
  • Reporting averages for cycle time. A few long items distort them; use percentiles.
  • Counting partly finished work. Only work that meets the Definition of Done should count.
  • Ignoring the system at scale. Every team can look healthy while the whole programme waits on dependencies.

Most of these come from using metrics as verdicts instead of questions. Read Scrum metrics and KPIs for the Scrum-specific view.

Tools for tracking Agile metrics

You can track every metric here in a spreadsheet, but most teams let their work management tool collect the data automatically.

  • Jira includes the standard Agile reports: sprint reports, burndown and burnup charts, velocity, cumulative flow diagrams and control charts for cycle time. It is widely used by software teams.
  • ClickUp offers sprint reporting with burndown, burnup, velocity and cumulative flow, plus dashboards and goals.
  • Wrike, our top-ranked tool overall, suits organisations that need configurable dashboards and portfolio reporting across many teams, including Agile and non-software work.
  • monday.com and Asana suit business teams that want easy dashboards and goal tracking alongside their projects.

Whatever you choose, the tool can only report what the board shows, so keep item states current and make sure "done" means the Definition of Done. See our comparison of Scrum software tools.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important Agile metrics?

There is no single list, but a strong starter set is cycle time, throughput, WIP, escaped defects or change fail rate, one outcome measure such as adoption or NPS, and a team health measure. Scrum teams often add Sprint Goal success rate.

What is the difference between Agile metrics and KPIs?

A metric is any measure a team finds useful for improving. A KPI is one of the few measures leadership chooses as a critical signal of progress towards a goal, usually reviewed monthly or quarterly.

How do you measure Agile team performance?

Look at several areas together: flow (cycle time, throughput), predictability (say/do ratio), quality (escaped defects), outcomes (adoption, satisfaction) and team health. Compare each team with its own past, never with other teams.

Should velocity be used as a KPI?

No. Velocity is a planning aid for one team. As a KPI it encourages inflated estimates and cannot be compared across teams. Use forecast ranges, lead time or outcome measures instead.

What is the difference between cycle time and lead time?

Lead time starts when a request is made; cycle time starts when work begins. Both end at delivery, so lead time is always at least as long as cycle time.

What are the DORA metrics?

Five software delivery metrics: change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate and deployment rework rate. The fifth was added in 2024.

What are flow metrics in Agile?

Measures of how work moves through a process: WIP, cycle time, work item age and throughput, often with lead time and flow efficiency. They work in any Agile method.

How many Agile metrics should a team track?

Usually four to six, covering at least three of the five categories. More than that tends to go unread.

How do you forecast delivery without story points?

Use throughput. Divide remaining items by your lowest, average and highest weekly throughput to get a range, or run a Monte Carlo simulation on your throughput history for probability-based forecasts.

What Agile metrics should executives see?

Forecast ranges, predictability, lead time, quality trends and outcome KPIs such as adoption or customer satisfaction. Keep story points, burndowns and individual data inside the team.

How do you measure the success of an Agile transformation?

Take a baseline before the change and track outcomes after it: lead time, release frequency, change fail rate, customer satisfaction and employee engagement, rather than the number of teams trained.

What is a good say/do ratio?

There is no universal standard. A stable ratio that is high but not always 100% suggests realistic planning; a ratio that is always 100% may mean plans are too cautious.
