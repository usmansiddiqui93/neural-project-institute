Agile vs Waterfall: Differences and How to Choose

Agile vs Waterfall is a choice between two ways of running a project: Waterfall plans the whole project up front and delivers in sequential phases, while Agile delivers in short cycles and adapts the plan as the team learns. Neither is better in general; the right choice depends on how uncertain the work is and whether it can be delivered in pieces. This guide compares them side by side, gives you a scoring check for choosing, and shows illustrative worked examples of Agile, Waterfall and hybrid plans.

Key takeaways

  • Waterfall is a predictive approach: fix the scope, plan it in detail, then design, build and test in sequence. Agile is an adaptive approach: fix the timebox and team, and let scope evolve through feedback.
  • Agile is a family of values and frameworks (Scrum, Kanban, XP, Lean), not a single method, so Agile vs Waterfall compares two philosophies rather than two rulebooks.
  • Choose Waterfall for stable, regulated or physical work that only has value when complete; choose Agile when what you learn will change what you build and the result can be released in parts.
  • A quick suitability check across culture, team and project factors turns the decision into a conversation with evidence rather than a matter of taste.
  • Hybrid approaches are common and often the most practical: predictive governance and milestones with Agile delivery inside them.
  • The PMBOK Guide 8th edition and the July 2026 PMP exam cover predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches, so practitioners are expected to know all three.

What is the difference between Agile and Waterfall?

The main difference between Agile and Waterfall is how they handle uncertainty. Waterfall assumes requirements can be known at the start, so it plans everything first and completes each phase before the next. Agile assumes you will learn as you go, so it delivers working results in short iterations and re-plans after each one, letting scope change while time and cost stay fixed.

PMI calls these predictive and adaptive approaches, and a deliberate mix of the two hybrid. Waterfall is the classic predictive life cycle. Agile is an umbrella term for adaptive ways of working that share the values of the 2001 Agile Manifesto and are put into practice through frameworks such as Scrum and Kanban.

If you are choosing between Waterfall and a specific framework, our guides to Scrum vs Waterfall and Kanban vs Waterfall go into that detail. This page stays at the level of approaches: how to choose, and how to combine the two.

What is Waterfall?

Waterfall is a sequential, plan-driven approach. A project moves through phases such as requirements, design, build, test and deployment, and each phase produces approved deliverables that feed the next. A gate at the end of each phase decides whether the project continues. Scope, schedule and cost are planned in detail, saved as a project baseline and protected through formal change control.

Where Waterfall came from

Phased development is older than the name: Herbert Benington described it for the SAGE air-defence system in 1956. Winston Royce's 1970 paper is usually cited as the first detailed diagram of the model, but Royce did not call it Waterfall and warned that a single pass with testing only at the end was risky, recommending feedback between phases. The term "waterfall" may first appear in a 1976 paper by Bell and Thayer, and the US defence standard DOD-STD-2167 (1985) required sequential phases, which helped make the model the default for large projects.

Before you commit to a fully sequential plan, it helps to know where Waterfall projects usually go wrong. Read our guide to Waterfall project management for the phases, gates, a worked example and how to reduce its weaknesses.

What is Agile?

Agile is a set of values and principles for delivering work in small, usable increments with frequent feedback. It was named in the Agile Manifesto, written by 17 software practitioners in 2001, which values individuals and interactions, working results, customer collaboration and responding to change over their more formal counterparts. Agile drew on earlier iterative methods, so the ideas are older than the label.

Agile is applied through frameworks. The most common are:

FrameworkHow it worksBest for
ScrumFixed Sprints of one month or less, a Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers, and a usable Increment every Sprint (2020 Scrum Guide)Complex product work with a clear goal and evolving requirements
KanbanContinuous flow on a visual board with limits on work in progressSteady streams of requests, support, operations, content
Extreme Programming (XP)Engineering practices such as pair programming, test-driven development and continuous integrationSoftware teams that need high technical quality
LeanEliminate waste, optimise the whole flow of value, deliver fastProcess improvement and product development at any scale

The spectrum: predictive, iterative, incremental and adaptive

Agile vs Waterfall is not a binary switch. PMI's guidance describes a spectrum of life cycles, and the PMBOK Guide 8th edition (published November 2025) frames the choice as predictive, adaptive or hybrid development approaches. Two ideas sit between the extremes: iterative (repeat a cycle to refine the solution) and incremental (deliver the solution in usable pieces).

Life cycleRequirementsDeliveryExample
Predictive (Waterfall)Fixed up frontOnce, at the endBuilding a warehouse to an approved design
IterativeRefined through prototypes and feedbackOnce, at the end, after several refinement loopsA new brand identity developed through rounds of concepts
IncrementalMostly known, split into partsIn usable pieces, frequentlyRolling a new system out to one region at a time
Adaptive (Agile)Emerge and change throughoutSmall usable increments, frequentlyA new mobile app shaped by user feedback each Sprint

Agile combines iterative and incremental delivery. Our guide to iterative and incremental development explains both ideas. The spectrum gives you a better question than "Agile or Waterfall?": how much of this project is known, and how much can be delivered in parts?

The key differences explained

1. The triple constraint is turned upside down

In Waterfall, scope is fixed first and time and cost are estimated from it. When estimates prove wrong, time and cost usually give way. Agile reverses this: a stable team and fixed iteration length make time and cost predictable, and scope flexes, with the most valuable items done first. If an Agile project runs out of money, it stops with the most valuable features already delivered; a Waterfall project that runs out may have nothing usable yet.

2. Planning happens at different times

Waterfall front-loads planning: a work breakdown structure, schedule and cost estimate exist before the build starts. Agile still plans, but in layers and as late as is responsible: a roadmap, a release plan and detailed planning only for the next iteration. Our guide to the role of the project plan in Agile shows how those layers fit together.

3. Change costs different amounts

In Waterfall, a change after sign-off may ripple through design, build and test, so it goes through impact assessment and approval. In Agile, the unbuilt part of the plan is kept deliberately light, so most changes are simply new or re-ordered backlog items. Agile does not make change free; it makes change cheaper by delaying detailed decisions until they are needed.

4. Testing and quality

Waterfall tests in a dedicated phase after the build, and that phase is often squeezed when earlier phases overrun. Agile builds testing into every iteration through a Definition of Done and acceptance criteria, so defects and misunderstood requirements are found within weeks.

5. Risk surfaces at different points

Waterfall reduces risk through analysis and contingency, but integration and acceptance problems often appear late, when they are most expensive to fix. Agile exposes technical and product risk early by integrating working output and showing it to users every iteration.

6. Stakeholders, documentation and leadership

Waterfall asks for intensive stakeholder input during requirements and at gates, produces comprehensive documents phase by phase, and usually has a project manager who plans and assigns the work. Agile asks for a little stakeholder time often, keeps documentation to what is needed to build, support and audit the product, and splits leadership: a product owner decides what to build and a cross-functional team decides how. If stakeholders cannot attend regular reviews, Agile loses much of its benefit.

When to use Waterfall

Waterfall, or a predominantly predictive approach, is the better choice when most of these are true:

  • Requirements are clear and stable, and the customer can describe the result in detail before work starts.
  • The product only has value when complete, such as a bridge, a factory line or a physical installation.
  • Late change would be very expensive or unsafe, for example once concrete is poured or hardware is manufactured.
  • Regulation or contracts demand it: mandatory documentation, formal approvals or a fixed-price contract against a specification.
  • The team has done very similar projects before, so estimates are reliable.
  • Stakeholders cannot commit regular time and need to approve the plan once, then see milestones.

Typical examples are construction, civil engineering, infrastructure, office relocations, equipment installation and many compliance-driven system rollouts. Even here, parts of the work, such as interior design or software components, may benefit from an Agile approach.

When to use Agile

Agile, or a predominantly adaptive approach, is the better choice when most of these are true:

  • The goal is clear but the best solution is not, and the team will learn what works by building and testing.
  • Users or a product owner can give regular feedback.
  • The result can be delivered and used in pieces, so early releases create value or learning.
  • The environment is changing: technology, competitors, regulation or customer needs are moving during the project.
  • Speed to first value matters more than delivering everything at once.
  • The team is stable and cross-functional, or can become so.

Typical examples are software products, websites and apps, digital services, data and analytics products, marketing campaigns, research and new product development. Agile is not limited to software; any work with uncertain requirements and usable increments can benefit.

How to choose: a suitability check

PMI's Agile Practice Guide (published in 2017 with the Agile Alliance) includes a suitability filter that rates a project on culture, team and project factors and plots the answers on a radar chart: answers near the centre suggest Agile, answers at the edge suggest a predictive approach, and a mix suggests hybrid. The check below is our simplified adaptation for quick use; it is not PMI's exact tool.

Step by step

  1. Gather the right people: the sponsor, a delivery lead and someone who represents users.
  2. Describe the project in two sentences: the outcome, the deadline and any hard constraints such as regulation or a fixed contract.
  3. Score the nine questions below from 1 to 5. Discuss each one and agree a score; disagreement is useful information.
  4. Total the scores and read the band, then look at individual low scores, because one very low answer can matter more than the total.
  5. Decide how to handle the gaps. A low score is a risk to manage, not automatically a veto. Poor access to users, for example, can be fixed by appointing a product owner.
  6. Record the decision and the reasons in the project charter, and revisit it at the first major review.

Tip

Ask one question if you have no time for a full check: how likely is it that what we learn during the project will change what we build? The higher that likelihood, the more adaptive your approach should be.

Worked example: choosing an approach for three projects

The following example is illustrative: the organisation, scores and figures are invented to show how the suitability check works. A mid-sized insurance company has three projects starting in the same quarter.

  • Office relocation. Move 220 staff to a new building by a fixed lease date. Fit-out design is approved; contractors are on fixed-price contracts.
  • Customer claims app. Let customers submit and track claims on their phones. Nobody knows which features customers will actually use.
  • CRM replacement. Replace an old customer database with a commercial CRM. Data migration and regulatory reporting are well defined; how sales teams will use the new screens is not.

What the scores tell the team

  • Office relocation (18): predictive. The design is fixed, the move cannot be half done and the lease date cannot move. The project manager builds a detailed schedule with a Gantt chart template, tracks dependencies and controls changes formally.
  • Claims app (41): Agile. A cross-functional Scrum team works in two-week Sprints, releases a minimal version to a pilot group of customers early, and lets usage data shape the backlog. A product backlog template holds the ordered work.
  • CRM replacement (29): hybrid. The low score on safe early release (regulatory reporting must be right first time) and the high score on delivering in parts point to a mix, shown in the hybrid plan in the next section.

Notice that the decision was made per project. A company can be "Agile" and still run its office move as a Waterfall project.

Hybrid approaches: combining Agile and Waterfall

A hybrid approach deliberately mixes predictive and adaptive elements. It is common in large organisations, regulated industries and projects that combine physical and digital work. Some people call it "wagile" or "water-scrum-fall", sometimes as a criticism, but a well-designed hybrid is a legitimate approach recognised in the PMBOK Guide.

Four common hybrid patterns

PatternHow it worksGood for
Predictive governance, Agile deliveryA milestone plan, budget and stage gates at the top; teams deliver in Sprints within each stageOrganisations with formal funding and approval processes; see PRINCE2 vs Agile
Agile discovery, predictive rolloutExplore and prototype in short iterations, then plan the full rollout in detail once the solution is knownNew services that must then be deployed widely, such as a new process across 40 branches
Split by componentStable or physical components run predictively; uncertain components run in Sprints; they synchronise at integration milestonesHardware plus software, buildings plus digital systems
Predictive front end, Agile buildRequirements and architecture agreed up front, then built and tested incrementallyContracted software where the customer needs a baseline but wants early working releases

Worked example: the CRM replacement as a hybrid plan

Continuing the illustrative example, the insurance company has a budget of £600,000 and 36 weeks. The fixed elements (data migration, regulatory reports, go-live date) are planned predictively; the sales screens and workflows are built in two-week Sprints with sales staff reviewing each Increment.

StageWeeksApproachGate or output
1. Initiation and vendor setup1–4PredictiveBusiness case, contract, architecture and data-migration plan approved

The project manager keeps one integrated plan showing milestones, migration tasks and Sprint boundaries, reports progress with a milestone chart and a burn-up of sales features, and agrees with the sponsor that lower-priority sales features may move to a post-launch release if the budget tightens. That agreement is what keeps the hybrid honest: the predictive parts protect the date and compliance, and the Agile parts protect usefulness.

Tip

Decide in advance which changes are routine backlog decisions and which need formal change control. A useful rule: anything that threatens a fixed milestone, compliance or the budget is a change request; everything else is a backlog decision.

Contracts, budgets and reporting

Contracts

Waterfall fits a fixed-price contract against a detailed specification or statement of work: the supplier carries the risk of estimating, and changes are priced separately. Agile fits time-and-materials contracts or a fixed budget with flexible scope, where the customer buys a team for a period and the product owner decides what that capacity is spent on. A common middle ground is a fixed price per release, with the scope of later releases agreed as you learn.

Budgeting

A Waterfall budget is built bottom-up from the work breakdown structure and controlled against the baseline. An Agile budget is usually built from team capacity: in an illustrative case, a team of seven at a blended £5,000 per person per week costs £35,000 a week, so a 20-week release costs about £700,000. The question becomes how much value that capacity can deliver, and the forecast is refreshed every iteration.

Moving from Waterfall to Agile

If your organisation mostly runs Waterfall projects, start with one pilot that scores high on the suitability check. Appoint a product owner with real authority, form a stable cross-functional team, turn the requirements into an ordered backlog and agree with governance what the sponsor will see each iteration. Run a few iterations and review honestly before scaling. If you are switching an existing project, do it at a natural boundary such as the start of a phase.

Common mistakes when choosing between Agile and Waterfall

  • Treating it as a belief system. Picking Agile because it is fashionable, or Waterfall because it feels safe, instead of looking at the project.
  • One approach for every project. Organisations run many kinds of work; choose per project, or per component.
  • Calling no plan "Agile". Agile teams still have goals, roadmaps, release plans and forecasts.
  • Running Waterfall in two-week chunks. Sprints that hand designs to builders and builds to testers keep Waterfall's late feedback while adding meetings.
  • Fixing scope, time and cost all at once. No approach can guarantee all three under uncertainty; decide which one flexes.
  • Over-engineering the hybrid. Two full sets of controls on one project creates double reporting. Keep the predictive layer focused on what genuinely must be fixed.

Tools for Agile, Waterfall and hybrid projects

Most modern project management tools support both approaches, but they lean in different directions.

  • Wrike, our top-rated tool (9.4/10), suits hybrid work well: Gantt charts with dependencies for the predictive layer, boards and custom workflows for Agile teams, and dashboards that show both. It takes time to set up properly.
  • Jira is the most complete Agile tool for software teams, with backlogs, Sprints and boards, but it does not offer a classic Gantt chart with critical path and baselines, so it is less suited to plan-driven projects.
  • monday.com, Asana and ClickUp offer boards plus timeline or Gantt views for mixed portfolios; compare them in our project management software guide.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between Agile and Waterfall?

Waterfall plans the whole project up front and delivers it in sequential phases, usually once at the end. Agile plans in layers and delivers working increments in short cycles, letting scope change as the team learns while time and cost per cycle stay fixed.

Which is better, Agile or Waterfall?

Neither is better in general. Agile suits uncertain work that can be delivered in pieces and where users can give regular feedback. Waterfall suits stable, well-understood, regulated or physical work that only has value once complete. Many projects are best served by a hybrid.

Is Waterfall outdated?

No. It remains the right choice for stable, regulated or physical projects such as construction and equipment installation. What has changed is that it is no longer the default for everything, especially software and digital products.

Is Scrum Agile or Waterfall?

Scrum is an Agile framework. It delivers work in Sprints of one month or less, with a usable Increment each Sprint, as defined in the 2020 Scrum Guide. See Scrum vs Waterfall for a detailed comparison.

Can you combine Agile and Waterfall?

Yes. A hybrid approach might use a Waterfall-style milestone plan, budget and stage gates for governance while teams deliver in Agile iterations, or run physical components predictively and software in Sprints. Decide in advance which changes need formal approval.

Can you switch from Waterfall to Agile mid-project?

Yes, but do it at a natural boundary such as the start of a phase. Convert the remaining requirements into an ordered backlog, appoint a product owner, agree with the sponsor how progress will be reported, and start with short iterations.

What are examples of Waterfall projects?

Construction, civil engineering, office relocations, equipment installation, hardware manufacturing and heavily regulated system implementations are classic examples, because requirements are fixed and partial results have little value.

Is Agile or Waterfall cheaper?

Neither is inherently cheaper. Waterfall can look cheaper on paper because costs are estimated up front, but late changes and building features nobody uses can make it expensive. Agile needs more stakeholder time but reduces wasted work, and its cost per iteration is predictable.

How is testing different in Agile and Waterfall?

In Waterfall, testing is usually a separate phase after the build. In Agile, testing happens within every iteration as part of the Definition of Done, so defects and misunderstandings are found sooner and fixed more cheaply.

Does the PMP exam cover Agile and Waterfall?

Yes. PMI states that the PMP exam introduced in July 2026 covers predictive and Agile methods and hybrid applications, so candidates need to understand all three. See our PMP certification guide for the exam details.
