How to Run an Agile Project: Step-by-Step With Example

To run an Agile project, agree a clear vision, form a small cross-functional team with a Product Owner, build and order a backlog, plan a minimum viable product with a forecast range, then deliver in short sprints and review working results with stakeholders every sprint until you release. This guide takes one project from kickoff to closure in 10 steps, with a sprint calendar, a worked release forecast and an illustrative three-month example. If you are introducing Agile across several teams or a whole organisation, see our guide to implementing Agile instead.

Key takeaways

  • An Agile project fixes the team, the time window and the budget, and lets scope flex by value. The Product Owner decides what is built next.
  • Start with a one-page vision and a short inception phase, not a detailed plan. You need just enough backlog for the first release.
  • Aim for a minimum viable product and forecast it as a range, using the team's velocity, then update the forecast every sprint.
  • Run a steady cadence of Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Retrospective, and invite stakeholders to every review.
  • Report working output, a burnup chart, the forecast range and the top risks rather than percentage complete.
  • Release as soon as the increment is valuable, measure real use, then close the project or hand the product to a continuing team.

What does running an Agile project involve?

Running an Agile project means delivering a product or outcome in short, fixed-length iterations, with a stable team that plans just enough, builds a usable increment every cycle, shows it to stakeholders and adjusts the plan based on what it learns. Scope is ordered by value and can change; the team, the cadence and the quality standard stay fixed.

The table shows how this differs from running the same project in a traditional, plan-driven way.

Traditional projectAgile project
PlanningDetailed plan up frontVision and release plan up front; detail planned each sprint
ScopeFixed; changes go through change controlFlexible; the Product Owner reorders the backlog
DeliveryOne delivery near the endA usable increment every sprint; releases as soon as valuable
Progress measureTasks complete against the planWorking output and a forecast range
Stakeholder involvementMainly at the start and endAt every Sprint Review

For a fuller comparison, see Agile vs Waterfall. This guide assumes you have the freedom to run one project the Agile way. Read how to implement Agile if you are introducing Agile across teams or a department.

The Agile project lifecycle at a glance

Agile projects still have a beginning, middle and end. The phases below are a practical way to organise them; the sprint phase repeats until the product is ready to release.

PhasePurposeMain outputsTypical length
InitiateConfirm the problem, sponsor and fitVision, lightweight charterDays to 2 weeks
Inception (Sprint 0)Align the team, shape the first backlogStory map, initial backlog, Definition of Done, release forecast1–2 weeks
SprintsDeliver and learn in short cyclesDone increments, updated backlog and forecastMost of the project
ReleasePut the product in users' handsReleased increment, support planCan happen every sprint or at milestones
Close or continueEnd the project or hand over to a product teamLessons learned, handover1–2 weeks

Many UK public-sector teams use a similar phased approach called discovery, alpha, beta and live, where discovery tests whether the problem is worth solving and alpha tests possible solutions before building the real service.

Step 1: Check that Agile fits the project

Agile works best when the outcome is clear but the best way to reach it is not, and when users can give feedback on partial results. Use this quick check before you commit.

SignalPoints to AgilePoints to a predictive or hybrid approach
RequirementsLikely to change as people see resultsStable and well understood
DeliveryCan be split into usable incrementsOnly useful when complete, such as a building
UsersAvailable to give regular feedbackNot available until acceptance
TeamCan be dedicated and stablePeople join for short specialist tasks
ConstraintsSome flexibility in scopeScope, date and cost all fixed by contract

Mixed signals usually suggest a hybrid: a predictive plan for fixed parts, such as procurement or installation, and Agile sprints for the parts where learning matters, such as software or service design.

Step 2: Write the vision and a lightweight charter

Before anyone writes a user story, agree why the project exists. A short product vision keeps decisions consistent when priorities change. A simple template:

Tip

For [target users] who [need or problem], the [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [current alternative], it [main differentiator].

Add the essentials a sponsor needs to approve the work. Keep it to one or two pages.

  • Goal and success measures: what will be different, and how you will measure it, such as online bookings or time saved.
  • Constraints: budget, time window, regulations and fixed dates.
  • Team and roles: who is the sponsor, Product Owner and team.
  • Assumptions and top risks.
  • Governance: how often the sponsor sees progress, usually at Sprint Reviews.

Our project charter guide explains each element, and the free Agile project plan template includes a lightweight Agile charter alongside the vision, epics and release plan.

Step 3: Form the team and agree roles

An Agile project team is small, cross-functional and stable. The Scrum Guide describes a Scrum Team of 10 or fewer people, including the Product Owner and Scrum Master, with all the skills needed to create a usable increment each Sprint.

RoleAccountable forTime on the project
SponsorFunding, the business case, removing organisational blockersAttends Sprint Reviews
Product OwnerVision, ordering the backlog, accepting work, stakeholder decisionsMost of their week
Scrum Master or Agile leadThe process, coaching, removing impedimentsPart- or full-time
Developers (all skills)Building, testing and delivering the incrementFull-time where possible
Stakeholders and usersFeedback on increments, domain knowledgeReviews and workshops

Where does the project manager fit? On a single Scrum team the project management work is shared out: the Product Owner owns scope and priorities, the team owns delivery and the Scrum Master owns the process. Many organisations keep an Agile project manager to handle budget, contracts, risks, dependencies and reporting, especially on larger or hybrid projects.

Finish by agreeing a team working agreement: core hours, communication channels, how decisions are made and how the team handles urgent requests. See what is an Agile team for more on team design.

Step 4: Hold a kickoff and an inception phase

A kickoff gets everyone on the same page; a short inception phase, often called Sprint 0 or iteration zero, prepares enough to start delivering. The Scrum Guide does not define a Sprint 0, so treat it as a common practice: keep it short, and if it can produce something usable, even a basic working page, do so.

SessionWhoOutputTypical time
Kickoff: vision, goals, constraintsSponsor, Product Owner, team, key stakeholdersShared understanding of why2 hours
Story-mapping workshopProduct Owner, team, usersStory map and first release sliceHalf a day
Technical and design spikesDevelopersApproach agreed for the riskiest parts1–3 days
Definition of Done and working agreementTeam, Product OwnerAgreed quality standard1–2 hours
Initial estimationTeamStory points or sizes for the first release2–3 hours
Board and tools set-upScrum Master, teamBoard, backlog, dashboardsHalf a day

By the end of inception you should be able to answer: what are we building first, roughly how big is it, what does done mean, and when is the first Sprint Review?

Step 5: Build the backlog with a story map

A story map lays out the user's journey from left to right and the stories that support each step underneath, most important at the top. A horizontal line then marks what goes into the first release. It stops the backlog becoming a flat list of features with no sense of the whole.

Example: a story map for an online class booking service

This example is illustrative.

Find a classBookPayManage booking
Search by day and centre (MVP)Book a place (MVP)Pay by card (MVP)Cancel a booking (MVP)
Filter by class type (MVP)Join a waiting list (release 2)Use membership credits (release 2)Email reminder (MVP)
Save favourite classes (release 2)Book for a friend (later)Pay by gift voucher (later)Rate a class (later)

Write stories the team can deliver

Turn each item into a user story with acceptance criteria, for example: "As a member, I want to cancel a booking up to two hours before the class, so that someone else can take my place." Acceptance criteria then state the rules, such as the two-hour cut-off and the confirmation email. See our guide to user stories and the free user story template.

Order the backlog by value, risk and dependencies, put the riskiest learning early, and make sure the top items meet your Definition of Done criteria before you call them finished.

Step 6: Define the MVP and forecast the release

A minimum viable product (MVP) is the smallest version that delivers value to real users and generates learning. Use MoSCoW (Must, Should, Could, Won't this time) to agree the line on the story map with stakeholders, then estimate the items above it.

Worked example: a release forecast

The numbers are illustrative. The MVP backlog is estimated at 120 story points. After the first sprints, the team's velocity ranges from 18 to 24 points per two-week sprint.

ScenarioVelocityCalculationSprints needed
Optimistic24 points120 ÷ 24 = 5.05 sprints (10 weeks)
Pessimistic18 points120 ÷ 18 = 6.7, rounded up7 sprints (14 weeks)

Communicate the forecast as a range: "The MVP is likely to be ready in 10 to 14 weeks." Before the team has any velocity, use a capacity-based estimate and say clearly that the range will narrow after two or three sprints. If stakeholders need a fixed date, fix it and let the scope beneath the MVP line flex instead.

Step 7: Run the sprint cycle

Most of the project happens here. A sprint is a fixed period of one month or less, usually two weeks, in which the team turns backlog items into a Done increment. The 2020 Scrum Guide sets maximum timeboxes for a one-month Sprint (Sprint Planning 8 hours, Sprint Review 4 hours, Sprint Retrospective 3 hours); for shorter Sprints the events are usually shorter. The Daily Scrum is 15 minutes.

A typical two-week sprint calendar

WhenEventTypical lengthPurpose
Day 1 morningSprint Planning2–4 hoursAgree the Sprint Goal and select backlog items
Every dayDaily Scrum15 minutesInspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan
Around day 6Backlog refinement1–2 hoursClarify and size items for the next sprint
Day 10Sprint Review1–2 hoursShow the increment to stakeholders and update the backlog
Day 10Sprint Retrospective1–1.5 hoursAgree improvements to how the team works

Protect the Sprint Goal. If something urgent arrives, the Product Owner decides whether it waits for the next sprint or, in rare cases, whether the Sprint Goal is still worth pursuing. For more on how sprints work, see what is a sprint.

Tip

Keep the Sprint Review a working session, not a presentation. Let stakeholders use the increment, then discuss what to change in the backlog.

Step 8: Manage scope, risks and change

In an Agile project, change is expected, but it still needs managing. The rule is simple: the team's capacity is fixed, so adding something means something else moves down or out.

  1. New request arrives. It goes to the Product Owner, not straight to a team member.
  2. Product Owner assesses value against what is already in the backlog and the vision.
  3. Team sizes it during refinement.
  4. Product Owner places it in the backlog. If it goes above the release line, an item of similar size moves below it.
  5. Forecast is updated and the trade-off is explained at the next Sprint Review.

Treat risks the same way. Add risk-reducing work, such as a prototype or a load test, to the backlog and order it early. Keep a simple log of risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies, for example our RAID log template, and review the top items at each Sprint Review.

Larger changes that affect the vision, budget or time window go back to the sponsor. That is a governance decision, not a backlog decision.

Step 9: Report progress to stakeholders

The best report on an Agile project is the working increment at the Sprint Review. Between reviews, a one-page status update is usually enough.

IncludeWhy
Sprint Goal and whether it was metShows focus and reliability
What was completed, with a link or demoEvidence of real progress
Release burnup chartShows completed work against total scope, including scope changes
Forecast range for the next releaseAnswers when it will be ready, honestly
Top three risks or blockers and who owns themWhere stakeholders can help
Decisions neededKeeps the sponsor engaged

Avoid percentage complete: on an Agile project the total scope changes, so the percentage misleads. A burnup chart shows both lines, completed work and total scope, so everyone can see whether the gap is closing. Our guide to the Agile velocity chart explains how to read the team's delivery trend.

Step 10: Release, measure and close

Release as soon as the increment is valuable, rather than waiting for everything. Before each release, check:

  • All items meet the Definition of Done, including testing, accessibility and security checks.
  • Users, support and operations teams know what is changing.
  • There is a way to measure use, such as analytics or feedback forms, linked to the success measures in the charter.
  • There is a rollback or fix plan if something goes wrong.

After release, feed real usage data and feedback back into the backlog. Many Agile products then continue with the same team, and the project simply becomes ongoing product development.

Closing an Agile project

If the project does end, close it deliberately: hold a final retrospective covering the whole project, hand over to the team that will support the product, close budgets and contracts, archive the backlog and share lessons learned. A lessons learned template keeps this short and useful.

Worked example: running a three-month Agile project

This example is illustrative. The organisation, figures and events are invented to show how the steps fit together.

A group of leisure centres wants members to book classes online instead of by phone. The sponsor sets a budget for a team of seven for three months: a Product Owner from the membership team, a Scrum Master and five developers covering design, front-end, back-end and testing. Success means at least half of class bookings made online within three months of launch.

Inception (week 1)

The kickoff agrees the vision. A half-day story-mapping session with two centre managers and four members produces the map shown in step 5. The MVP, covering search, book, pay by card, cancel and reminders, is estimated at 120 points. The deadline allows six two-week sprints.

Sprint by sprint

SprintVelocityDone so farWhat happened
11616 / 120Search and basic booking work end to end; payment provider set-up takes longer than expected
22238 / 120Card payment live in test; forecast after Sprint 2: 4–6 more sprints, so the deadline is at risk
32462 / 120At the review, centre managers ask for a waiting list (13 points)
42385 / 120Waiting list built; scope changes below take the MVP to 112 points
521106 / 112Accessibility review finds issues; fixes added to the backlog
614 + fixes112 / 112, plus class filterLast MVP items, accessibility fixes and load test; release

How the scope change was handled

After Sprint 3, 58 points remained and three sprints were left. Recent velocity of 16–24 points gave a capacity of roughly 48–72 points. Adding the 13-point waiting list made 71 points, close to the optimistic end. The Product Owner moved "email reminder" (13 points) to release 2 and placed "filter by class type" (8 points) below the line as a stretch item, giving 50 points and a realistic plan. The sponsor agreed the trade-off at the Sprint 4 review. Because Sprints 4 and 5 went well, the team also delivered the class filter in Sprint 6.

Outcome

The service launched at the end of week 13. The team stayed together for a further quarter to add email reminders, membership credits and the waiting-list improvements users asked for, so the project closed with a handover to that continuing product team and a project retrospective rather than a disbanded team.

Running an Agile project with a fixed budget or deadline

Most real projects have at least one fixed constraint. Agile handles this by fixing time and cost and letting scope flex, which is the opposite of the traditional triangle where scope is fixed and time and cost absorb the changes.

ConstraintWhat stays fixedWhat flexesHow to manage it
Fixed budgetTeam size and number of sprintsScope below the MVP lineOrder by value so the most important items are done first
Fixed deadlineRelease dateScope below the MVP lineForecast every sprint; move items early, not in the last week
Fixed scopeMust-have featuresDate and costUse a predictive or hybrid plan; still deliver incrementally
Fixed price contractPrice and outcomeDetailed features within an agreed scopeAgree a swap rule: new items replace items of equal size

Whatever is fixed, make the MVP smaller than the full capacity, so the project has a buffer for surprises.

Common mistakes when running an Agile project

  • Skipping the vision. Without it the backlog becomes a list of requests with no way to choose between them.
  • A Product Owner with no time or authority. Decisions stall and the team guesses.
  • Too long an inception. Weeks of analysis before any delivery is a waterfall phase with a new name.
  • Promising a single date. Give a range and narrow it as velocity becomes reliable.
  • Adding work mid-sprint. It breaks the Sprint Goal and makes velocity meaningless.
  • Reporting percentage complete. Use burnup charts and working output.
  • Treating the Sprint Review as a demo only. Its purpose is to decide what to change next.
  • Leaving testing and release tasks to the end. Build them into the Definition of Done from the start.

Tools for running an Agile project

For a small co-located team, a physical board and a spreadsheet backlog can be enough. Most teams use a digital tool for the board, backlog, burnup chart and stakeholder visibility.

  • Jira has mature Scrum and Kanban boards, backlogs and sprint reports, and is a strong choice for software projects.
  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, works well for cross-functional Agile projects that combine sprints with request intake, dashboards for sponsors and reporting across several projects.
  • monday.com and ClickUp offer flexible boards and sprint features that non-technical teams find easy to adopt.

Whatever tool you choose, start from the Agile project plan template and the release plan template to agree the vision, epics and forecast before you configure the tool.

Frequently asked questions

What are the steps to run an Agile project?

Check Agile fits, write the vision and a light charter, form the team, hold a kickoff and short inception, build the backlog with a story map, define the MVP and forecast it, run sprints, manage scope and risk through the backlog, report with working output and burnup charts, then release, measure and close or continue.

How do you start an Agile project with no backlog?

Run a story-mapping workshop with users and stakeholders. Lay out the user journey, list the stories under each step, draw a line for the first release and write user stories with acceptance criteria for the items above it.

Who manages an Agile project?

Management is shared. The Product Owner owns scope and priorities, the team owns delivery and the Scrum Master owns the process. Many organisations also use an Agile project manager for budget, contracts, risks and dependencies.

What is Sprint 0 in an Agile project?

A short preparation period, usually one or two weeks, used to align the team, build the first backlog, agree the Definition of Done and set up tools. It is a common practice rather than part of the Scrum Guide.

How long should sprints be in an Agile project?

One month or less under the Scrum Guide. Two weeks is the most common choice because it gives frequent feedback without too much planning overhead. Keep the length consistent once chosen.

How do you forecast how long an Agile project will take?

Divide the remaining backlog estimate by the team's lowest and highest recent velocity to get a range of sprints, then update it every sprint. See Agile release planning.

How do you report on an Agile project?

Show the working increment at Sprint Reviews and share a short update with the Sprint Goal result, a release burnup chart, the forecast range, top risks and decisions needed. Avoid percentage complete.

Can you run an Agile project with a fixed budget?

Yes. Fix the team and the number of sprints, order the backlog by value and let lower-priority scope flex so the most valuable features are delivered within the budget.

How do you handle scope changes in an Agile project?

New requests go to the Product Owner, who weighs them against the backlog. If a new item goes above the release line, an item of similar size moves below it, and the forecast is updated at the next Sprint Review.

What documents does an Agile project need?

Usually a product vision, a lightweight charter, an ordered backlog with user stories, a Definition of Done, a release plan with a forecast range, a risk or RAID log and short status updates. Add whatever your governance or regulations genuinely require.

How do you close an Agile project?

Hold a final project retrospective, hand over to the team supporting the product, close budgets and contracts, archive the backlog and share lessons learned. Many Agile products continue with the same team instead.

Can a non-software project be run with Agile?

Yes, if the work can be split into increments that people can review, such as marketing campaigns, service designs or process changes. Projects that are only useful when complete often suit a hybrid approach.
