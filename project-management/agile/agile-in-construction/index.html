Agile in Construction: Where It Works and How to Use It

Agile in construction means applying short planning cycles, frequent feedback, visual management and continuous improvement to the parts of a building project that benefit from them: mainly design, preconstruction and day-to-day site coordination. The overall build usually stays predictive, with a baseline programme, budget and contract, so in practice most teams run a hybrid. This guide shows where Agile fits phase by phase, how it relates to Lean construction and the Last Planner System, how Scrum maps onto a building project, three illustrative worked examples and a step-by-step way to introduce it.

Key takeaways

  • Agile in construction works best in design, preconstruction and site coordination; the master programme, budget and contract usually stay predictive.
  • Lean construction and the Last Planner System are the construction industry's own route to the same ideas: plan in layers, let the people doing the work commit, and learn every week.
  • In design, Scrum-style sprints with regular client reviews catch changes while they are still cheap, before the design is frozen.
  • On site, weekly work plans, daily huddles and percent plan complete (PPC) make short-term planning more reliable.
  • Start with one problem, one team and a few simple practices, and change the contract and governance only when the practices have proved themselves.
  • Physical boards remain common on site; a work management platform such as Wrike helps with design management, approvals and reporting across projects.

What is Agile in construction?

Agile in construction is the use of Agile and Lean practices, such as short planning cycles, regular stakeholder reviews, daily coordination, visual boards and retrospectives, on building and infrastructure projects. It is usually applied to design, preconstruction and site coordination inside a predictive master plan, rather than replacing the stage-gated approach for the whole project.

The question comes up because Agile grew out of software, where a team can release a working product every few weeks. Construction produces a one-off physical asset, built by a temporary group of firms bound by contracts. You cannot hand over half a building and ask the client what they think.

Yet construction has always had the problems Agile was designed to address: uncertain requirements early on, many specialists who depend on each other, plans that go out of date within days and long feedback loops between the people who design and the people who build. The honest answer to 'does Agile work in construction?' is partly, in the right places. Why Agile does not transfer directly to the build

These constraints explain why the build phase resists Agile. They are real, not a lack of imagination.

  • No usable increments. A software team can ship a small working feature. A contractor cannot ship a working ground floor while the frame above is still being designed.
  • Late change is expensive and can be unsafe. Moving a column, a riser or a fire compartment after construction starts can mean demolition, re-approval and new risk assessments.
  • Contracts and permits need detail up front. Planning or zoning approval, building regulations or code approval and fixed-price tenders all depend on a defined design.
  • Physical sequence. Substructure comes before frame, frame before envelope, envelope before fit-out. Dependencies are dictated by gravity and weather, not just by priorities.
  • Materials and supply chains. Long-lead items such as switchgear, lifts and façade panels must be ordered months ahead, which fixes parts of the design early.
  • Safety law. Health and safety duties require planned, assessed methods of work. Improvised changes on site are a safety risk, not agility.

For these reasons the overall project normally keeps a baseline programme built with the critical path method, a cost plan and formal change control. See our comparison of Agile vs Waterfall for the general trade-offs.

What an 'increment' can mean in construction

Agile's idea of delivering something usable and reviewable in small steps still has useful equivalents if you redefine the increment:

Construction incrementWhat the client or team can reviewTypical phase
Coordinated design packageA section of the model or drawing set checked across disciplinesDesign
Room layout or mock-upA physical or virtual mock-up of a typical room, façade bay or bathroom podDesign and preconstruction
First-in-place installationThe first completed apartment, ward or classroom, inspected before the rest are repeatedConstruction
Completed zone or floorA zone that passes inspection and is handed to the next tradeConstruction and fit-out
Phased handoverA building, block or phase handed over and used while others continueLarge or multi-phase projects

Where Agile fits: a phase-by-phase view

The fit varies across the life of the project. Use this table to decide which practices are worth trying at each stage.

Tip

The rule of thumb: the cheaper it is to change something, the more Agile you can be about it. Drawings and models are cheap to change; concrete is not.

Agile, Lean construction and the Last Planner System

Many construction teams never use the word Agile because they reached similar ideas through Lean construction, which applies Lean thinking (reduce waste, improve flow, involve the people who do the work) to building projects. Agile and Lean share a lot: small batches, pull rather than push, visual management, respect for the people doing the work and continuous improvement. Our guide to Lean project management covers the wider Lean toolkit, and our explanation of pull vs push systems shows why pull planning matters.

The planning layers of the Last Planner System

The best-known Lean construction method is the Last Planner System, conceived by Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell and developed with the Lean Construction Institute (LCI), which holds 'Last Planner' as a registered trademark. The 'last planners' are the people who directly assign or do the work, such as trade foremen and crew leads. LCI's Last Planner System workbook describes planning in layers, from the master schedule down to the weekly work plan, and LCI treats weekly learning from missed commitments as part of the system:

LayerHorizonWho plansOutput
Master scheduleWhole projectProject and planning teamMilestones and overall phases
Phase scheduling (pull planning)One phase, such as frame or envelopeTrade foremen together, working backwards from a milestoneAgreed handoffs between trades
Lookahead planning (make-ready)Typically about five to six weeks aheadSite team and tradesConstraint log: information, materials, permits, prior work
Weekly work planningNext weekLast plannersWeekly commitments that are ready to be done
LearningEvery weekWhole teamPercent plan complete (PPC), reasons for misses, actions

The workbook also describes daily meetings in which supervisors and crews set targets and adjust the plan, which play a similar role to a Daily Scrum.

What makes a sound weekly commitment

A constraint is anything that stops a task being ready to start. LCI's quality criteria for an assignment are a useful checklist before anyone commits:

  • Definition: specific enough that everyone knows what done looks like.
  • Soundness: prerequisites, materials, information and design are in place.
  • Sequence: the work is in the right order and releases work others need.
  • Size: it fits the crew's capacity for the week.
  • Learning: if it is not completed, the reason is recorded and acted on.

Takt planning

Takt planning divides the building into zones with similar amounts of work and moves each trade through the zones at a fixed rhythm, so trades follow each other like a production line. It works especially well for repetitive work such as apartments, hotel rooms, hospital wards and office floors, and it pairs naturally with weekly Last Planner commitments.

Mapping Scrum to a construction project

Pure Scrum rarely fits the build phase, but it maps well onto a design team or a self-contained package such as a fit-out. This translation shows how the 2020 Scrum Guide's elements typically look on a building project.

Scrum elementConstruction equivalentPractical notes
Product OwnerClient representative or design manager with authority to prioritiseMust be able to make decisions quickly; see the Product Owner role
DevelopersArchitects, engineers and BIM coordinators (or a fit-out crew)Keep the whole Scrum Team small (the Scrum Guide says typically 10 or fewer people) and stable for the phase
Scrum MasterDesign manager, Lean coach or facilitatorMakes sure the events happen and stay useful, and helps remove blockers; not the person approving the design
Product BacklogPrioritised list of design decisions, deliverables and open questionsLinked to the information release schedule
SprintA one- or two-week design cycleFixed length (the Scrum Guide allows up to one month), with a clear Sprint Goal
IncrementA coordinated design package or model sectionMust be checked across disciplines, not just drawn
Sprint ReviewDesign review with the client and end usersShow models, mock-ups and costs; see Sprint Review
Sprint RetrospectiveTeam review of how the cycle wentShort and frequent; see Sprint Retrospective
Daily ScrumDaily design stand-up or site huddle15 minutes, focused on progress toward the Sprint Goal and what is blocking it
Definition of DoneChecked, coordinated, cost-reviewed and issued for the next stageAgree it before the first sprint

Keep the formal stage gates. A design sprint does not replace the client's stage sign-off; it creates a series of smaller reviews on the way to it, so the sign-off contains no surprises.

Worked example: two-week design sprints

This example is illustrative. A design team is developing a new single-storey public library and community hub for a local council. The client side is represented by the council's library services manager, who can make day-to-day decisions within agreed limits, with the council's property team and community representatives joining reviews. The team runs six two-week sprints through concept and spatial coordination, ending with the formal stage sign-off.

SprintSprint GoalReviewed with
1Agree massing option and site layout, including entrances, parking and outdoor spaceLibrary services manager, property team, community representatives
2Library floor, children's area and meeting room layouts with furniture plansLibrary staff and services manager
3Structural grid and services strategy coordinated with layoutsServices manager, cost consultant
4Façade, daylighting and acoustic approachServices manager, planning consultant
5Cost check against budget and agreed value engineering optionsServices manager, property team
6Coordinated model and drawing set for stage sign-offAll client stakeholders

At the Sprint 2 review, library staff point out that the children's area sits behind the book stacks, out of sight of the help desk, so a single member of staff cannot supervise it during quiet hours. The team adds a backlog item, moves the children's area next to the help desk behind a low glazed screen and shows the revised layout at the Sprint 3 review. Because the structural grid and services had not yet been fixed, the change costs the design team part of one sprint.

If the same request had arrived after technical design, it would have meant revised architectural, structural and services drawings and, once a contractor was appointed, a priced variation. That is the core benefit of Agile in design: move the moment of feedback earlier, while change is still cheap.

A sprint planning template is enough to plan each cycle: sprint goal, backlog items, who is doing what and the review date.

Worked example: weekly work plan and PPC

This example is illustrative. A main contractor is fitting out three floors of an office building. Every Wednesday the site manager holds a weekly work planning meeting with the trade foremen. Each foreman commits only to tasks that meet the soundness checks above. The following Wednesday they review what was completed.

TradeTasks committedTasks completedMissed
Drylining14122
Mechanical and electrical first fix1284
Ceilings972
Decorating770
Fire stopping624
Total483612

Percent plan complete (PPC) = tasks completed ÷ tasks committed = 36 ÷ 48 = 0.75, or 75%. PPC is a ratio of task counts, so it treats a small task and a large one equally; it measures whether promises were kept, not how much work was done. The number matters less than the reasons behind it, so the team records a reason for every missed task:

Reason for varianceMissed tasksExample
Prerequisite work not finished4M&E could not start in three areas because boarding was incomplete
Waiting for approval or inspection3Fire stopping waiting for the fire engineer's sign-off of two details and an inspection visit
Materials late2Plasterboard delivery short; fire collars on back order
Design information missing2Open RFIs on a ceiling bulkhead detail and access hatch positions
Labour shortfall1Drylining crew one person short for two days

The biggest cause, prerequisite work, points to a planning problem rather than a performance problem: trades committed to work that depended on another trade finishing first. The team agrees two actions. Foremen will confirm handoffs at the daily huddle, and the lookahead will now include inspection bookings as constraints. Over the following weeks, PPC rises as the causes are removed; the point is the weekly learning loop, not a target number.

Tip

Never use PPC to blame a trade. If foremen are punished for low PPC, they will commit to less, and the plan will look reliable while the project slows down.

For a structured way to dig into a recurring reason, use a root cause analysis template.

Worked example: a Kanban board for RFIs

This example is illustrative. On a mixed-use scheme, requests for information (RFIs) are piling up: 46 are open and the oldest is 31 days old. Site teams are waiting, and nobody can see where each RFI is stuck. The design manager sets up a Kanban board with five columns:

ColumnMeaningPolicy
RaisedRFI logged by siteMust state the location, the drawing reference and the date the answer is needed
TriageChecked and assigned to a disciplineWithin one working day
With designerBeing answeredWork-in-progress limit of 8 per discipline
Answer under reviewChecked by design manager and siteWithin two working days
ClosedAnswer issued and drawings updated if neededLinked to any change it causes

Two rules make it work. First, a WIP limit per discipline stops designers starting new RFIs before finishing old ones. Second, any RFI older than five working days is discussed at the weekly coordination meeting. After six weeks, in this illustrative case, 18 RFIs are open and the oldest is nine days old. It works for submittals and snags too. A ready-made Kanban board template is a quick way to start, and our guide to visual management explains why boards help teams spot problems early.

How to introduce Agile practices on a construction project, step by step

Introduce Agile and Lean practices in small steps, proving each one before adding the next. These ten steps work for both client-side and contractor-side teams.

  1. Pick one problem. For example, late design information, unreliable weekly plans or slow client decisions. Agile practices are a means, not the goal.
  2. Choose the scope. Start with one design team, one package or one floor rather than the whole project.
  3. Keep the master plan. Confirm the baseline programme, budget and contract stay in place, so governance and the client have nothing to fear. Our guide to the role of the project plan in Agile explains how the two layers fit together.
  4. Agree the decision-maker. Name the client representative or design manager who can prioritise and decide within agreed limits, and the limits beyond which formal change control applies.
  5. Set a cadence. For design, two-week sprints with a review at the end. For site, a weekly work plan meeting and a 15-minute daily huddle at the same time and place.
  6. Make work visible. A board on the site office wall or a shared digital board showing tasks, constraints and blockers. Keep it simple enough to update in minutes.
  7. Plan backwards with the trades. Run a pull-planning session for the next phase so the people doing the work agree the handoffs.
  8. Remove constraints in the lookahead. Every week, review the next five to six weeks and clear information, materials, permits, access and inspection constraints before work is due.
  9. Measure and learn. Track PPC, RFI turnaround or review turnaround, and hold a short retrospective at the end of each sprint or phase. A sprint retrospective template works for site teams too.
  10. Scale what works. After two or three cycles, extend the practices to other packages, and capture the lessons in a lessons learned review for the next project.

Most construction teams that use Agile ideas end up with layers. Each layer uses the method that suits its horizon and its cost of change.

Contracts that support collaboration

The form of contract shapes how far you can go. Collaborative arrangements make Agile ways of working easier:

  • NEC4 builds early warnings into the contract, obliging parties to raise risks as soon as they become aware of them and discuss them at early warning meetings.
  • Two-stage tendering and early contractor involvement bring the contractor into design reviews, so buildability feedback arrives during design rather than after tender.
  • Alliancing and integrated project delivery (IPD) share risk and reward across the core team, which supports joint planning and open reporting.
  • Traditional lump-sum contracts still allow Agile practices in design before tender and inside the contractor's own site planning, but every change to the contract scope must go through formal change control.

Tools for Agile construction teams

Many site teams prefer physical boards for pull planning, because everyone can stand at the wall together. Digital tools help when teams are spread across sites or the client needs to see the same information.

Most teams combine pull-planning walls on site, specialist construction platforms for drawings, RFIs, inspections and BIM, and a work management platform for design backlogs, approvals and reporting. For that work management layer, Wrike is our top-rated platform and a good fit for design management and multi-project reporting, with Kanban boards, Gantt charts with dependencies and baselines, request forms for approvals and cross-project dashboards. Smartsheet suits teams that like a spreadsheet-style grid with Gantt views and roll-up reports, and monday.com is quick for teams to adopt for visual boards and simple job tracking. Use them alongside the field tools your contractors already use rather than trying to make one product do everything.

Frequently asked questions

Can Agile be used in construction?

Yes, partly. Agile practices work well in design, preconstruction, digital work and site coordination, while the overall project usually keeps a predictive master programme, budget and contract. Most teams run a hybrid.

What is an example of Agile in construction?

A design team working in two-week sprints and reviewing models with the client at the end of each sprint is a common example. On site, weekly work plans agreed with trade foremen, daily huddles and visual boards apply the same ideas.

Can you use Scrum for construction projects?

Scrum fits design teams and self-contained packages such as fit-out better than the whole build. The client representative acts as Product Owner, the designers as Developers, and design reviews as Sprint Reviews, inside the project's formal stage gates.

Is Lean construction the same as Agile?

No, but they overlap. Lean construction applies Lean production ideas such as waste reduction, flow and pull planning to building projects. Agile comes from software and focuses on short delivery cycles with feedback. Both value small batches, visual management and continuous improvement.

What is the Last Planner System?

A Lean construction planning method conceived by Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell and developed with the Lean Construction Institute. It plans in layers, from the master schedule to phase pull planning, lookahead, weekly work plans and learning, with the people doing the work making the weekly commitments.

What is lookahead planning?

Reviewing the next few weeks of work, typically about five to six, to identify and remove constraints such as missing information, materials, permits, access or inspections before the work is due to start.

What is percent plan complete (PPC)?

The number of tasks completed in a week divided by the number committed. It measures how reliable short-term planning is. Teams record a reason for every missed task and act on the most common causes.

What is takt planning?

A method that splits the building into zones with similar work content and moves each trade through the zones at a fixed rhythm. It suits repetitive work such as apartments, hotel rooms and office floors.

Agile vs waterfall in construction: which is better?

Neither on its own. Waterfall-style stage gates and a critical path programme suit the overall build, contracts and approvals. Agile and Lean practices suit design and short-term planning. Combining them usually works best.

How do you start using Agile on a construction project?

Pick one problem, such as late design information or unreliable weekly plans, and one team. Keep the master plan, set a regular cadence, make work visible on a board, remove constraints in a lookahead and measure PPC or turnaround times before scaling up.
