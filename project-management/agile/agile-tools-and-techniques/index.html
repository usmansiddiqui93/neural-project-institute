Agile Tools and Techniques: 20 Essentials With Examples

Agile tools and techniques are the practical methods, such as user stories, backlogs, estimation, task boards and burndown charts, and the software or physical boards that support them, which Agile teams use to plan, deliver and improve work in short cycles. Techniques are what the team does; tools make those techniques easier to run and share. This guide explains 20 essential techniques with worked examples, shows which framework each comes from, and gives you a step-by-step way to choose a tool.

Key takeaways

  • Techniques are practices (user stories, planning poker, WIP limits); tools are what support them (boards, spreadsheets, Agile software).
  • Twenty techniques cover six needs: requirements, prioritisation, estimation, visual flow, tracking and improvement, plus engineering practices for software teams.
  • Start with four: a single ordered backlog, user stories with acceptance criteria, a visible board and a regular retrospective.
  • Forecast with ranges, not single numbers: divide the remaining work by your best and worst recent velocity.
  • Choose software after your process is working on paper, and trial two or three tools on a real sprint.
  • In our tool review, Jira scores highest for Agile and software teams, with ClickUp second; Wrike suits organisations running Agile and non-Agile work side by side.

What are Agile tools and techniques?

Agile techniques are repeatable practices that help a team deliver value in small increments, get fast feedback and improve continuously, for example user stories, relative estimation, timeboxing and retrospectives. Agile tools are the physical or digital aids that support those practices, such as task boards, backlog software, burndown charts and online whiteboards.

The distinction matters. A team can be highly Agile with sticky notes on a wall, and a team can use expensive Agile software while working in a completely traditional way. Get the techniques right first; then pick tools that make them easier, especially for remote teams and reporting.

Most Agile techniques come from a handful of sources: Scrum, Extreme Programming (XP), Kanban and Lean, DSDM and, for large organisations, scaling frameworks such as SAFe. You do not need to adopt a whole framework to use them. Our overview of types of Agile methodologies explains where each one fits.

Requirements and planning techniques

1. User stories

A user story is a short description of something a user needs, written from their point of view: "As a [role], I want [goal] so that [benefit]." Ron Jeffries described stories in terms of the 3 Cs: a Card (the short written statement), a Conversation (the discussion that fills in the detail) and a Confirmation (the tests that show it is done).

Good stories pass the INVEST checklist: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small and Testable. Large stories are called epics and are split before they enter a sprint.

2. Acceptance criteria: a worked example

Acceptance criteria turn the Confirmation into specific, testable statements. Many teams use the Given-When-Then format from behaviour-driven development (BDD).

ElementExample
StoryAs a returning customer, I want to reset my password by email so that I can log in without calling support.
Criterion 1Given I am on the login page, when I select "Forgot password" and enter my registered email, then I receive a reset link within five minutes.
Criterion 2Given I have a reset link, when I use it after 24 hours, then I see a message that the link has expired.
Criterion 3Given I enter an email that is not registered, when I submit the form, then I see the same confirmation message (so account existence is not revealed).

Our free user story template includes fields for the story, acceptance criteria and estimate.

3. Product backlog

The product backlog is a single ordered list of everything that might be done to the product. The Product Owner orders it by value, risk and dependencies, and the top items are refined until they are small and clear. One backlog per product is the rule; several competing lists are a common source of confusion. Try the product backlog template if you are starting in a spreadsheet.

4. Story mapping

Story mapping, popularised by Jeff Patton, lays out the steps of the user's journey from left to right (the backbone) and places stories under each step, most important at the top. Drawing a horizontal line across the map shows the smallest set of stories that gives a complete, usable path: a natural first release.

Example for an online booking service: the backbone is Search, Choose, Book, Pay, Confirm. Release 1 might include basic search, a single room type, card payment and an email confirmation; filters, saved preferences and alternative payment methods go below the line for later releases.

5. Definition of Done

The Definition of Done is the team's shared quality standard, for example: code reviewed, tests passing, documentation updated, accepted by the Product Owner. It applies to every item and prevents "almost done" work from building up.

Prioritisation techniques

Agile teams rarely have time to do everything, so prioritisation is a core skill. Three techniques cover most needs.

6. MoSCoW

MoSCoW, widely used in DSDM, sorts requirements into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time. DSDM guidance suggests keeping Must haves to no more than about 60% of the effort, so there is contingency if estimates are wrong.

CategoryExample: new customer portal release
Must haveSecure login, view open orders, download invoices
Should haveChange delivery address, email notifications
Could haveDark mode, order history export to CSV
Won't have this timeLive chat, mobile app

A free priority list template is a quick way to run a MoSCoW session with stakeholders.

7. Value vs effort matrix

Plot each item on a two-by-two grid: value (high or low) against effort (high or low). Do quick wins (high value, low effort) first, plan big bets (high value, high effort) carefully, fit in fill-ins (low value, low effort) when there is capacity, and challenge money pits (low value, high effort). It is simple enough to use in a 30-minute workshop.

8. Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF): a worked example

WSJF is the prioritisation model used in the Scaled Agile Framework. It divides the cost of delay by the job size. In SAFe, cost of delay is the sum of three relative scores: user-business value, time criticality, and risk reduction or opportunity enablement.

FeatureBusiness valueTime criticalityRisk reductionCost of delayJob sizeWSJF
A: Self-service returns8531682.0
B: Regulatory consent banner5821535.0
C: New recommendations engine133521131.6

Feature B goes first. It is not the most valuable, but it is small and urgent, so delaying it costs more per unit of effort. Feature C has the highest value but is large; splitting it into smaller slices would raise its WSJF score.

Estimation techniques

Agile estimation is usually relative: items are sized against each other rather than in hours. Relative estimates are quicker to agree and, in many teams' experience, more consistent than hour estimates.

9. Planning poker, step by step

  1. The Product Owner reads a story and answers questions.
  2. Each estimator privately chooses a card from a sequence such as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100.
  3. Everyone reveals at once, so nobody anchors on another person's number.
  4. The highest and lowest estimators explain their reasoning.
  5. The team votes again, usually converging within two or three rounds.

Example round: the first votes are 3, 3, 5 and 13. The developer who chose 13 points out that the story needs a change to the payment provider's API. After discussion the second round is 5, 5, 5 and 8, and the team settles on 5 and notes the API dependency as a risk.

10. T-shirt sizing

For early roadmap or backlog sizing, teams assign XS, S, M, L or XL. It is fast, easy for non-technical stakeholders to understand, and good enough to spot items that must be split. Many teams convert sizes to rough point ranges once items move closer to a sprint.

Tip

Never compare story points between teams or use them to judge individuals. Points are a planning aid for one team, not a productivity measure.

Visual management and flow techniques

11. Timeboxing

A timebox is a fixed period for an activity, after which you stop and assess. Sprints, the 15-minute stand-up and the other Agile ceremonies are all timeboxes. The principle is to fix time and cost and let scope flex, which forces prioritisation and gives a regular rhythm of feedback.

12. Task boards

A task board makes the Agile workflow visible: each column is a stage (for example To do, In progress, Review, Done) and each card is a work item. Scrum boards usually show only the current sprint and are reset each sprint; Kanban boards show continuous flow. A free Kanban board template is an easy way to start.

13. WIP limits

A WIP limit caps the number of items allowed in a column at once. Example: a team of five sets a limit of four on In progress and two on Review. When Review is full, a developer who finishes a task helps review existing work instead of starting something new. The result is fewer half-finished items and shorter cycle times. Several people working together to finish one item in this way is known as swarming.

Tracking and forecasting techniques

14. Burndown chart: a worked example

A sprint burndown chart plots the work remaining (in points, tasks or hours) against the days of the sprint, with an ideal straight line from the total to zero. Here is a 10-day sprint with 40 points committed:

Day0246810
Ideal remaining4032241680
Actual remaining40403123133

The flat start (no change by day 2) is typical when stories are too large to finish quickly; the team was working, but nothing was done yet. The 3 points left at the end go back to the Product Backlog. The lesson for the retrospective might be to split stories smaller so progress shows earlier.

15. Burnup chart

A burnup chart shows completed work rising towards a separate line for total scope. Because scope is shown on its own line, stakeholders can see when the target moves because work was added, which a burndown hides. Burnups are the usual choice for release and project-level reporting.

16. Velocity and forecasting: a worked example

Velocity is the amount of work a team completes per sprint, usually in story points. Use it to forecast with a range:

  1. Take your last three to five sprints, for example 22, 26 and 24 points (average 24).
  2. Find the remaining backlog for the release, for example 150 points.
  3. Divide by the best and worst velocity: 150 ÷ 26 = 5.8 and 150 ÷ 22 = 6.8.
  4. Round up: the release needs 6 to 7 sprints, or 12 to 14 weeks with two-week sprints.
  5. Update the forecast every sprint as velocity and scope change.

17. Cumulative flow diagram

A cumulative flow diagram is a stacked area chart showing how many items are in each stage over time. Parallel bands mean stable flow; a band that widens shows work piling up in that stage, which is where to look for a bottleneck. It is the core chart for Kanban teams.

Feedback and improvement techniques

18. Retrospectives

A retrospective is a regular, structured reflection on how the team is working, ending with one or two concrete improvements. Formats such as Start-Stop-Continue, Mad-Sad-Glad and the Sailboat keep it fresh. The key technique is follow-through: give each action an owner, add it to the next sprint and check it at the following retrospective.

Two related feedback practices are the sprint review or demo, where stakeholders try working results and shape what comes next, and the daily stand-up, where the team adjusts its plan every day. Our guide to Agile meetings and ceremonies includes agendas for each.

Engineering techniques for software teams

Extreme Programming contributed the technical practices that let software teams deliver small increments safely. Non-software teams can skip this section, though the ideas behind pairing and frequent integration apply widely.

19. Pair and mob programming

In pair programming, two developers work at one screen: one writes the code while the other reviews and thinks ahead, swapping roles often. In mob (or ensemble) programming, the whole team works on one task together. Both spread knowledge, catch defects early and reduce handoffs.

20. Test-driven development and continuous integration

  • Test-driven development (TDD): write a failing automated test, write just enough code to pass it, then refactor. Repeat in small steps.
  • Continuous integration (CI): every developer merges small changes into the main code line at least daily, with an automated build and tests on each merge.
  • Continuous delivery: keep the product in a releasable state at all times so the team can release whenever value is ready.
  • Refactoring: improve the structure of existing code without changing its behaviour, to keep it easy to change.

How to choose an Agile tool step by step

  1. Run your process on paper first. Use a physical or simple digital board for two or three sprints so you know which techniques you actually use.
  2. List your must-haves. For example: an orderable backlog, sprint planning with capacity, configurable boards with WIP limits, burndown or burnup charts, roadmap views, and integrations with chat, documents or code repositories.
  3. Consider who else needs access. Stakeholders often need read-only views; other departments may need Gantt charts or resource planning.
  4. Shortlist two or three tools that fit your method and team type, using the table above.
  5. Trial them on a real sprint, not a demo project, with the people who will use them daily.
  6. Check administration and security: single sign-on, permissions, data residency and how easily you can export your data.
  7. Configure lightly and review after a month. Add fields, workflows and automations only when the team asks for them.
Tip

If two tools seem equal, pick the one your team will open every day. An unused tool is the most expensive option.

Adding every technique at once overwhelms a team. This sequence works well for a new team on two-week sprints:

Use the retrospective at the end of each sprint to decide whether to keep, adapt or drop each technique. For a full roll-out approach, read how to implement Agile.

Agile techniques for non-software teams

Most of these techniques work well outside software, with small changes in language:

  • Marketing: a backlog of campaign ideas ordered by expected impact, two-week sprints, a board with Draft, Review, Approved and Live columns, and WIP limits on items waiting for approval. See Agile marketing.
  • HR and recruitment: a Kanban board per vacancy pipeline, cycle time from application to offer, and monthly retrospectives on hiring bottlenecks.
  • Operations and IT support: a Kanban board with an expedite lane for urgent requests, WIP limits and a cumulative flow diagram to spot backlogs building up.
  • Creative teams: user stories framed around the audience ("As a first-time visitor, I want..."), T-shirt sizing and regular reviews with clients.

Engineering practices such as TDD do not transfer directly, but their principles (build quality in, work in small batches, get feedback early) do.

Common mistakes with Agile tools and techniques

  • Buying a tool before agreeing a process. The tool's default workflow then becomes your process by accident.
  • Configuring every field on day one. Complex tools slow teams down; start with the minimum and add as needed.
  • Keeping several backlogs. One product, one ordered backlog.
  • Treating velocity as a target. It leads to inflated estimates rather than more output.
  • Writing stories as tasks. "Update database table" is a task; a story describes value for a user.
  • Skipping acceptance criteria. Without them, "done" becomes a matter of opinion.
  • Measuring activity instead of outcomes. Ticket counts say little about value delivered.
  • Copying another team's setup. Techniques should fit your context; adapt them in retrospectives.

Frequently asked questions

What are Agile tools and techniques?

Agile techniques are practices such as user stories, backlogs, relative estimation, timeboxing, task boards, burndown charts and retrospectives. Agile tools are the boards, templates and software that support those practices.

What are the most common Agile techniques?

User stories, an ordered product backlog, story mapping, planning poker and story points, task boards, WIP limits, burndown and burnup charts, velocity, timeboxing, retrospectives and MoSCoW prioritisation.

What is the difference between an Agile tool and an Agile technique?

A technique is something the team does, such as estimating with planning poker. A tool supports it, such as a planning poker app or a backlog in Jira. Techniques matter more; tools make them easier.

What is the INVEST checklist?

A test for good user stories: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small and Testable.

What are the 3 Cs of user stories?

Card, Conversation and Confirmation: a short written statement, the discussion that adds detail, and the acceptance tests that confirm it is done.

What is MoSCoW prioritisation?

Sorting requirements into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time. It is widely used in DSDM and other Agile approaches to agree scope with stakeholders.

What is WSJF?

Weighted Shortest Job First, the prioritisation model in SAFe. It divides the cost of delay (business value + time criticality + risk reduction or opportunity enablement) by job size, so small, urgent, valuable items go first.

What is the difference between a burndown and a burnup chart?

A burndown shows work remaining falling towards zero. A burnup shows work completed rising towards a separate scope line, so it also shows when scope changes.

Do I need software to be Agile?

No. A whiteboard and sticky notes work well for co-located teams. Software helps with remote work, reporting, integrations and scale.

Can Agile teams use spreadsheets?

Yes, especially at first. A spreadsheet can hold a backlog, sprint plan and simple burndown. Dedicated tools make boards, collaboration and reporting easier as the team grows.

Is Jira the only Agile tool?

No. ClickUp, Wrike, monday.com, Asana, Trello, Azure DevOps, Linear and Notion are also widely used. In our review Jira scores highest for Agile and software teams, and ClickUp second.

What is the best Agile tool?

The one your team will use every day and that supports your method. Jira suits software teams; Wrike suits organisations mixing Agile and traditional projects; Trello suits small teams wanting simple boards. Trial two or three on a real sprint.

Which Agile techniques work for non-software teams?

Backlogs, user stories, task boards, WIP limits, timeboxed sprints, MoSCoW and retrospectives all work well in marketing, HR, operations and creative teams.
