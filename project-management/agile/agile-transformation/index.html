Agile Transformation: Roadmap, Stages and Examples

An Agile transformation is an organisation-wide change in how work is prioritised, funded, organised, governed and led, so that the organisation can deliver value in small increments and change direction quickly. It goes well beyond teams adopting Scrum or Kanban: structures, budgets, HR practices and leadership behaviour change too. This guide explains what changes, how to phase the work over several years, how to measure it and why so many transformations stall.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 7 September 2026
  • Last updated 4 November 2026
  • 19 min read

Key takeaways

  • An Agile transformation changes the operating model: strategy and portfolio, structure, funding, governance, people practices, technology and leadership. Team practices are only one of seven dimensions.
  • Start from business problems you can measure, not from a framework. The framework is a means to an outcome.
  • Work in phases with exit criteria: discover, design the target operating model, prove it in pilots, expand in waves, then embed.
  • Moving from project funding to persistent product or value-stream funding is usually the change that makes the rest stick.
  • Measure outcomes such as lead time, quality, customer results and engagement. Counting trained people or "Agile teams" measures activity, not results.
  • Most transformations stall because leaders change the teams but not the system around them. Plan for years, not months, and run the transformation itself in short cycles.

What is an Agile transformation?

An Agile transformation is a deliberate, organisation-wide change to the operating model so that strategy, funding, structure, governance and leadership support small, frequent delivery and fast learning. Teams work in short cycles with clear product ownership, money follows long-lived products or value streams, decisions move closer to the work and leaders manage outcomes rather than plans.

The term is often used loosely. Three different kinds of change are frequently confused:

Agile adoption (implementing Agile)Agile transformationDigital transformation
Main goalTeams deliver in an Agile wayThe whole organisation can adapt quicklyUse technology to change products, services or processes
ScopeSeveral teams or a departmentThe organisation and its operating modelTechnology, data, channels and processes
What changesTeam practices, roles, local governance and metricsStructure, funding, governance, HR, portfolio management and cultureSystems, platforms, customer journeys and data
Led bySponsor, change lead and coachesExecutive team with a transformation officeExecutive team, often led by technology and digital leaders
Typical timescaleMonths for the first teamsTypically several yearsTypically several years

They often run together. Most organisations begin with adoption in a few teams and then decide whether a full transformation is needed.

Why organisations transform

A transformation needs a reason leaders will still care about in year three: specific business problems the current operating model keeps producing.

Problem the business feelsHow the current operating model causes itWhat a transformation changes
New products and changes take too long to reach customersAnnual planning, large projects and many hand-offs between departmentsSmall, cross-functional teams release in short cycles
Too many priorities and nothing finishesEvery department funds its own projects; no single place to trade offOne portfolio view, limits on work in progress, regular re-prioritisation
Customer feedback arrives too late to act onRequirements are fixed at the start and users see results at the endRegular reviews with users and outcome-based targets
Teams are rebuilt for every projectFunding and staffing follow temporary projectsStable teams funded around products or value streams

Weak reasons, such as "our competitors are Agile" or buying a tool and hoping the way of working follows, give no way to judge success.

Write the case in outcome terms: problem, cost, measures and leadership commitments. A business case template and a gap analysis template are practical starting points.

The seven dimensions of an Agile transformation

A useful way to scope a transformation is to look at seven dimensions of the operating model. Team practices usually change first; the other six lag behind unless someone owns them explicitly.

DimensionTypical starting pointTarget stateUsually owned by
1. Strategy and portfolioAnnual plan; projects approved one at a timeQuarterly priorities and outcomes; one visible portfolio; limits on work in progressExecutive team, portfolio office
2. StructureFunctional departments; temporary project teamsLong-lived, cross-functional teams aligned to products, customers or value streamsExecutive team, HR
3. FundingBudget per project, fixed scope and costPersistent funding for products or value streams, reviewed regularlyFinance
4. Governance and riskStage gates, change boards, document-based approvalsOutcome-based reviews, risk-based controls built into the workRisk, compliance, PMO
5. People and HRJob titles by function, annual individual appraisalsRole-based career paths, team and individual feedback, skills growthHR
6. Technology and engineeringManual testing and release, large batch deploymentsAutomated testing, continuous integration and frequent releaseTechnology leadership
7. Leadership and cultureCommand and control; status reporting upwardsLeaders set direction and remove obstacles; transparency is safeEvery leader

If your plan only names owners for structure and part of leadership, you are planning adoption, not transformation. That is a legitimate choice, but set expectations accordingly.

Tip

Score each dimension from 1 (traditional) to 5 (target) with its owners, and repeat every quarter. The trend matters more than the starting score.

Choosing an operating model

There is no single Agile structure. Most organisations land on one of three broad options, or a mix by division.

Option 1: Agile teams inside the functional structure

Teams adopt Scrum or Kanban, but people still report to functional managers and budgets still follow projects. It is the least disruptive option and a common first step, but hand-offs between departments remain.

Option 2: Product or value-stream teams

Long-lived, cross-functional teams are aligned to a product, customer journey or value stream, and funding follows them. Expertise is kept through communities of practice. Most of the end-to-end benefit comes from here. Use value stream mapping to find sensible boundaries.

Option 3: A network model

The whole organisation, including business functions, is reorganised into small teams grouped around missions, with separate structures for skills and knowledge sharing. The squads, tribes, chapters and guilds terminology comes from a 2012 paper by Henrik Kniberg and Anders Ivarsson describing how Spotify worked at the time. Treat it as one example, not a template; see squads, tribes, chapters and guilds.

Agile teams in functionsProduct or value-stream teamsNetwork model
DisruptionLowMedium to highVery high
End-to-end speed gainLimited by hand-offsLargeLarge
Changes to reporting linesFewManyAlmost all
Best whenStarting out, or work is mostly within one functionCustomer outcomes need several functionsThe whole business must work this way
Main riskStalls at team levelUnclear skills ownershipCopying a model without understanding it

Scaling frameworks such as the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) and Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS) help when many teams work on one product and must plan together. They are not required; choose one only when coordination problems appear. Our guide to Agile team structures compares team options.

Moving from project funding to product funding

In project funding, money is approved for a defined scope, a team is assembled, the project ends and the team disbands. In product (or value-stream) funding, a stable team receives a budget for a period and its work is prioritised continually against outcomes. This is often the most important structural change, because it removes the incentive to fix scope up front.

Project fundingProduct or value-stream funding
Unit of fundingA project with fixed scopeA stable team or group of teams
When decidedOnce, at approvalAt regular portfolio reviews
Changing prioritiesChange request and re-approvalRe-order the backlog; reallocate at the next review
What is measuredOn time, on budget, to scopeOutcomes, flow and cost per period
Team continuityFormed and disbanded per projectKept together; knowledge stays
Typical concernLate discovery that the scope was wrongFinance needs confidence that money is well spent

Worked example: three projects versus two product teams (illustrative)

A customer-service division has £3.0 million for next year. Under project funding it approves three projects: a new web self-service portal (£1.4 million), a chatbot (£0.9 million) and a complaints-system upgrade (£0.7 million). Each has a fixed scope and its own team.

Under product funding, the division funds two persistent teams for four quarters: a Digital self-service team (£1.6 million) and a Complaints and case handling team (£1.4 million). Each team has a product owner and outcome targets, for example raising the share of queries resolved online and cutting complaint resolution time.

QuarterWhat happens under product funding
Q1Self-service team releases the three most-used online journeys first; complaints team automates case routing
Q2Data shows the chatbot idea would duplicate the improved online journeys; the review drops it and reallocates effort to search and help content
Q3Complaints resolution time has fallen; the review moves budget for one quarter of a developer pair from complaints to self-service
Q4Both teams continue; next year's funding is set on results, not on a new set of project bids

The money is the same, but the chatbot is cancelled after learning rather than after spending £0.9 million, and the teams stay together. Finance keeps control through quarterly reviews, a fixed envelope and published outcome measures.

Who leads an Agile transformation

The executive team owns the outcome; a small transformation office runs the change like a product.

RoleResponsibilities
Executive sponsorOwns the business case and outcomes; chairs the quarterly transformation review; removes structural obstacles
Leadership teamChanges its own planning, funding and decision habits; models transparency
Transformation lead and officeMaintains the transformation backlog, plans waves, tracks the scorecard, coordinates coaching and communications
Agile coachesCoach teams, product owners and leaders; spot systemic obstacles and raise them
Finance and HR partnersRedesign funding, job architecture, performance management and career paths
PMO or Agile PMOMoves from compliance checking to portfolio visibility, capacity and enabling teams; see Agile PMO
Change championsRespected staff in each area who share experience, gather feedback and answer questions

Run the transformation itself in short cycles: keep a visible backlog of transformation work (for example "design product-funding pilot with finance"), review it monthly and re-plan quarterly. A communication plan template keeps messages consistent across waves.

Agile transformation roadmap: five phases

Stage models vary, but most describe the same arc. The phases overlap; move on when the exit criteria are met, not when the calendar says so.

PhaseIllustrative durationKey outputsExit criteria
1. Discover and decide2–3 monthsBusiness case, current-state assessment, sponsor and leadership commitmentsLeaders agree the problems, measures and their own commitments
2. Design the target operating model2–4 months (overlaps phase 3)Draft structure, funding approach, governance changes, role profilesA first version is agreed and ready to test, not perfect
3. Prove it in pilots4–9 monthsTwo or three pilot areas working in the new model, with measured resultsPilots beat their baselines and the system obstacles are known
4. Expand in waves1–3 yearsSuccessive groups of teams move to the new model; funding and HR changes roll outMost of the target scope works in the new model
5. Embed and sustainOngoingPolicies, budgeting and leadership development reflect the new modelThe new way survives leadership changes and budget pressure

Durations are illustrative for an organisation of several thousand people; regulated organisations usually need longer in phases 2 and 4.

Phase 1: Discover and decide

  1. Name three to five business problems and attach a measure to each.
  2. Assess the seven dimensions with the people who own each one.
  3. Map one or two end-to-end value streams; most delay sits in waiting and hand-offs, not in the work itself.
  4. Agree with leaders what will change in how they plan, fund, approve and report.
  5. Decide the scope and the sponsor.

Phase 2: Design the target operating model

  1. Choose an operating model option and draft team boundaries around products or value streams.
  2. Agree with finance how pilot areas will be funded and reviewed.
  3. Agree with risk and compliance how control objectives will be met inside the delivery cycle.
  4. Draft role profiles and agree how line management of skills will work.
  5. Keep the design light: it is a hypothesis the pilots will test.

Phase 3: Prove it in pilots

Pilots test the operating model, not just Scrum or Kanban. Record every obstacle that comes from outside the team, such as a funding rule, an approval step or an HR policy. That list is the most valuable input to phase 4.

Phase 4: Expand in waves

  1. Group the next teams into waves of a manageable size, typically one value stream or business area at a time.
  2. Fix the systemic obstacles the pilots found before the next wave starts.
  3. Seed each new wave with people from earlier waves who can coach by example.
  4. Change funding, governance and HR for each wave as it moves, not for the whole organisation at once.
  5. Add cross-team coordination, such as a shared planning event, only where teams genuinely depend on each other.
  6. Review the scorecard after every wave and adjust the plan for the next one.

Phase 5: Embed and sustain

Embedding means the model no longer depends on the transformation office: budgeting uses product funding by default, performance management rewards team outcomes and new hires learn the Agile way of working from day one. Retire the office gradually.

Changing governance, risk and compliance

Governance is where many transformations quietly fail: teams deliver every two weeks, but approvals arrive once a quarter. The aim is to meet the same control objectives with lighter mechanisms built into the work.

Traditional mechanismControl objectiveAgile equivalent
Stage gates with document sign-offSpend is justified and on trackQuarterly outcome reviews against measures; funding continues or stops
Change advisory board for every releaseChanges do not break live servicesAutomated testing, small releases, risk-based approval for high-impact changes
Monthly written status reportsLeaders know progress and riskLive dashboards plus attendance at sprint or iteration reviews
Detailed requirements approved up frontThe right thing is builtProduct owner accountability, regular user feedback, a clear Definition of Done
Risk register reviewed at gatesRisks are identified and managedRisks reviewed in planning and reviews; escalation routes kept

In regulated sectors, involve risk and compliance teams from phase 2 and show how each control objective is still met and evidenced. Our guides to project governance and change control explain the principles.

The people side: roles, careers and HR

People judge a transformation by what happens to their job and prospects. If HR practices still reward individual heroics and functional loyalty, behaviour will follow them, whatever the training says.

HR practiceBeforeAfter
Job architectureTitles by department and gradeRole families such as product owner, engineer, analyst and coach, with skills levels
Performance managementAnnual individual appraisal against fixed objectivesFrequent feedback; team outcomes and individual growth both count
IncentivesBonuses tied to personal or departmental targetsRewards linked to shared outcomes and collaboration
Career pathsProgress mainly by managing more peopleProgress by deepening expertise or broadening scope, with or without line reports
Line managementManager assigns work and approvesManager develops people and skills; teams plan their own work

What happens to project managers and middle managers

Many project managers move into roles such as Scrum Master, product owner, delivery lead or Agile project manager; others coordinate across teams, manage portfolios or run complex programmes that still need planning and dependency management. Middle managers often shift from allocating work to building capability and removing obstacles. Be honest early about which roles change, and offer training and real choices.

Culture follows these changes rather than leading them. Workshops on the Agile mindset help, but people believe what the organisation rewards.

Measuring an Agile transformation

Measure whether the business problems are being solved and whether the system is changing. Keep the scorecard small and publish it quarterly.

AreaExample measuresLeading or lagging
Flow and speedLead time from idea to customer; release frequency; work in progress per teamLeading
QualityDefects found after release; incidents; reworkLeading and lagging
Customer and businessCustomer satisfaction; adoption of new features; revenue, cost or service targets in the business caseLagging
PeopleEngagement; retention of key skills; team stabilityLeading
System changeShare of spend on product funding; approval time for releases; seven-dimension assessment scoresLeading

Track lead time end to end, not only inside teams; the difference between cycle time and lead time shows how much time is lost before work starts. Our Agile metrics and KPIs guide covers team-level measures in detail, and an OKR template works well for quarterly transformation goals.

Tip

Avoid headline measures such as the number of people trained, the number of "Agile teams" or velocity compared between teams. They are easy to raise without changing anything that matters to customers.

Real example: ING Netherlands

ING's change in the Netherlands is one of the most widely cited bank transformations. In a McKinsey Quarterly interview published in January 2017, Peter Jacobs (CIO, ING Netherlands) and Bart Schlatmann (former COO) described how it worked:

  • Strategy work began in late 2014, and the new way of working was introduced in June 2015.
  • The initial scope was about 3,500 staff at headquarters, including marketing, product management, channel management and IT. After the change, the organisation had about 2,500 people in those roles.
  • All headquarters staff were asked to reapply for positions in the new organisation; nearly 40 percent ended up in a different role from before.
  • Work was organised into about 350 squads of nine people, grouped into 13 tribes.
  • The ambition was to release changes every two weeks instead of five to six large launches a year.

Support functions such as HR, finance, risk, operations and IT infrastructure were outside the first phase. The useful lessons are that business and IT were reorganised together and the bank designed its own model after studying others. The reapplication process suited its situation; it is not a step every organisation needs.

Worked example: a three-year transformation (illustrative)

Northfield Mutual is a fictional insurer with about 2,000 staff. Changes to its claims and policy systems take around nine months to reach customers, departments compete for the same specialists, and customer complaints about slow claims are rising. The board sponsors an Agile transformation with three goals: deliver small improvements at least monthly, cut average claims handling time and stop the churn of project teams.

PeriodWhat changesMeasures at the end of the period (illustrative)Decision
Months 0–3Business case agreed; seven-dimension assessment; claims value stream mapped; executive team agrees to quarterly funding reviews for pilotsBaseline: lead time about 9 months; releases twice a yearProceed with three pilots in claims
Months 4–12Three claims teams formed around customer journeys, each with a business product owner; pilots funded as one claims product; light release review replaces the change board for low-risk changesPilot areas release monthly; lead time for small changes under 2 months; engagement up in pilot teamsExpand to the whole claims value stream; fix HR role profiles first
Year 2Claims becomes a product-funded value stream with eight teams; policy administration starts as wave 2; new role families and team-based objectives introducedClaims handling time down; post-release defects stable despite faster releasesWave 3 adds digital channels; finance adopts product funding for all change spend in these areas
Year 3Annual budget allocated to value streams by default; PMO becomes a portfolio office; transformation office shrinks to two coachesMost change spend product-funded; new leaders onboarded into the modelTransformation office closed; quarterly scorecard continues as normal governance

Note what did not happen: no big-bang reorganisation, no single framework imposed everywhere and no declaration of success after the pilots. Each wave was approved on evidence from the previous one.

Why Agile transformations fail

Failure rarely looks dramatic. The transformation fades: meetings continue, but decisions, funding and reporting work as before. Watch for these warning signs.

Failure patternEarly warning signResponse
Renaming without changingProject managers retitled Scrum Masters; phases renamed sprints; approvals unchangedCheck the seven dimensions; change at least funding and governance for each wave
Leaders exempt themselvesExecutives ask for fixed dates and scope while teams are told to be adaptiveAgree written leadership commitments; review them in the quarterly transformation review
Framework as the goalSuccess measured by framework compliance or certification countsReturn to the business case measures
Big-bang roll-outWhole organisation reorganised in one go with no pilot evidencePause and move in waves; fix what the first wave learns
Copying another company's modelTerminology adopted from a case study without the underlying conditionsDesign for your own value streams and constraints
Too little coachingA two-day course, then teams are on their ownFund coaches for each wave and for leaders, not just teams
Engineering left behindTeams plan in sprints but release quarterly because testing and deployment are manualInvest in automation and DevOps practices alongside the operating model
Loss of sponsorSponsor leaves or moves; reviews are cancelledBuild sponsorship across the executive team, not in one person; embed changes in policy early

Tools that support a transformation at scale

Tools should follow the operating model, not define it. At organisation scale you need three things from them: a single view of the portfolio, visible flow of work across teams and dashboards that replace written status reports.

  • Wrike, our top-rated work management platform, suits cross-functional transformations where business and technical teams share a portfolio, thanks to its custom workflows, dashboards and resource planning on higher tiers.
  • Jira is our pick for software and Agile teams, with Scrum and Kanban boards and backlog management built in.
  • monday.com and Asana are strong for visual workflows and cross-team goals, and are quick for business teams to adopt.
  • Smartsheet suits organisations that prefer spreadsheet-style enterprise planning and reporting.

Frequently asked questions

What is an Agile transformation?

An organisation-wide change in how work is prioritised, funded, organised, governed and led, so that value is delivered in small increments and the organisation adapts quickly. It changes structures, budgets, HR practices and leadership behaviour, not just team methods.

How long does an Agile transformation take?

Usually several years for a large organisation. Pilot areas can show results within months, but funding, governance, HR and leadership habits take much longer to change. Plan in phases with exit criteria rather than a fixed end date.

What are the stages of an Agile transformation?

Discover and decide; design the target operating model; prove it in pilots; expand in waves; embed and sustain. The phases overlap, and you move on when each phase's exit criteria are met.

What is the difference between Agile adoption and Agile transformation?

Adoption means teams start using Agile practices such as Scrum or Kanban. A transformation also changes the structure, funding, governance, HR and leadership around them.

What is the difference between Agile transformation and digital transformation?

Digital transformation uses technology to change products, services and processes. Agile transformation changes how the organisation works and decides. They often run together.

Who leads an Agile transformation?

The executive team, with a named sponsor. A small transformation office runs it day to day, supported by coaches, finance and HR partners, the PMO and change champions.

Why do Agile transformations fail?

Usually because leaders change the teams but not their own behaviour, funding and governance still expect fixed plans, a framework becomes the goal, the roll-out is big-bang, coaching is thin or the sponsor leaves.

Do we need a scaling framework such as SAFe?

Not necessarily. SAFe or LeSS help when many teams work on one product and must plan together. Add one only where real dependency problems appear.

What does an Agile coach do in a transformation?

Coaches teach and mentor teams, product owners and leaders, and surface systemic obstacles, such as funding rules or approval steps, to the transformation office.

How do you measure Agile transformation success?

With a small outcome scorecard: end-to-end lead time, release frequency, quality, customer satisfaction, business-case results, engagement and signs of system change such as the share of spend on product funding.

What happens to project managers and middle managers?

Many project managers become Scrum Masters, product owners, delivery leads or Agile project managers; others run portfolios or complex programmes. Middle managers shift from assigning work to developing people and removing obstacles.

What is an Agile transformation office?

A small team that runs the transformation like a product: it keeps the transformation backlog, plans waves, coordinates coaching, tracks the scorecard and escalates obstacles. It should shrink as the new model becomes normal.

Can non-IT departments go through an Agile transformation?

Yes. Marketing, operations, HR, finance and service teams can work in Agile ways, often with Kanban-style flow. Transformations that include business functions usually achieve more than those that stay inside IT; see is Agile only for software?

What is the first step in an Agile transformation?

Agree the business problems to solve and how to measure them, and secure the leadership team's commitment to change its own planning, funding and decisions, before choosing frameworks or tools.
