Agile Release Train (ART): Roles, Events and Example

An Agile Release Train (ART) is a long-lived team of Agile teams, typically 50 to 125 people, that plans, commits and delivers together on a shared cadence in the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe). This guide explains how an ART is structured, who does what, which events it runs each Planning Interval and how to launch one step by step. It finishes with an illustrative worked example, a comparison with other team structures and the mistakes that most often derail a train.

Key takeaways

  • An Agile Release Train is a long-lived team of Agile teams, generally 50 to 125 people, organised around a flow of value and able to define, build, test and release its solution.
  • Since SAFe 6.0 (2023), PI means Planning Interval, the program backlog is the ART backlog and the program board is the ART planning board; older names are still common in tools.
  • Four roles lead the train: the Release Train Engineer, Product Management, the System Architect and Business Owners, supported by Product Owners and Scrum Masters/Team Coaches in each team.
  • Every PI follows the same rhythm: PI planning, synchronised iterations, ART Sync, System Demos and Inspect and Adapt.
  • Launch a train deliberately: choose the value stream, define the ART, set the PI calendar, train everyone, prepare the ART backlog, then run the first PI planning.
  • Most ART failures come from organising around departments instead of value, overloading the plan and treating SAFe events as status meetings.

What is an Agile Release Train?

An Agile Release Train is a long-lived team of Agile teams, usually 50 to 125 people, that plans together every Planning Interval and delivers a shared solution on a common cadence. It contains every skill needed to define, build, test and release that solution, and it is organised around a flow of customer value rather than around departments.

The ART is the central delivery structure of the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) and appears in every SAFe configuration. Scaled Agile describes it as "a long-lived team of Agile teams" that builds, delivers and often runs one or more solutions.

You need a train when a solution is too large for one team. A single Agile team of ten or fewer people can manage its own backlog. Once eight teams work on the same product, someone has to align their priorities, dates, dependencies and integration. The ART gives them one backlog, one calendar, one set of events and shared leadership roles, while each team still works internally with SAFe Scrum or SAFe Kanban.

SAFe 6.0 terminology: Planning Interval and other changes

SAFe 6.0, released by Scaled Agile in 2023, renamed several terms you will meet on any train. Scaled Agile has added articles and competencies since then, but 6.0 remains the current numbered version. Many organisations, job adverts and tools still use the older names, so it helps to know both.

Older termCurrent SAFe 6.0 termWhat it means
Program Increment (PI)Planning Interval (PI)The fixed timebox, usually 8 to 12 weeks, in which the train plans and delivers
Program backlogART backlogThe prioritised list of features and enablers for the whole train
Program KanbanART KanbanThe board that shows how features flow from idea to release
Program boardART planning boardThe visual plan of features, milestones and cross-team dependencies by iteration
Scrum of ScrumsCoach Sync (part of ART Sync)The regular cross-team coordination meeting of Scrum Masters/Team Coaches
Scrum MasterScrum Master/Team CoachThe team-level coach and facilitator

The abbreviation PI did not change, so the event is still called PI planning. This guide uses the current names.

Why it is called a release train

The name comes from a railway timetable. A train leaves at fixed, published times: if your freight is ready, it goes on this train; if not, it waits for the next one, and the timetable does not move for late cargo. On an ART, the dates of PI planning, iterations and demos are fixed in advance, and features that are not ready move to the next PI instead of delaying everyone.

SAFe sums this up as develop on cadence, release on demand. Planning, integration and demos follow the shared calendar, but a team can release a feature whenever it is ready and the business wants it, using continuous delivery practices. The train is about synchronised planning, not about holding work back until the end of a PI.

ART roles and responsibilities

An ART has a small set of train-level roles plus the usual roles inside each team.

RoleLevelMain responsibility

Product ManagementTrainOwns the ART backlog and vision; defines and prioritises features

Business OwnersTrainSenior stakeholders accountable for business outcomes; assign business value to PI objectives
Product OwnerTeamOwns the team backlog; turns features into stories; accepts work

Release Train Engineer

The RTE is often called the chief Scrum Master of the train. They prepare and facilitate PI planning, ART Sync, System Demos and Inspect and Adapt, track risks and dependencies, escalate impediments and coach leaders. They do not assign work or own scope: Product Management owns the what, and teams own the how. Many RTEs come from project or programme management, but the job is facilitation and coaching rather than command and control.

Product Management and the System Architect

Product Management is the content authority. It turns strategy into a vision and roadmap, writes and prioritises features, and works with the Product Owners who refine those features into team-level user stories. The System Architect defines the technical direction, plans enablers and helps teams resolve technical dependencies, keeping the solution coherent without becoming a design approval gate. On large trains both are often small teams rather than single people.

Business Owners

Business Owners are typically a handful of senior people accountable for the value the train delivers, often a business sponsor, a technology leader and someone from operations. They present context at PI planning, score each team's PI objectives for business value, attend System Demos and help resolve risks. Their attendance is one of the clearest signs of whether leadership takes the train seriously.

How teams are organised on an ART

Deciding which teams sit on the train, and what each owns, is the most important design choice you will make. SAFe recommends organising around value and draws on the four team types from *Team Topologies* by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais.

Team typePurposeExample on an ART
Stream-alignedDelivers value directly to customers along a flow of workA team that owns the online quote journey end to end
Complicated subsystemOwns a part of the system that needs deep specialist knowledgeA pricing or risk-calculation engine team
PlatformProvides internal services that make other teams fasterA team running shared APIs, data pipelines or test environments
EnablingHelps other teams build new skills for a periodA small group coaching teams on test automation

Most teams on a healthy train should be stream-aligned. The other types exist to reduce the load on stream-aligned teams, not to become queues that every feature must pass through.

Choosing the boundary of the train

  1. Map the development value stream: the steps and people needed to turn a customer request into a released solution. Our value stream mapping guide shows how.
  2. Group people so that most features can be delivered inside one train, ideally inside one team.
  3. Check the size. Well above 125 people, split along a natural seam such as customer segment or product line; well below 50, consider joining a neighbouring train or a lighter approach.
  4. Name the remaining cross-train dependencies and agree how they will be handled, for example through shared services.

For more team shapes, see Agile team structures.

The ART calendar: iterations and the Planning Interval

A Planning Interval is a fixed timebox, typically 8 to 12 weeks. With two-week iterations, a common pattern is four development iterations followed by one Innovation and Planning (IP) iteration. Every team on the train uses the same dates.

WeeksIterationWhat happens
Before week 1Previous IP iterationPI planning for this PI
1–2Iteration 1Teams start the PI plan; System Demo at the end
3–4Iteration 2Delivery continues; dependencies tracked at ART Sync
5–6Iteration 3Mid-PI check against objectives; releases as features are ready
7–8Iteration 4Final development iteration and System Demo
9–10IP iterationInnovation, learning, final integration, Inspect and Adapt, next PI planning

The IP iteration is also the PI's main buffer. Fill it with planned features and you lose both the time for improvement and the margin for estimates that prove wrong.

Features come from the ART backlog, which Product Management orders using Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF): cost of delay divided by job size. The ART Kanban shows each feature's flow from funnel through analysis, implementation and release; WIP limits on it stop the train starting more than it can finish.

ART events

SAFe defines train-level events that run on the PI cadence. Team-level events (iteration planning, stand-ups, review and retrospective) continue inside each team. Durations are typical; adjust them to your train.

EventWhenWho attendsPurpose and output
PI planningOnce per PI, usually two daysEveryone on the train plus Business OwnersTeam PI objectives with business value, ART planning board, ROAMed risks, confidence vote
ART SyncWeekly or twice weekly, about 30 to 60 minutesRTE, Product Management, Product Owners, Scrum Masters/Team CoachesProgress against objectives, dependencies, impediments, scope adjustments
System DemoEnd of every iterationTeams, Product Management, Business Owners, stakeholdersIntegrated increment shown working; feedback
Inspect and AdaptEnd of every PI, in the IP iterationWhole train plus Business OwnersPI system demo, measures review and a problem-solving workshop

ART Sync: Coach Sync and PO Sync

ART Sync combines two conversations. The Coach Sync (formerly Scrum of Scrums) brings the RTE and Scrum Masters/Team Coaches together on progress, dependencies and impediments. The PO Sync brings Product Management and Product Owners together on scope and priorities. Small trains often hold one combined meeting; larger trains often run them on different days. Our Scrum of Scrums guide covers the facilitation technique.

System Demo and Inspect and Adapt

The System Demo shows the integrated work of all teams, not a series of separate team demos. If teams can only show their own components on their own machines, the train is not yet integrating, and that is the first thing to fix.

Inspect and Adapt is the train's retrospective, scaled up. It has three parts: a PI System Demo of everything delivered, a quantitative and qualitative review of measures such as predictability, and a structured problem-solving workshop in which the train finds root causes of its biggest problems and adds improvement items to the ART backlog.

How to launch an Agile Release Train step by step

Scaled Agile's implementation roadmap places the ART launch after leaders have been trained and value streams identified. Its preparation activities include defining the ART, setting the launch date and PI calendar, training leaders and roles, forming teams, assessing readiness and preparing the ART backlog. The steps below turn them into a practical sequence.

  1. Choose the value stream. Pick one with a clear business problem, strong leadership support and people mostly in one organisation. Early wins matter more than a perfect first choice.
  2. Define the ART. Agree its mission, solution, boundary and rough size. A one-page canvas covering vision, customers, Business Owners and key roles is enough.
  3. Fill the train-level roles. Appoint the RTE, Product Management, System Architect and Business Owners first; they design the rest of the launch.
  4. Design and form the teams. Use the value stream map and team types. A skills matrix shows whether each team has the skills it needs.
  5. Set the launch date and PI calendar. Publish the dates of the first PI planning and the next three or four PIs, including iterations, System Demos and Inspect and Adapt.
  6. Train everyone. Leaders first, then each role, then the teams. Scaled Agile recommends training all teams together in one room rather than team by team.
  7. Prepare the ART backlog. Write and prioritise the top features and enablers for the first PI, with acceptance criteria and rough sizes.
  8. Check readiness. Roles filled and Business Owners committed, vision and backlog ready, room, tools and remote set-up in place.
  9. Run the first PI planning. Expect it to feel messy. The goal is a credible plan and shared understanding of dependencies, not perfection.
  10. Coach the first PI closely. Run ART Sync, System Demos and the first Inspect and Adapt with experienced coaches, and act on what you learn.
Tip

Launch one train well before launching a second. The first ART becomes the reference for everyone else, and its problems are much easier to fix while it is the only one.

Worked example: a 60-person onboarding ART

This example is illustrative: the organisation, teams and figures are invented. A mid-sized insurer wants to cut the time it takes a new customer to buy home insurance online. The work is spread across web, mobile, IT integration, testing and pricing departments, and every release needs four sign-offs.

Team design

The insurer maps the onboarding value stream and forms one train of seven teams plus train-level roles.

TeamTypePeopleOwns
Quote and ApplyStream-aligned9Online quote and application journey
Identity and VerificationStream-aligned8ID checks, fraud screening, customer records
Policy DocumentsStream-aligned8Policy schedule, emails and document portal
Onboarding MobileStream-aligned9Mobile app onboarding journey
Pricing EngineComplicated subsystem7Premium calculation and rating rules
Onboarding PlatformPlatform8Shared APIs, test data and environments
System TeamSupporting6Build pipeline, integration and end-to-end testing
Train-level rolesTrain5RTE, two product managers, System Architect, UX lead

That gives 60 people. Three Business Owners (the head of personal lines, the IT director and the operations director) are accountable for outcomes but are not counted as train members.

PI calendar and capacity

The train uses two-week iterations and a ten-week PI: four development iterations and one IP iteration, with dates published four PIs ahead. Each team estimates its capacity from recent velocity and planned leave, then plans committed work to roughly 80% of it, leaving room for support and surprises. (Planning to about 80% is our own rule of thumb, not a SAFe requirement.)

PI objectives and the predictability measure

At PI planning, the Quote and Apply team drafts four objectives, and the Business Owners score each from 1 to 10 for business value. At Inspect and Adapt they score what was actually achieved.

PI objectiveTypePlanned valueActual value
Launch the new online home quote to 10% of web trafficCommitted109 (reached 5% after a late pricing defect)
Cut the application form from 42 to 25 fieldsCommitted77
Use the new ID verification API (depends on Identity and Verification)Committed85 (live for new customers only)
Pilot save-and-resume for long applicationsUncommitted53 (staff pilot only)

Planned value counts committed objectives only: 10 + 7 + 8 = 25. Actual value includes anything achieved, uncommitted objectives too: 9 + 7 + 5 + 3 = 24. Predictability is 24 / 25 = 96%. SAFe guidance treats roughly 80% to 100% as predictable. If the seven teams range from 72% to 98% and average 87%, the train is predictable overall, but the lowest team's plan becomes a topic for the problem-solving workshop.

At Inspect and Adapt, the train traces the pricing defect to test data that did not match production rating rules. The improvement item for the next PI is an enabler for the Onboarding Platform team: refresh rating test data automatically every night. That is the cycle working as intended: plan, deliver, measure honestly, fix the system.

A Solution Train belongs to SAFe's Large Solution configuration, for very large systems that need several ARTs and suppliers to coordinate. For decisions above the train, see Agile portfolio management.

Measuring ART performance

SAFe 6.0 groups measures into three domains: outcomes (is the train delivering the intended business results?), flow (how efficiently does value move?) and competency (how well are Lean-Agile practices applied?). A new train should start with a handful of measures reviewed at every Inspect and Adapt.

MeasureWhat it tells youHow to use it
PredictabilityHow reliably the train meets its PI objectivesAim for a stable range rather than 100%
Flow velocityFeatures completed per PIWatch the trend, not single PIs
Flow timeTime from starting a feature to releasing itShorten it by cutting WIP and hand-offs
Flow distributionMix of features, enablers, defects and risk workProtect time for enablers and technical debt
Business outcomesChanges in customer or business metricsLink each feature's benefit hypothesis to a measure

Avoid comparing teams' velocities or ranking teams by predictability; both encourage gaming. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs explains how to calculate and present these measures.

Common Agile Release Train mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Organising the train by department or componentEvery feature crosses several teams, creating queuesRe-form around the value stream; make most teams stream-aligned
Overloading the PI planNo buffer; uncommitted objectives disappear; morale dropsPlan below full capacity and protect the IP iteration
Renaming the old programme officeSame reporting and approvals with new labelsGive teams real decision rights; the RTE coaches rather than controls
Business Owners who never attendTeams lose context; value scores become guessesMake PI planning and System Demos fixed diary commitments
Turning ART Sync into a status reportLong meetings with little coordinationFocus on dependencies, risks and decisions
Constantly reshuffling teamsVelocity and trust reset every PIKeep teams stable and bring work to them

If you see three or more of these, the train has probably adopted SAFe's events without changing how decisions, funding and teams are organised. Review the train's design before tuning its meetings.

Tools and templates for running an ART

You can run a first PI with a wall, sticky notes and spreadsheets. As the train grows, you will want a tool that links features to team stories, shows cross-team dependencies and rolls progress up into dashboards.

  • Jira is a common choice for software-heavy trains. Every plan has a timeline for epics, and Premium adds Plans for cross-team roadmaps and capacity.
  • Wrike, our top-rated tool, suits trains that mix software with marketing, operations or other business work, thanks to custom workflows, cross-project dashboards and portfolio views. Compare them in our Wrike vs Jira review.
  • Before a tool is configured, a product backlog template can hold the ART backlog for the first PI.

Criticisms of ARTs and lighter alternatives

SAFe has critics. Common concerns are that it is heavy and prescriptive, that it lets organisations keep top-down control under Agile labels, and that a synchronised cadence limits team self-organisation. Supporters answer that the ART gives large organisations a practical way to align many teams, and that most problems come from poor implementation.

  • Two or three teams on one product rarely need a train; a Scrum of Scrums and a shared backlog are often enough.
  • Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS) keeps one Product Owner and one Product Backlog for up to eight teams, with fewer added roles.
  • An ART tends to suit organisations with many teams, real integration complexity, governance needs and leaders who want a well-documented model.

Frequently asked questions

What is an Agile Release Train in simple terms?

A large, permanent team made up of several smaller Agile teams, usually 50 to 125 people in total, working on the same product or service. The teams plan together every 8 to 12 weeks, share one calendar and combine their work regularly into one working solution.

Why is it called an Agile Release Train?

Like a train timetable, the ART runs on fixed, predictable dates. Features that are ready catch the train; those that are not wait for the next one, and the timetable does not move for late work.

How many people and teams are in an Agile Release Train?

Scaled Agile says an ART is generally 50 to 125 people. With teams of ten or fewer, that is typically about 5 to 12 teams plus the train-level roles.

What does PI stand for in SAFe?

Since SAFe 6.0 (2023), PI stands for Planning Interval, a fixed timebox of usually 8 to 12 weeks. Older material and many tools still say Program Increment. The abbreviation, and the name PI planning, did not change.

What does a Release Train Engineer do?

The RTE is the servant leader and coach of the train. They facilitate PI planning, ART Sync, System Demos and Inspect and Adapt, manage risks and dependencies, escalate impediments and help the train improve its flow.

Is a Release Train Engineer the same as a project manager?

No. A project manager often owns scope, schedule and budget for a temporary project. An RTE facilitates a long-lived train, while Product Management owns scope and teams plan their own work. The role relies on coaching rather than direction.

What is the difference between an Agile Release Train and a Scrum team?

A Scrum team is one team of ten or fewer people planning one sprint at a time. An ART is several such teams sharing a backlog and planning a whole Planning Interval together. Each team on an ART can still use Scrum or Kanban internally.

What is ART Sync?

The regular coordination event during a PI. It combines the Coach Sync, where the RTE and Scrum Masters/Team Coaches review progress, dependencies and impediments, and the PO Sync, where Product Management and Product Owners review scope and priorities.

Does an Agile Release Train release only at the end of a PI?

No. SAFe's principle is develop on cadence, release on demand. Planning, demos and integration follow the PI cadence, but teams can release whenever a feature is ready and the business wants it.

How long does it take to launch an Agile Release Train?

It depends on the organisation. Before the first PI planning you need to identify the value stream, define the train, fill roles, train everyone and prepare the backlog. Expect the first two or three PIs to be a learning period.

Can an Agile Release Train be fully remote?

Yes. Many trains run PI planning, ART Sync and System Demos online with a shared digital planning board. Remote trains need more preparation, firm facilitation and schedules that respect time zones.

Do you need SAFe certification to work on an ART?

No certification is required to be a team member. Scaled Agile offers role-based certifications such as SAFe Release Train Engineer and SAFe Scrum Master, and organisations launching a train usually train people in their roles. See our guide to Agile certifications.
