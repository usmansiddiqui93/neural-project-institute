Agile Ceremonies: A Practical Guide to Agile Meetings

Agile ceremonies are the small set of recurring meetings an Agile team uses to plan its work, coordinate every day, review results with stakeholders and improve how it works. In Scrum they are Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective, held inside the Sprint, plus ongoing backlog refinement. This guide gives you the purpose, time-box, attendees and a ready-to-use agenda for each one, a worked two-week meeting calendar and the fixes for the most common problems.

Key takeaways

  • Agile ceremonies cover four needs: planning, daily coordination, inspecting the product and improving the process.
  • In Scrum, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Retrospective all sit inside the Sprint; refinement is an ongoing activity, not an event.
  • Scrum Guide time-boxes are maximums for a one-month sprint (8 hours, 15 minutes, 4 hours, 3 hours); shorter sprints usually need less.
  • In a typical two-week sprint, all ceremonies together take roughly 9 to 11 hours per person, about an eighth of the sprint.
  • Every ceremony should end with a concrete output: a Sprint Goal, an adjusted plan, a revised backlog or an improvement with an owner.
  • Kanban and scaled frameworks such as SAFe use different meetings, but the same principle applies: short, regular and purposeful.

What are Agile ceremonies?

Agile ceremonies are the recurring, time-boxed meetings an Agile team holds to plan work, coordinate daily, inspect what it has built with stakeholders and improve its way of working. Each ceremony has a single purpose, a fixed maximum length and a clear output, and together they replace most ad hoc status meetings.

"Ceremony" is an informal word. The Scrum Guide calls these meetings events, and many coaches prefer "events" or simply "meetings" because "ceremony" can suggest ritual for its own sake. You will see all three terms used for the same thing.

Whatever you call them, the ceremonies exist to serve four needs that every team has:

NeedQuestion it answersScrum ceremony
PlanWhat will we achieve in this sprint, and how?Sprint Planning
CoordinateAre we on track today, and what do we need to change?Daily Scrum (stand-up)
Inspect the productDid we build the right thing, and what should come next?Sprint Review
Improve the processHow can we work better as a team?Sprint Retrospective
Prepare upcoming workAre the next items clear, small and estimated?Backlog refinement (an ongoing activity)

If you are new to the wider approach, start with our guide to Agile project management.

This table summarises the core ceremonies. The one-month figures are the Scrum Guide maximums; the two-week figures are a common rule of thumb, not a Scrum rule.

Daily ScrumEvery working day, same time and place15 minutes15 minutesDevelopers (PO and SM if working on items)Adjusted plan for the next day

Scrum.org stresses that these are maximums, not minimums. If a meeting achieves its purpose in less time, finish early.

Agile ceremonies vs Scrum events

Most articles use "Agile ceremonies" and "Scrum events" interchangeably, but there are differences worth knowing.

  • Scrum has five events: the Sprint itself, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. The Sprint is the container for the other four. See Scrum ceremonies for the formal rules.
  • Backlog refinement is not a Scrum event. The Scrum Guide 2020 describes it as an ongoing activity. Most teams still book regular refinement sessions, so it is usually listed as a ceremony.
  • Other Agile methods use similar meetings with different names. Extreme Programming (XP) has iteration and release planning; many teams call the review a showcase or demo; Kanban uses cadences such as replenishment meetings.
  • Agile itself prescribes no meetings. The Agile Manifesto values individuals and interactions and regular reflection, but leaves the format to each team.

If your team runs sprints, use the Scrum set; if you work in continuous flow, the Kanban cadences later in this guide fit better.

Sprint Planning

Sprint Planning starts the sprint. The whole Scrum Team agrees why the sprint is valuable, what can be done and how the work will get done. The Scrum Guide time-box is up to eight hours for a one-month sprint.

Inputs to prepare

  • An ordered, refined Product Backlog, with the top items small enough to finish in a sprint.
  • The team's capacity for the sprint: holidays, part-time members, support duties. See Agile capacity planning.
  • Recent velocity or throughput as a sanity check, not a target.
  • The current Definition of Done and the Product Goal.

Sample agenda for a two-week sprint (2.5 hours)

TimeItemLed by
0:00–0:10Context: Product Goal, stakeholder feedback, capacity for this sprintProduct Owner, Scrum Master
0:10–0:35Topic 1, why: draft and agree the Sprint GoalProduct Owner with the team
0:35–1:20Topic 2, what: select backlog items that support the goal and fit capacityDevelopers
1:20–1:30Break
1:30–2:15Topic 3, how: break the first items into tasks of a day or less; spot dependencies and risksDevelopers
2:15–2:30Confidence check (for example, fist of five) and confirm the Sprint GoalScrum Master

Outputs and an example

The output is a Sprint Goal and a Sprint Backlog (the goal, the selected items and a plan for delivering them). A good Sprint Goal is one sentence that describes an outcome, for example: "Customers can reset their password without contacting support." Items that do not support the goal can still be selected, but the goal should be the reason the sprint is worth doing.

Daily stand-up (Daily Scrum)

The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for the Developers, held at the same time and place every working day. Its purpose is to inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan for the next day. According to the Scrum Guide, the Product Owner and Scrum Master take part as Developers if they are actively working on items in the Sprint Backlog.

Two common formats

FormatHow it worksBest for
Three questionsEach person says what they did yesterday, what they will do today and what is blocking themNew teams that need structure
Walk the boardGo through the board from right to left (closest to done first) and ask what it will take to finish each itemTeams that want to focus on flow and the Sprint Goal

The 2020 Scrum Guide no longer prescribes the three questions. Developers can choose any structure, as long as the meeting focuses on progress towards the Sprint Goal and produces an actionable plan for the next day.

Example: a walk-the-board stand-up

  1. 0:00 The facilitator shares the board and restates the Sprint Goal.
  2. 0:01 Items in Review: "Password reset email is waiting for review. Can someone pick it up this morning?" Priya agrees.
  3. 0:04 Items in Progress: one story has been in progress for four days; Tom and Ana agree to pair on it after the stand-up.
  4. 0:09 Blockers: the test environment is down. The Scrum Master takes the action to chase the platform team.
  5. 0:12 Check: "Are we still on track for the Sprint Goal?" The team agrees yes, with the pairing in place.
  6. 0:13 Close. Follow-up conversations happen straight after, with only the people involved.
Tip

Problems raised at the stand-up are solved after it. Use a "parking lot" for anything that needs more than a minute.

Backlog refinement

Backlog refinement (sometimes called grooming) is the work of breaking down, clarifying, ordering and estimating upcoming Product Backlog items so they are ready for Sprint Planning. The Scrum Guide 2020 treats it as an ongoing activity rather than an event and sets no time-box. Earlier editions of the Scrum Guide suggested refinement usually took no more than 10% of the Developers' capacity, and many teams still use that as a guide.

Sample refinement session (60 minutes)

  1. Review the top of the backlog (5 minutes). The Product Owner explains any changes in priority since the last session.
  2. Clarify two to four items (30 minutes). Discuss each user story, agree acceptance criteria and note open questions.
  3. Split large items (10 minutes). Break anything too big for one sprint into smaller, valuable slices.
  4. Estimate (10 minutes). Size the clarified items, for example with planning poker and story points.
  5. Confirm next steps (5 minutes). Who will answer the open questions before the next session?

Aim to keep about one to two sprints' worth of items ready at any time. Some teams agree a short "definition of ready" (clear acceptance criteria, estimated, small enough, no blocking dependencies), but use it as a guide rather than a gate.

Sprint Review

The Sprint Review happens at the end of the sprint. The Scrum Team presents the results of its work to key stakeholders, and together they discuss progress towards the Product Goal and decide what to do next. The Scrum Guide describes it as a working session and warns against limiting it to a presentation. The maximum time-box is four hours for a one-month sprint.

Sample agenda (90 minutes)

TimeItem
0:00–0:10Product Owner recaps the Sprint Goal and what was and was not done
0:10–0:40Developers show the working Increment; stakeholders try it themselves where possible
0:40–1:05Feedback and discussion: what did we learn, what has changed in the market or organisation?
1:05–1:25Look ahead: likely next items, release forecast, budget or timeline implications
1:25–1:30Product Owner confirms the changes to the Product Backlog

How to prepare

  • Show only work that meets the Definition of Done; do not demo half-finished items.
  • Invite the stakeholders who can give useful feedback or make decisions, not everyone.
  • Prepare realistic data and a short script, but leave most of the time for conversation.
  • Bring a release burnup or roadmap view so stakeholders can see the bigger picture.

The output is a revised Product Backlog.

Sprint Retrospective

The Sprint Retrospective closes the sprint. The Scrum Team inspects how the last sprint went with regard to individuals, interactions, processes, tools and its Definition of Done, and identifies the most helpful changes. The maximum time-box is three hours for a one-month sprint.

A five-stage agenda (75 minutes)

This structure is adapted from Esther Derby and Diana Larsen's book *Agile Retrospectives*:

  1. Set the stage (5 minutes). Restate the purpose and ground rules; a one-word check-in from everyone.
  2. Gather data (20 minutes). What happened? Use a timeline, sprint metrics and sticky notes.
  3. Generate insights (20 minutes). Group the notes and ask why the main themes happened.
  4. Decide what to do (20 minutes). Vote and choose one or two improvements, each with an owner and a check date.
  5. Close (10 minutes). Review the actions and ask for quick feedback on the retrospective itself.

Popular retrospective formats

FormatPromptsUse it when
Start, stop, continueWhat should we start, stop and keep doing?You want a quick, familiar structure
Mad, sad, gladWhat frustrated, disappointed or pleased you?Feelings and morale need attention
4LsLiked, learned, lacked, longed forYou want a balanced view after a big sprint
SailboatWind (what helps), anchors (what slows us), rocks (risks), island (goal)You want to link problems to a goal

Add the agreed improvements to the next Sprint Backlog so they actually happen, and check them at the next retrospective. For more ideas, see sprint retrospective games and the free sprint retrospective template.

How long should Agile ceremonies take?

The Scrum Guide sets maximums only for a one-month sprint. For shorter sprints, a common rule of thumb is to scale the time-boxes in proportion. The table below uses that rule; it is guidance, not part of Scrum.

Sprint lengthSprint PlanningDaily ScrumSprint ReviewRetrospective
1 weekUp to 2 hours15 minutesUp to 1 hourUp to 45 minutes
2 weeksUp to 4 hours15 minutesUp to 2 hoursUp to 1.5 hours
3 weeksUp to 6 hours15 minutesUp to 3 hoursUp to 2.25 hours
4 weeks / 1 monthUp to 8 hours (Scrum Guide)15 minutes (Scrum Guide)Up to 4 hours (Scrum Guide)Up to 3 hours (Scrum Guide)

Mature teams with a well-refined backlog often need well under the maximum: a two-week Sprint Planning of 90 minutes is common. If your meetings regularly hit the time-box, look at refinement first, because poorly prepared items are the main cause of long planning sessions.

The 3-5-3 rule

You may hear Scrum summarised as "3-5-3": three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), five events (the Sprint and the four ceremonies inside it) and three artefacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Increment). It is a memory aid, not a term from the Scrum Guide.

Worked example: a two-week sprint meeting calendar

Here is how the ceremonies fit into a real two-week sprint for a team of seven (five Developers, a Product Owner and a Scrum Master), working Monday to Friday with the sprint starting on a Wednesday. Starting mid-week avoids Monday holidays and Friday afternoon reviews.

Day 1 (Wed)Sprint Planning 9:30–12:002.5 hours
Day 2 (Thu)Daily Scrum 9:3015 minutes
Day 3 (Fri)Daily Scrum15 minutes
Day 4 (Mon)Daily Scrum; refinement 14:00–15:001 hour 15 minutes
Day 5 (Tue)Daily Scrum15 minutes
Day 6 (Wed)Daily Scrum15 minutes
Day 7 (Thu)Daily Scrum15 minutes
Day 8 (Fri)Daily Scrum; refinement 11:00–12:001 hour 15 minutes
Day 9 (Mon)Daily Scrum15 minutes
Day 10 (Tue)Daily Scrum; Sprint Review 13:30–15:00; Retrospective 15:30–16:453 hours
TotalAbout 9.5 hours

With 80 working hours per person in the sprint, the ceremonies take roughly 12% of the team's time. That can look like a lot until you compare it with what it replaces: weekly status meetings, separate planning workshops, stakeholder update calls and lessons-learned sessions at the end of a project. Many teams find their total meeting time goes down once the ceremonies are running well.

Tip

Put all ceremonies in the calendar for the next three months as recurring invitations. A stable rhythm saves hours of scheduling and makes it easier for stakeholders to attend the Review.

The Scrum Master is accountable for making sure the events take place and are positive, productive and kept within the time-box, but does not have to facilitate every one. Rotating facilitation, especially for the stand-up and retrospective, builds ownership and keeps meetings fresh.

Meetings beyond Scrum: Kanban and scaled Agile

Not every Agile team runs sprints, and large organisations need meetings that connect many teams. The principle is the same, but the meetings change.

Kanban cadences

The Kanban Method describes a set of regular meetings called cadences. Most teams start with two or three and add others as they grow.

CadenceTypical frequencyPurpose
Daily Kanban (stand-up)Daily, 15 minutesFocus on flow, blockers and ageing work on the board
ReplenishmentWeekly or as neededChoose which items enter the system next
Delivery planningPer releasePlan and coordinate delivery to customers
Service delivery reviewEvery two weeks or monthlyCheck whether the service meets customer expectations, using flow metrics
Operations, risk and strategy reviewsMonthly to quarterlyBalance capacity, risks and direction across services

Scaled Agile events

  • Scrum of Scrums: representatives from several Scrum teams meet regularly to coordinate dependencies.
  • PI Planning: in SAFe, all teams on an Agile Release Train plan the next Program Increment together, usually over two days. See PI Planning.
  • System Demo: a combined demonstration of the integrated work of all teams on the train.
  • Inspect and Adapt: a larger retrospective and problem-solving workshop at the end of each increment.
  • ART Sync: a regular coordination meeting for the train, combining coach and Product Owner syncs.

Optional Agile meetings

Some meetings are not part of any framework but are widely used. Add them only if they solve a real problem.

  • Planning poker sessions: short estimation workshops, usually folded into refinement.
  • Release planning: a periodic session to forecast what will be delivered by a date. See Agile release planning.
  • Roadmap review: a quarterly look at the Agile roadmap with senior stakeholders.
  • Lean Coffee: an agenda-less meeting in which participants propose and vote on topics.

Before adding any meeting, ask: which decision will this help us make, and could an existing ceremony cover it instead? For the techniques used inside the ceremonies, such as story mapping and burndown charts, read our guide to Agile tools and techniques.

How to set up your ceremony cadence step by step

  1. Choose a sprint length. Two weeks suits most teams. Shorter if priorities change fast; longer only if work genuinely cannot be finished in two weeks.
  2. Pick a start day. Mid-week starts avoid clashes with bank holidays and Friday absences.
  3. Book the recurring meetings for at least the next quarter: planning, daily stand-up, one or two refinement sessions, review and retrospective.
  4. Agree time-boxes using the scaled table above, and make them visible in the invitations.
  5. Prepare a lightweight agenda for each meeting; use our meeting agenda template as a starting point.
  6. Decide where outputs live: the Sprint Goal on the board, the backlog in your tool, retrospective actions in the Sprint Backlog, notes in a shared space.
  7. Agree facilitation: who facilitates each event, and whether it rotates.
  8. Review the cadence after three sprints in a retrospective. Shorten, merge or move meetings that are not earning their time.

Running Agile ceremonies remotely

Remote and hybrid teams can run every ceremony well, but they need more structure than co-located teams.

  • Use one shared digital board that everyone can see and update during the meeting.
  • Cameras on for planning, review and retrospective where people are comfortable; it helps with engagement and reading the room.
  • Rotate meeting times if the team spans time zones, so the same people are not always joining early or late.
  • Try an asynchronous stand-up on some days: written updates in a chat channel, with a live call only when there are blockers.
  • Record the Sprint Review for stakeholders who cannot attend, and collect their feedback in writing.
  • Use anonymous input in retrospectives (digital sticky notes or polls) to make it easier to raise difficult issues.

Most Agile tools support remote ceremonies with boards, backlog views and sprint reports. Our guides to Scrum software tools and the Jira review cover the options.

Frequently asked questions

What are the 5 Agile ceremonies?

Most lists give five: Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum (stand-up), backlog refinement, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. Strictly, Scrum's five events are the Sprint, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Retrospective, and refinement is an ongoing activity.

What are the four core Agile ceremonies?

Sprint Planning, the daily stand-up, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. They cover planning, daily coordination, inspecting the product and improving the process.

How long should each Agile ceremony last?

For a one-month sprint the Scrum Guide maximums are 8 hours for planning, 15 minutes for the Daily Scrum, 4 hours for the Review and 3 hours for the Retrospective. For a two-week sprint, many teams use up to 4, 2 and 1.5 hours respectively.

What is the 3-5-3 rule in Scrum?

A memory aid: three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), five events (the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Retrospective) and three artefacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment).

Who facilitates Agile ceremonies?

Often the Scrum Master, but anyone on the team can. The Scrum Master is accountable for making sure the events happen and stay within their time-box; many teams rotate facilitation.

Is backlog refinement a ceremony?

Not in the Scrum Guide, which calls it an ongoing activity. Most teams still schedule one or two refinement sessions per sprint, so it is commonly listed alongside the ceremonies.

What are the three questions in a stand-up?

Traditionally: what did I do yesterday, what will I do today, and is anything blocking me? The 2020 Scrum Guide no longer requires them; many teams walk the board and focus on the Sprint Goal instead.

What is the difference between the Sprint Review and the Retrospective?

The Review inspects the product with stakeholders and updates the Product Backlog. The Retrospective inspects how the team worked and agrees improvements. The Review comes first.

Can we skip the retrospective if the sprint went well?

It is better not to. In Scrum all events are required, and a good sprint is a chance to understand what worked and try a small improvement. Shorten it rather than cancel it.

Do Kanban teams have ceremonies?

Yes. The Kanban Method uses cadences such as the daily Kanban meeting, replenishment, delivery planning and the service delivery review. Many Kanban teams also hold retrospectives.

How much time do Agile ceremonies take per sprint?

In a typical two-week sprint, about 9 to 11 hours per person, or roughly 12% of working time. In return, they usually replace separate status, planning and review meetings.

Why are they called ceremonies?

The term became popular in early Scrum writing as an informal name for recurring meetings. The Scrum Guide uses "events", and many coaches prefer that word.

Should managers attend the daily stand-up?

They can observe occasionally, but the Daily Scrum belongs to the Developers. If a manager's presence turns it into a status report, share progress through the board and the Sprint Review instead.
