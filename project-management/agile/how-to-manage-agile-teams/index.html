How to Manage Agile Teams: A Practical Guide for Managers

To manage Agile teams well, you set clear goals and decision boundaries, build the team's capability and remove the obstacles it cannot remove alone, while the team plans and manages its own day-to-day work. This guide explains what changes for managers, how to split responsibilities with the Scrum Master and Product Owner, how to delegate using clear levels and how to measure performance fairly. It also gives you a step-by-step process, a weekly rhythm, illustrative worked examples and the mistakes to avoid.

Key takeaways

  • Managing an Agile team means shaping the system the team works in (goals, boundaries, people, environment), not assigning tasks or checking hours.
  • Agree in writing who decides what: the team owns how the work is done, the Product Owner owns what is built and in which order, and the manager owns people, budget and organisational obstacles.
  • Use explicit delegation levels, from tell to delegate, so the team knows exactly how much authority it has for each type of decision.
  • Measure team outcomes and trends (value, flow, quality, predictability, health) and never compare velocity between teams.
  • Protect focus: route new requests through the Product Owner, keep teams stable and avoid splitting people across many teams.
  • Your own role keeps changing as the team matures; review it every quarter and delegate more as trust grows.

What does it mean to manage an Agile team?

Managing an Agile team means creating the conditions for a self-managing team to deliver value: a clear purpose, agreed decision boundaries, the right people and skills, and an environment free of avoidable obstacles. The team decides how to do its work; the manager looks after people, direction and the wider organisation.

In a traditional project team, the manager breaks down the work, assigns tasks, chases progress and solves problems. In an Agile team, most of that moves inside the team. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes Scrum Teams as self-managing, meaning they decide internally who does what, when and how. Kanban teams work in a similar way, pulling work when they have capacity.

That does not make managers unnecessary. Someone still has to hire and develop people, set budgets, agree priorities with senior leaders, fix cross-team problems and create a culture where people can raise bad news early. The difference is where your attention goes: from the work itself to the system that produces the work. If you are new to Agile teams, start with our guide to what an Agile team is.

What changes for managers

The table shows the most important shifts. You do not have to make them all at once, but you should know where you are heading.

From (traditional)To (Agile)What it looks like in practice
Assigning tasksSetting clear goals and contextYou explain the outcome and constraints; the team plans the sprint
Checking progressMaking progress visible and trusting the teamYou read the board and attend the Sprint Review instead of asking for status reports
Solving problems for the teamHelping the team solve problemsYou ask questions and remove organisational blockers, not technical ones

Many managers find the hardest change is emotional rather than procedural. Your value used to come from knowing the answer; now it comes from building a team that finds better answers than you would. Our guide to the Agile mindset explores this shift.

Who does what: manager, Scrum Master and Product Owner

Confusion about roles causes more friction than any other issue in Agile teams. Agree this split with your Scrum Master and Product Owner and write it down, for example in a RACI matrix.

AreaTeam (Developers)Product OwnerScrum MasterLine manager
What to build and in which orderAdvisesDecidesCoaches the processShares strategy and constraints
How to build it and who does which taskDecidesInformedCoachesStays out
Sprint Goal and sprint scopeCommits with the Product OwnerProposesFacilitatesInformed
Process and ways of workingDecides in retrospectivesContributesCoaches and facilitatesSupports changes beyond the team
Hiring and team membershipInterviews and recommendsContributesContributesDecides
Career development and payPeer feedbackFeedbackFeedbackDecides
Budget and headcountInformedConsultedInformedDecides or secures

The Scrum Master is accountable for the team's effectiveness and coaches the organisation on Scrum. The Product Owner is accountable for maximising the value of the product. The line manager is not a Scrum accountability at all: you sit outside the Scrum Team and support it. See Scrum team roles for the full detail.

When the manager is also the team lead

In smaller organisations, one person may line-manage the team and also work in it. That can work, but be careful: when the person who writes your appraisal is in the room, people speak less openly. If you hold both positions, make your role explicit in each meeting ("I am speaking as a Developer now"), let others facilitate retrospectives and keep performance conversations strictly in one-to-ones.

Managing several Agile teams

If you manage people across several teams, your job is mostly at the system level: keeping teams stable, resolving dependencies, aligning priorities with other managers and growing people across teams. Organise your reports by team where possible so you are not constantly switching context, and rely on each team's Scrum Master for the day-to-day picture. Coordination patterns such as the Scrum of Scrums help when teams share a product.

How to manage an Agile team: step by step

Use these ten steps when you take on an Agile team, or to reset an existing one. Steps one to five usually take the first few weeks; steps six to ten become your ongoing routine.

  1. Clarify purpose and outcomes. Agree with senior stakeholders and the Product Owner what the team exists to achieve this year, expressed as outcomes such as a Product Goal or a few OKRs, not a list of features.
  2. Agree roles and responsibilities. Use the table above as a starting point. Confirm who the Product Owner and Scrum Master are and what you will and will not decide.
  3. Set decision boundaries. Run a delegation exercise (see the next section) for the 10 to 15 decisions that cause most friction, and publish the result.
  4. Check team design. Make sure the team is stable, dedicated and cross-functional, with typically ten or fewer people. Use a skills matrix to find gaps and single points of failure. Our guide to Agile team structures explains the options.
  5. Make work visible. Agree one board and one backlog the team uses every day, so you can see progress without asking for status reports.
  6. Set up your rhythm. Book regular one-to-ones, attend Sprint Reviews as a stakeholder and hold a regular slot with the Scrum Master to review impediments.
  7. Protect focus. Route all new requests to the Product Owner, keep people on one team and push back on mid-sprint changes that do not support the Sprint Goal.
  8. Remove organisational obstacles. Keep an impediment list of problems the team cannot solve alone, such as access, approvals, environments or dependencies on other departments, and work through it.
  9. Measure and learn. Review a small, balanced set of measures each month with the team, and use them to ask questions, not to rank people.
  10. Grow people and step back. Invest in training and pairing, widen delegation as trust grows and review your own role every quarter.
Tip

Tell the team explicitly what you are changing and why. A manager who silently stops assigning tasks is often read as disengaged rather than empowering.

Set decision boundaries with delegation levels

"Self-managing" does not mean the team decides everything. It means the boundaries are clear and the team has full authority inside them. A simple way to agree those boundaries is the seven levels of delegation described by Jurgen Appelo in Management 3.0 (often played as a card game called delegation poker).

LevelNameWhat it means
1TellThe manager decides and explains the decision
2SellThe manager decides and tries to win the team's support
3ConsultThe manager asks for input, then decides
4AgreeThe manager and team reach consensus together
5AdviseThe team decides; the manager offers an opinion first
6InquireThe team decides, then explains its decision to the manager
7DelegateThe team decides and the manager does not need to be told

How to run a delegation session

  1. List 10 to 15 recurring decisions that cause friction or delay.
  2. For each one, ask everyone to choose a level privately, then reveal at the same time.
  3. Discuss the highest and lowest answers until you agree a level.
  4. Record the result on a delegation board visible to everyone.
  5. Revisit the board every quarter and move decisions to higher levels as trust and capability grow.

Worked example: a delegation board (illustrative)

A manager of a seven-person product team runs the session in a 60-minute workshop. The agreed board looks like this:

DecisionAgreed levelWhy
Choice of tools and libraries within the approved list7 DelegateTeam has the expertise and bears the consequences
Sprint length and event times6 InquireTeam decides; manager wants to know for planning with other teams
Working hours and core collaboration time5 AdviseTeam decides within company policy
Hiring a new team member4 AgreeTeam interviews; team and manager must both say yes
Spending over the team's training budget3 ConsultManager is accountable for the budget
Moving a person to another team3 ConsultAffects other teams; manager decides after hearing the team
Company-wide security standards1 TellSet outside the team; not negotiable

The value is less in the board itself than in the conversation. In the example, the team assumed it could choose any tool, while the manager assumed new tools needed approval. Agreeing "within the approved list" removed a recurring source of tension.

A weekly rhythm for managers of Agile teams

A predictable rhythm stops you drifting back into status-chasing. This example assumes two-week sprints and a manager with six to eight direct reports on one or two teams.

ActivityCadenceTimePurpose
One-to-ones with each reportEvery week or two30 minutes eachGrowth, wellbeing, feedback, career
Impediment review with the Scrum MasterWeekly30 minutesClear organisational blockers the team has raised
Product Owner check-inWeekly30 minutesPriorities, stakeholder pressure, upcoming demand
Sprint ReviewEvery sprint60 minutesSee the increment and give feedback as a stakeholder
Read the board and metricsTwice a week15 minutesSpot trends without interrupting the team
Peer managers syncWeekly30 to 45 minutesDependencies, shared people, cross-team problems
Team health and measures reviewMonthly45 minutesDiscuss trends with the team and agree experiments
Delegation and role reviewQuarterly60 minutesWiden delegation; adjust your own role

Notice what is missing: daily status meetings with you, and attendance at the Daily Scrum. The Daily Scrum is the Developers' planning meeting. Attend only if invited, and if you do, listen rather than ask for updates. Use a consistent meeting agenda template for your one-to-ones and check-ins so they stay focused.

Protecting focus and handling stakeholder requests

Teams lose more time to interruptions and context switching than to slow work. Protecting focus is one of the most valuable things you can do, and it often means saying "not now" to people more senior than you.

  • One front door. All new requests go to the Product Owner, who decides where they sit in the backlog.
  • Stable sprints. Changes during a sprint should support the Sprint Goal. If something urgent truly cannot wait, the Product Owner makes the trade-off openly.
  • One team per person. Splitting people across three teams means each team gets less than a third of them, once switching costs are counted.
  • Limits on work in progress. Fewer items in progress means faster delivery of each one. See our guide to WIP limits.
  • A buffer for the unplanned. Teams that support live products often reserve part of their capacity for urgent fixes. Our Agile capacity planning guide shows how to size it.

Worked example: a mid-sprint request (illustrative)

On day four of a ten-day sprint, a sales director asks you to have the team build a custom report for a prospect by Friday. The team's Sprint Goal is to release a new onboarding flow.

  1. Do not accept the work yourself. Thank the director and explain that requests go to the Product Owner, who owns priorities.
  2. Bring the Product Owner in quickly. Help the director explain the value: the size of the deal, the deadline and what happens if it slips.
  3. Let the Product Owner make the trade-off. In this case the report is about 3 days of effort. Adding it would put the Sprint Goal at risk, so the Product Owner offers two options: a simpler export the team can produce in half a day this sprint, or the full report as the top item next sprint.
  4. Back the decision publicly. The director accepts the export. You confirm the outcome to both sides and note the pattern: if sales requests keep arriving mid-sprint, the Product Owner may agree a small regular slot for them.

Your role was to protect the process and support the Product Owner, not to decide the priority. Our guide to stakeholder management covers how to prepare for these conversations.

Measuring Agile team performance

Measure the team, not individuals, and use a balanced set so that improving one number does not damage another. Review trends over several sprints rather than reacting to a single data point.

DimensionExample measuresQuestion it answers
Outcomes and valueProgress against OKRs or the Product Goal; customer feedback; usage of released featuresAre we building the right things?
FlowCycle time, throughput, work item age, flow efficiencyHow smoothly does work move?
PredictabilitySprint Goals met; forecast vs actual over several sprintsCan stakeholders rely on our forecasts?
QualityEscaped defects, incidents, rework, technical debt trendAre we building things right?
Team healthRegular health checks or short surveys; retention; sustainable working hoursCan we keep this up?
Tip

Never compare velocity between teams or use it as a performance target. Story points are relative to each team, so targets simply inflate the estimates. See our guide to Scrum velocity.

Worked example: reading the signals (illustrative)

Over four sprints, a team's throughput rises from 14 to 19 items per sprint, which looks positive. But escaped defects rise from 2 to 7 per sprint and the health check shows people working late before every review. Instead of praising the speed, the manager brings the three trends to the team's monthly review. The team decides to strengthen its Definition of Done with automated tests and to cap work in progress. Two sprints later throughput settles at 16, defects fall to 3 and late working stops. The balanced view prevented a short-term gain from hiding a long-term cost.

Feedback, one-to-ones and performance reviews

Team-based work does not remove the need for individual conversations. It changes what they are about. Team-level feedback belongs in the Sprint Retrospective; individual feedback belongs in private one-to-ones.

A simple one-to-one agenda

  1. Their agenda first. What is on their mind? What would make their work easier?
  2. Wellbeing and workload. Are they working at a sustainable pace?
  3. Feedback both ways. One specific piece of feedback for them, and ask for one for you.
  4. Growth. Progress on their development goals, such as a new skill or wider responsibility.
  5. Actions. Agree who will do what before the next meeting.

Fair performance reviews in a team system

  • Assess contribution to team outcomes, collaboration, skill growth and how the person helps others, not individual output counts such as tickets closed.
  • Collect peer feedback from the team, the Product Owner and the Scrum Master, ideally against a few agreed behaviours.
  • Avoid forced ranking within a team; it rewards competition over collaboration.
  • Keep development goals visible in a simple training plan so reviews are not a surprise.

Handling underperformance

Teams usually notice performance problems before managers do, and good teams often address them directly. When they cannot, act early and privately: describe specific behaviour and its effect, ask about causes (unclear expectations, skills gaps, personal circumstances or a poor fit with the role), agree clear expectations and support, and set a date to review. Do not use team events or metrics to single people out. If the problem continues, follow your organisation's formal process with HR support.

Building capability and motivation

Self-managing teams need the skills and confidence to manage themselves. Building them is a core part of your job, not an optional extra.

  • Map skills and spread them. A skills matrix shows who can do what. Use pairing, rotation and short internal sessions to remove single points of failure.
  • Grow T-shaped people. Encourage deep expertise in one area and working skills in neighbouring ones, so the team can swarm on bottlenecks. See Agile swarming.
  • Give time to improve. Protect time each sprint for learning, tooling and process improvement, not only feature work.
  • Explain the why. Share business goals, customer feedback and financial context so the team can make good trade-offs.

Daniel Pink's book Drive argues that knowledge workers are motivated by autonomy, mastery and purpose. Agile practices map neatly onto those three: delegation provides autonomy, investment in skills builds mastery and clear outcomes give purpose. Recognition matters too: celebrate learning and useful failed experiments, not only successful releases.

Google's Project Aristotle research on its own teams found that psychological safety, the belief that you can take interpersonal risks without being punished, was the most important of the team dynamics it studied. As a manager, you set the tone: admit your own mistakes, thank people for raising problems and never punish the messenger. Handling conflict in Agile teams

Agile teams work closely and decide things together, so disagreement is normal. Healthy conflict about ideas (task conflict) improves results. Personal conflict (relationship conflict) damages trust and should be addressed early.

  1. Agree how decisions are made in advance. For example: the person doing the work decides small technical choices; the team decides bigger ones by consent; the Product Owner breaks ties on priority.
  2. Let the team try first. Many disagreements resolve in a well-run retrospective. The Scrum Master facilitates; you stay out unless asked.
  3. Talk privately when it becomes personal. Meet each person separately, listen, then bring them together to agree specific behaviours.
  4. Use a neutral facilitator for deeper conflict. The Scrum Master, an Agile coach or HR can help structure a conversation.
  5. Follow up. Check in after a sprint or two to see whether the agreed behaviours have stuck.

Conflict models such as Thomas-Kilmann describe five common styles: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding and accommodating. None is always right; the useful skill is noticing which style you and others default to and choosing deliberately. A working agreement agreed by the team also prevents many conflicts by making expectations explicit.

Managing remote and hybrid Agile teams

Remote and hybrid Agile teams can work very well, but informal communication does not happen by itself. Design it in.

  • Default to written and visible. Keep decisions, plans and progress on shared boards and documents so nobody depends on overheard conversations.
  • Agree core collaboration hours. A short daily window when everyone is available makes the Daily Scrum, pairing and quick questions easy.
  • Use video for key events. Planning, reviews and retrospectives work better on camera, with digital whiteboards.
  • Rotate inconvenient meeting times fairly when the team spans time zones.
  • Make connection deliberate. Short social calls, in-person meet-ups for planning or kick-offs, and pairing all build trust.
  • Watch for invisible overwork. Remote people often work longer without anyone noticing. Ask about workload in one-to-ones.

Worked example: a new manager's first 90 days

This illustrative example follows Priya, newly appointed to manage eight people across two Scrum teams in a mid-sized insurance company. Her predecessor assigned tasks daily and asked for a weekly status report.

PeriodWhat Priya doesResult
Days 1 to 30: listenHolds a one-to-one with every report, meets both Scrum Masters and Product Owners, reads past retrospective notes, attends Sprint ReviewsFinds three recurring problems: two people split across both teams, mid-sprint requests from operations, unclear approval rules for tools
Days 31 to 60: clarifyAgrees a responsibilities table with the Scrum Masters and Product Owners, runs a delegation session with each team, moves the two shared people to one team each, sets up an intake route for operations requests via the Product OwnersWeekly status report replaced by the boards and Sprint Reviews; fewer mid-sprint changes
Days 61 to 90: improveIntroduces a monthly measures and health review, builds a skills matrix with each team, starts a pairing rotation to cover a single point of failure in testingTeams propose their own improvement experiments; Priya's time shifts from coordination to growth and cross-team issues

The lesson is sequence: listen before changing, clarify roles and boundaries before chasing metrics, and remove structural problems (shared people, uncontrolled intake) before asking the team to improve its process.

Attitudes and mistakes that sink Agile teams

Each of these attitudes undermines trust or quality and, over time, turns Agile into a set of rituals without results.

Mistake or attitudeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
"We'll do Agile, but scope and deadline are both fixed."Removes the team's only levers, so quality is cut silentlyFix the date or the scope, and let the other flex; agree a minimum viable scope
"Just tell me who is behind."Encourages blame and hides problemsAsk what is blocking the team and how you can help
"Retrospectives are a waste of time."Stops the team improvingProtect the retrospective and act on what it raises; try a retrospective template to refresh the format
"The team can decide, as long as it decides what I want."Destroys trust in delegationAgree delegation levels honestly and respect them
"We don't have time to fix technical debt."Slows every future sprintReserve regular capacity for quality work
"Documentation isn't Agile."Creates knowledge gaps and riskDocument what is useful to readers, just enough and kept current
Comparing velocity between teamsInflates estimates and damages collaborationCompare each team with its own past trends
Reshuffling teams for every projectResets teams to the start of their developmentKeep teams stable and bring work to them
Attending the Daily Scrum to collect statusTurns it into a report to the managerRead the board instead; attend only if invited

Tools that help you manage Agile teams

Tools do not make a team Agile, but the right one makes work visible and saves you from chasing status. Look for boards and backlogs for the team, plus portfolio views and reporting for managers.

  • Wrike, our top-rated platform, suits managers who look after several teams or need to connect Agile work with wider portfolio planning: it offers Kanban boards, Gantt charts, workload views, request forms and cross-project dashboards.
  • Jira is the long-standing choice for software teams, with mature Scrum and Kanban boards, configurable workflows and sprint reports.
  • monday.com is quick for mixed business teams to adopt, with flexible boards, automations and dashboards.
  • Asana works well for cross-functional teams that want clear goals linked to everyday tasks.

Start with the simplest setup the team will actually use, and let the team own its board. Read our guide to the Agile project manager role if you also coordinate budgets and dependencies across teams.

Frequently asked questions

Do Agile teams need managers?

They need leadership, but not task assignment. Managers remain important for hiring, people development, budgets, setting direction and removing organisational obstacles that the team cannot remove alone.

What does a manager do in an Agile team?

A manager sets goals and context, agrees decision boundaries, keeps the team stable and properly skilled, protects its focus, removes organisational obstacles and supports each person's growth through one-to-ones and feedback.

What is the difference between a manager and a Scrum Master?

The Scrum Master is accountable for the team's effectiveness and coaches the team and organisation on Scrum. A line manager looks after people, careers, budgets and the wider organisation. The Scrum Master usually has no authority over pay or appraisals.

Can a manager be the Scrum Master?

It is possible but risky. When the person who appraises you also facilitates retrospectives, people tend to speak less openly. If it cannot be avoided, separate the roles clearly and let someone else facilitate some events.

How do you measure an Agile team's performance?

Use a balanced set of team measures covering outcomes, flow, predictability, quality and team health, and review trends over several sprints. Avoid individual output counts and never compare velocity between teams.

Should managers attend Scrum events?

Managers usually attend Sprint Reviews as stakeholders. They should not attend the Daily Scrum or Sprint Retrospective unless invited, because their presence can reduce openness.

How do you delegate to an Agile team?

List the decisions that matter, agree a delegation level for each (from tell to delegate), publish the result on a delegation board and widen it as trust grows.

How do you handle an underperforming team member?

Act early and privately. Describe specific behaviour and its effect, explore causes, agree clear expectations and support, and review progress on a set date. Never use team events or metrics to single people out.

How do you handle conflict in an Agile team?

Agree how decisions are made in advance, let the team try to resolve task conflict in retrospectives, address personal conflict privately and early, and use a neutral facilitator when needed.

How do you manage remote Agile teams?

Default to written updates and shared boards, agree a short core collaboration window, use video for key events, rotate meeting times fairly and make informal connection deliberate.

How do you motivate an Agile team?

Explain why the work matters, give the team real autonomy within clear boundaries, invest in its skills and celebrate learning as well as results.

How many Agile teams can one manager look after?

There is no fixed rule. It depends on the number of direct reports and how mature the teams are. Many organisations aim for a span of roughly six to ten direct reports, ideally organised by team to reduce context switching.

What mistakes do managers make with Agile teams?

Fixing scope and deadline together, interrupting sprints, comparing velocity between teams, attending stand-ups to collect status, reshuffling teams often and treating retrospectives as optional.
