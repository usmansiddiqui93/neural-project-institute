Scrum Team Roles: Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers

The three Scrum team roles are the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the Developers, and the 2020 Scrum Guide calls them accountabilities rather than roles. Together they form one small, self-managing Scrum Team with no sub-teams or hierarchy. This guide explains what each accountability involves, who decides what, how big a team should be and how to set one up, with worked examples, comparison tables and the mistakes to avoid.

Key takeaways

  • A Scrum Team has one Product Owner, one Scrum Master and Developers: typically 10 or fewer people, with no sub-teams or hierarchies.
  • The Product Owner is accountable for maximising the value of the product; the Scrum Master for establishing Scrum and the team's effectiveness; the Developers for creating a usable Increment each Sprint.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide replaced the Development Team with Developers and speaks of accountabilities, not job titles.
  • The team is self-managing: nobody inside it assigns the Developers' daily work, and the whole team is accountable for a valuable Increment every Sprint.
  • When a team grows too large, split it into several Scrum Teams that share one Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product Owner.
  • Most role problems come from blurred accountabilities: a Product Owner without authority, a Scrum Master acting as task-master, or Developers split into silos.

What are the Scrum team roles?

Scrum team roles are the three accountabilities within a Scrum Team: the Product Owner, who maximises the value of the product; the Scrum Master, who establishes Scrum and makes the team effective; and the Developers, who create a usable Increment every Sprint. The 2020 Scrum Guide calls them accountabilities because they describe responsibilities, not job titles.

People often remember the framework as 3-5-3: three accountabilities, five events (the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective) and three artifacts.

What the Scrum Guide says about the Scrum Team

The Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland is the official definition of Scrum. The November 2020 edition is still the current official version (checked 26 October 2026); community add-ons such as the Scrum Guide Expansion Pack are not official revisions. The table summarises what it says about the team and what that means in practice.

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means for your team
The Scrum Team consists of one Scrum Master, one Product Owner and DevelopersExactly one person in each of the first two accountabilities; as many Developers as the work needs, within the size limit
There are no sub-teams or hierarchiesNo separate "dev team" and "test team", and no team lead who outranks the others
It is a cohesive unit focused on one objective at a time, the Product GoalThe team works towards one shared outcome rather than a list of unrelated projects
Cross-functional: members have all the skills needed to create value each SprintThe team should not need to hand work to another department to finish it
Self-managing: they internally decide who does what, when and howManagers set direction and boundaries; the team organises the work
Small enough to remain nimble, large enough to complete significant work, typically 10 or fewer peopleCount everyone, including the Product Owner and Scrum Master
Responsible for all product-related activities, from stakeholder collaboration to maintenance, operation, experimentation and researchSupport, operations and discovery work belong to the team too, not just new features
The entire Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every SprintSuccess and failure are shared; there is no "we did our part"

Roles or accountabilities? What changed in 2020

Older guides, many training courses and most top search results still describe a "Development Team" and a "servant leader" Scrum Master. That wording comes from the 2017 Scrum Guide. The 2020 edition changed several things about the team:

Topic2017 Scrum Guide2020 Scrum Guide
Name of the group doing the workDevelopment Team (a team within the Scrum Team)Developers, one accountability within a single Scrum Team
Team descriptionSelf-organisingSelf-managing: decides who does what, when and how
Size guidanceDevelopment Team of 3 to 9 peopleScrum Team typically 10 or fewer people in total
Scrum MasterDescribed as a servant leaderAccountable for the team's effectiveness; "true leaders who serve" the team and the organisation
LanguageRolesAccountabilities
Product focusNo Product GoalProduct Goal added as the commitment for the Product Backlog

Why does the wording matter? A role sounds like a job title in an organisation chart. An accountability is a set of outcomes someone answers for. A senior engineer, a tester and a technical writer can all hold the Developer accountability; a business analyst might become a Product Owner without changing job title. The 2020 wording also removes the idea of a team within a team, which used to encourage a "them and us" split between the Product Owner and everyone else.

The Product Owner

The Product Owner is accountable for maximising the value of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's work. How they do that varies widely between organisations, teams and individuals, but according to the Scrum Guide they are also accountable for effective Product Backlog management, which includes:

  • Developing and explicitly communicating the Product Goal.
  • Creating and clearly communicating Product Backlog items.
  • Ordering Product Backlog items.
  • Ensuring that the Product Backlog is transparent, visible and understood.

The Product Owner may do this work themselves or delegate it, but remains accountable. They are one person, not a committee, though they may represent the needs of many stakeholders. The Guide adds that for Product Owners to succeed, the entire organisation must respect their decisions, and that people who want to change the backlog do so by trying to convince the Product Owner.

What a Product Owner does in practice

  • Talks to customers, users and stakeholders to understand needs and measure outcomes.
  • Keeps the Product Backlog ordered and the top items clear, often during backlog refinement with the Developers.
  • Proposes how the product could increase its value in Sprint Planning and helps shape the Sprint Goal.
  • Leads the conversation about what to do next at the Sprint Review and adjusts the backlog afterwards.
  • Says no, or not yet, to requests that do not serve the Product Goal.

The Scrum Master

The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide, by helping everyone understand Scrum theory and practice, both within the Scrum Team and the organisation. They are also accountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. The 2020 Guide describes Scrum Masters as true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organisation.

The Guide lists three groups the Scrum Master serves:

ServesExamples from the Scrum Guide
The Scrum TeamCoaching members in self-management and cross-functionality; helping them focus on high-value Increments that meet the Definition of Done; causing the removal of impediments; ensuring all Scrum events take place and are positive, productive and kept within the timebox
The Product OwnerHelping find techniques for Product Goal definition and Product Backlog management; helping the team understand the need for clear, concise backlog items; helping establish empirical product planning; facilitating stakeholder collaboration
The organisationLeading, training and coaching Scrum adoption; planning and advising Scrum implementations; helping people understand an empirical approach; removing barriers between stakeholders and Scrum Teams

A Scrum Master is not a project manager, a team lead or the person who assigns work. Their influence comes from coaching, facilitation and making problems visible. For a full description of the role, a typical day and a step-by-step approach to removing impediments, read our guide to the Scrum Master role.

The Developers

In Scrum, Developers are the people in the Scrum Team who are committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint. The word is not limited to software developers: designers, testers, analysts, data specialists, writers, engineers and marketers can all be Developers. The Scrum Guide notes that the specific skills needed are often broad and vary with the domain of work.

According to the Guide, the Developers are always accountable for:

  1. Creating a plan for the Sprint, the Sprint Backlog.
  2. Instilling quality by adhering to a Definition of Done.
  3. Adapting their plan each day towards the Sprint Goal.
  4. Holding each other accountable as professionals.

What this looks like day to day

  • Sizing Product Backlog items and helping split them during refinement.
  • Selecting, with the Product Owner, how much work to take into a Sprint, based on their capacity and past performance.
  • Running the Daily Scrum, which is the Developers' event, and updating the Sprint Backlog as they learn.
  • Collaborating with the Product Owner to renegotiate scope within the Sprint, without endangering the Sprint Goal, when the work turns out differently from expected.

Cross-functional does not mean everyone does everything

The Scrum Guide requires the team as a whole to have the skills it needs. It does not require every person to do every kind of work. In our experience the most resilient teams mix deep specialists with people who can help outside their speciality (often called T-shaped skills), so that one absence does not stop the Sprint. A skills matrix template is a quick way to see where you are thin.

Who decides what? Decision rights in a Scrum Team

Most friction in Scrum Teams comes from unclear decision rights. The table shows who makes common decisions. Where the Scrum Guide is explicit, it says so; where it is silent, we give the usual practice.

DecisionWho decidesSource

The Product GoalProduct Owner develops and communicates itScrum Guide
The Sprint GoalThe whole Scrum Team, during Sprint PlanningScrum Guide
Which items are selected for the SprintDevelopers, through discussion with the Product OwnerScrum Guide

If you already use a RACI matrix, you can map these decisions into it. Keep Scrum's single points of accountability intact: one Product Owner for the order, the Developers for the plan.

Scrum team size and structure

The Scrum Guide says a team should be small enough to remain nimble and large enough to complete significant work within a Sprint, typically 10 or fewer people, adding that smaller teams generally communicate better and are more productive. The number includes the Product Owner and Scrum Master.

If a Scrum Team becomes too large, the Guide says it should consider reorganising into multiple cohesive Scrum Teams, each focused on the same product. Those teams share the same Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product Owner.

Worked example: splitting a 14-person team

An online travel company has one 14-person team working on its booking app: a Product Owner, a Scrum Master, five back-end engineers, three front-end engineers, two testers, a designer and a data analyst. Sprint Planning takes most of a day and the Daily Scrum regularly overruns.

  1. Keep one product view. The Product Owner keeps a single Product Backlog and Product Goal ("Travellers can change their own bookings without calling support").
  2. Split by customer value, not by layer. Rather than a front-end team and a back-end team, they form two cross-functional teams: Team Search and Book, and Team Manage My Trip.
  3. Spread scarce skills. Each team gets back-end, front-end and testing skills, ending up with eight people each including the shared Product Owner and Scrum Master. The designer and analyst join one team each and pair across teams when needed.
  4. Decide on the Scrum Master. The existing Scrum Master serves both teams for now; the organisation plans to add a second Scrum Master within two quarters.
  5. Share one Definition of Done. Because both teams work on the same product, they must agree and follow the same Definition of Done.
  6. Coordinate lightly. Teams hold a joint Sprint Review and a short cross-team sync twice a week. Our guide to the Scrum of Scrums explains this pattern.

For larger products, see Scrum at scale.

How to set up a Scrum Team, step by step

Use this process when you form a new team or reset one that has drifted. The steps are our recommended sequence; the rules they rely on come from the Scrum Guide.

  1. Define the product. Agree what the team will own: a product, service or clearly bounded part of one. Without a product, the Product Owner has nothing to maximise.
  2. Appoint one Product Owner with real authority. Choose someone who understands customers and can make ordering decisions that the organisation will respect. Make their mandate explicit to stakeholders.
  3. Assemble cross-functional Developers. List the skills needed to turn backlog items into a Done Increment (for example design, build, test, deploy, content) and make sure the team has them, keeping the total around 10 people or fewer.
  4. Appoint a Scrum Master. Prefer someone with coaching and facilitation skills who is not the line manager of the Developers.
  5. Agree a working agreement. Core hours, communication channels, how to raise problems and how decisions are made. Keep it to one page.
  6. Create or adopt the Definition of Done. Start from any organisational standard, then add what the product needs.
  7. Set the Product Goal and seed the backlog. The Product Owner drafts the goal and the first ordered items; the Developers help refine and size the top of the list.
  8. Run the first Sprint and inspect. Hold Sprint Planning, then use the first Sprint Retrospective to fix whatever was unclear about who does what.

A sprint planning template helps you capture capacity, the Sprint Goal and the selected items for that first Sprint.

Worked example: forming a Scrum Team for a customer app

A regional energy supplier wants customers to submit meter readings and manage payments in an app instead of phoning the call centre. The head of digital forms a Scrum Team. The names are illustrative.

PersonJob titleScrum accountabilityWhy
PriyaSenior product managerProduct OwnerKnows the customers, owns the budget for the app and can make ordering decisions
TomDelivery managerScrum MasterExperienced facilitator; not the line manager of any team member
Ana and KofiMobile engineersDevelopersBuild the iOS and Android app
LenaBack-end engineerDeveloperIntegrates with billing and meter systems
SamTesterDeveloperBuilds automated tests and checks accessibility
JoUX designerDeveloperResearches with customers and designs flows
RaviContent designerDeveloperWrites in-app text and help content

Sprint 1. The Product Goal is "Customers can submit meter readings and see their balance in the app." In Sprint Planning, Priya explains why meter readings come first: they generate the most calls. The Developers select three items and agree the Sprint Goal "A customer can log in and submit a meter reading." Tom facilitates and keeps the session within its timebox.

A role problem appears. Midway through the Sprint, the call-centre manager asks Ana directly to add a reading-history screen. Ana starts on it. At the Daily Scrum the Developers notice the Sprint Goal is at risk. Tom coaches the team to redirect the request to Priya, who adds it to the Product Backlog and explains to the call-centre manager how ordering works.

Sprint 1 Retrospective. The team agrees that all requests go through the Product Owner, and that Priya will join refinement every Tuesday. Sam raises that testing piles up at the end of the Sprint; the Developers agree to pair on tests from day one.

Roles outside the Scrum Team

Scrum defines only three accountabilities, but plenty of other people affect a Scrum Team's work. The Guide refers to them mostly as stakeholders. This table shows how common roles usually fit; it reflects common practice rather than Scrum rules.

RolePart of the Scrum Team?How they usually work with it
Stakeholders (customers, users, sponsors, other departments)NoGive feedback at the Sprint Review, collaborate with the Product Owner and influence the backlog through them; see stakeholder management
Line or functional managersNoHire, develop and support people; set boundaries and remove organisational impediments; do not assign Sprint work
Project managerNo accountability in ScrumOften becomes Product Owner or Scrum Master, or works across teams on budgets, contracts, dependencies and reporting; see the Agile project manager
Architects and specialists (security, data, legal)Only if they work on the Increment as DevelopersJoin refinement or pair with Developers; some organisations embed them part-time
Executives and sponsorsNoSet strategy and funding; respect the Product Owner's decisions; attend Sprint Reviews where useful
Tip

Scrum has no project manager accountability, but it does not ban project managers. The question to ask is which accountability, if any, the person holds inside the team, and how their other work supports it.

Can one person hold more than one Scrum role?

The Scrum Guide does not explicitly forbid combining accountabilities, apart from the requirement for one Product Owner and one Scrum Master per team. Whether it is wise is a different question. The advice column is our view.

CombinationAllowed by the Scrum Guide?Our advice
Scrum Master and DeveloperNot forbidden. If they work on Sprint Backlog items, they take part in the Daily Scrum as a Developer.Workable in small or mature teams; watch for coaching being squeezed out by delivery pressure
Product Owner and DeveloperNot forbidden; same Daily Scrum rule appliesPossible for very small products; risky if the Product Owner stops talking to stakeholders
Product Owner and Scrum MasterNot explicitly forbiddenAvoid. The two accountabilities provide a check on each other; one person doing both tends to drop coaching in favour of delivery
Line manager as Scrum MasterNot forbiddenAvoid where possible; people are less open about problems with the person who writes their appraisal

Common Scrum team role mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
A proxy Product Owner with no authorityEvery decision waits for someone outside the team; the backlog order keeps changingGive the Product Owner a real mandate, or make the real decision-maker the Product Owner
A Product Owner committeeConflicting priorities and no single orderOne Product Owner who represents many stakeholders
The Scrum Master as task-master or project managerThe team stops self-managing and waits to be told what to doDevelopers plan and assign their own work; the Scrum Master coaches
Sub-teams inside the team ("dev" and "test")Hand-offs, queues and late defectsOne group of Developers sharing the Definition of Done
Teams that are too largeLong events, side conversations and diluted ownershipSplit into cross-functional teams sharing one Product Backlog
Stakeholders bypassing the Product OwnerHidden work and missed Sprint GoalsRoute requests through the Product Owner and make the backlog visible

Many of these problems are easier to spot at the Sprint Retrospective if the team regularly asks, "Is it clear who decides what?"

Frequently asked questions

What are the three roles in Scrum?

The Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the Developers. The 2020 Scrum Guide calls them accountabilities rather than roles, to make clear they are responsibilities within one team rather than job titles.

Who is the leader of a Scrum Team?

Scrum has no single team leader. The Product Owner leads on product direction, the Scrum Master leads on how Scrum is used and the team's effectiveness, and the Developers lead their own work. The Scrum Guide says there are no hierarchies within the team.

How many people should be in a Scrum Team?

Typically 10 or fewer, including the Product Owner and the Scrum Master. The Scrum Guide notes that smaller teams generally communicate better and are more productive.

What is the difference between a Product Owner and a Scrum Master?

The Product Owner is accountable for the value of the product and owns the Product Backlog. The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum and the team's effectiveness. One focuses on what to build and why; the other on how the team works.

Can the Product Owner and Scrum Master be the same person?

The Scrum Guide does not explicitly forbid it, but it is widely discouraged. The two accountabilities pull in different directions, and combining them removes a useful check on each.

Can a Scrum Master also be a Developer?

Yes. The Scrum Guide does not prevent it. If the Scrum Master works on Sprint Backlog items, they take part in the Daily Scrum as a Developer. Make sure coaching does not disappear under delivery pressure.

Is there a project manager in Scrum?

Scrum does not define a project manager accountability. Its work is shared between the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the self-managing Developers. Project managers often take one of these accountabilities or work across teams on budgets, contracts and dependencies.

Who manages the Developers in Scrum?

Nobody inside the Scrum Team manages their daily work. The Developers are self-managing and decide who does what, when and how. Line managers outside the team still support people's development and careers.

Is the Development Team still a Scrum role?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide removed the Development Team. There is now one Scrum Team made up of the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the Developers.

Are testers, designers and analysts Developers in Scrum?

Yes. Anyone committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment is a Developer, whatever their job title or speciality.

Are stakeholders part of the Scrum Team?

No. Customers, users, managers and sponsors are stakeholders outside the team. They collaborate with it, mainly through the Product Owner and at the Sprint Review.

What happens when a Scrum Team becomes too large?

The Scrum Guide suggests reorganising into multiple cohesive Scrum Teams focused on the same product, sharing one Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product Owner. They must also share a Definition of Done.

Does every Scrum Team need a full-time Scrum Master?

The Scrum Guide requires a Scrum Master but does not say full-time. Many experienced Scrum Masters serve two or three teams; new teams usually benefit from more of their time.
