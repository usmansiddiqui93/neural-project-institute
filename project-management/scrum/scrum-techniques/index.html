Scrum Techniques: 20 Practices, Examples and How to Use Them

Scrum techniques are the practices teams add to the Scrum framework to carry out the work it defines, such as user stories, planning poker, burndown charts, swarming and the Five Whys. The 2020 Scrum Guide is purposefully incomplete: it sets out the events, artifacts and accountabilities, and leaves the techniques to you. This guide explains 20 widely used Scrum techniques, which problem each one solves, how to use it step by step and how to choose the right ones for your team.

Key takeaways

  • Scrum defines events, artifacts, commitments and accountabilities. Techniques such as user stories, story points and burndown charts are optional complementary practices.
  • Good techniques solve a specific problem. Start from the problem your team has, not from a list of popular practices.
  • Backlog techniques (user stories, acceptance criteria, INVEST, splitting, story mapping) make Product Backlog items clear and small enough to finish in a Sprint.
  • Estimation and tracking techniques (planning poker, capacity planning, burndowns, burn-ups) support forecasting, but the Scrum Guide says they do not replace empiricism.
  • Delivery and improvement techniques (swarming, WIP limits, pairing, test-driven development, Five Whys) help teams finish work and get better each Sprint.
  • Introduce one technique at a time as an experiment, inspect it in the Sprint Retrospective, and keep it only if it helps.

What are Scrum techniques?

Scrum techniques are optional practices that a Scrum Team uses inside the Scrum framework to carry out the work Scrum describes. Examples include user stories for Product Backlog items, planning poker for sizing, burndown charts for tracking progress and the Five Whys for solving problems. The Scrum Guide does not require any of them.

The 2020 Scrum Guide describes the framework as purposefully incomplete, defining only the parts needed to implement Scrum theory, and says that various processes, techniques and methods can be employed within it. That is deliberate. Scrum tells you that the Developers must size their work, for example, but not how. One team may use story points and planning poker; another may simply split items until they are all roughly the same size.

This flexibility is a strength, but it causes confusion. Many people believe user stories, velocity or a Definition of Ready are part of Scrum. They are not. Knowing the difference helps you keep the parts of Scrum that make it work and change the techniques freely when they stop helping. For the framework itself, start with our guide to what Scrum is.

Product Backlog techniques

These techniques help the Product Owner and Developers turn ideas into clear, ordered items that can be finished within a Sprint. Most of the work happens during backlog refinement.

User stories and the Three Cs

A user story describes a need from the point of view of the person who has it, usually in the form: As a [type of user], I want [goal] so that [benefit]. Ron Jeffries proposed in 2001 that a story has three parts, the Three Cs: the card (a short written reminder), the conversation (the discussion that fills in the detail) and the confirmation (the tests that show it works). The card is a placeholder for a conversation, not a specification. Our guide to user stories covers writing them in depth, and our free user story template gives you a ready-made format.

Acceptance criteria and Given-When-Then

Acceptance criteria are the conditions a specific item must meet to be accepted. Many teams write them in the Given-When-Then format, which comes from behaviour-driven development: Given a starting situation, When something happens, Then this result should follow. For example: Given a member has two active reservations, When they reserve a third book, Then they see a message that the limit is two. Acceptance criteria are specific to one item; the Definition of Done applies to every item.

The INVEST checklist

INVEST, attributed to Bill Wake, is a checklist for good backlog items: Independent (can be built in any order), Negotiable (details are open to discussion), Valuable (delivers something a user or customer cares about), Estimable (the team can size it), Small (fits comfortably in a Sprint) and Testable (you can tell when it works). Use it as a quick quality check during refinement, not as a scoring system.

Story splitting patterns

Large items should be split vertically, so each slice still delivers something usable from end to end, rather than horizontally into layers such as database, interface and testing. The worked example below is illustrative.

Original item: As a library member, I want to reserve books online so that I can collect them when they are ready.

Splitting patternResulting smaller item
Workflow stepMember can reserve an available book; notification and collection come later
Business ruleEnforce the limit of two active reservations
Simple then complexReserve from one branch first; choosing a collection branch comes later
Data variationReserve printed books first; audiobooks and DVDs as separate items
InterfaceReserve on the website first; the mobile app as a separate item
Happy path then exceptionsSuccessful reservation first; handling of books lost or withdrawn later

Each split item is still valuable on its own and can be ordered separately. See epics vs stories vs tasks for how splitting fits the wider backlog hierarchy.

User story mapping

Story mapping, described by Jeff Patton in his book User Story Mapping (2014), lays out the user's journey as a row of activities from left to right, with stories for each activity stacked beneath in priority order. Drawing a horizontal line across the map shows the smallest set of stories that delivers a complete journey, which is often a good first release. It helps teams avoid building one feature in depth while leaving the rest of the journey unusable.

Spikes

A spike is a timeboxed piece of research or experimentation to reduce uncertainty, for example testing whether a payment provider's interface supports refunds. The output is knowledge, not a feature: enough understanding to split or size the real item. Keep spikes short and give them a clear question to answer.

Estimation and Sprint Planning techniques

Scrum says the Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing it. These techniques help them do it quickly and consistently.

Story points and planning poker

Story points are a relative measure of the effort, complexity and uncertainty of an item compared with others. Planning poker is a technique for agreeing them. It was named by James Grenning in 2002 and popularised by Mike Cohn. Each Developer has cards with values from a scale such as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20.

  1. The Product Owner reads the item and answers questions about it.
  2. Each Developer privately chooses a card that reflects their estimate.
  3. Everyone reveals their card at the same time, which avoids anchoring on the first voice.
  4. The people with the highest and lowest estimates explain their reasoning.
  5. The team discusses briefly, then votes again.
  6. Repeat until the estimates converge, or agree on the higher value and move on. If an item is very large, split it instead.

Our guide to story points in Scrum covers scales, reference stories and common pitfalls.

T-shirt sizing and affinity estimation

When you have dozens of items to size, planning poker is slow. T-shirt sizing (XS, S, M, L, XL) gives quick rough sizes for roadmap and release discussions. Affinity estimation goes further: the team silently places item cards on a wall from smallest to largest, then discusses only the items that people keep moving. Both are useful early on, before items are refined in detail.

Capacity planning

Capacity planning checks how much time the Developers actually have in the coming Sprint, allowing for holidays, training, support duties and meetings, before they select work. A team that usually completes around 30 points but has a quarter of its time away might plan for closer to 22. See our guide to Agile capacity planning for a full method.

Definition of Ready

A Definition of Ready is an optional checklist an item should meet before the team selects it, for example: acceptance criteria written, dependencies known, sized by the Developers, small enough for one Sprint. It is not part of the Scrum Guide. Used lightly, it improves Sprint Planning. Used as a strict gate, it can recreate hand-offs and delay valuable work, so treat it as a guide for refinement rather than a rule. Our free Sprint Planning template includes a capacity calculation and a space for the Sprint Goal.

Tracking and transparency techniques

Scrum requires transparency and regular inspection, but not any particular chart. The 2020 Scrum Guide mentions burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flows as practices for forecasting progress and notes that, while proven useful, they do not replace the importance of empiricism. In other words, charts support decisions; they do not make them.

Scrum boards

A Scrum board shows the Sprint Backlog as columns such as To do, In progress and Done. It makes blockers and unfinished work visible at a glance and gives the Daily Scrum a shared focus.

Burndown and burn-up charts

A Sprint burndown chart plots the work remaining each day against an ideal straight line to zero. A burn-up chart plots work completed against total scope, which makes scope changes visible, so it is often better for release forecasts. Here is an illustrative burndown for a ten-day Sprint with 40 points of selected work. Points only count as burned when an item meets the Definition of Done.

End of dayIdeal remainingActual remainingWhat it suggests
04040Sprint starts
23237Many items started, few finished
42433Too much work in progress; team agrees to swarm
61622Items now finishing
8813Recovering, but behind
1003One item not Done; it returns to the Product Backlog

The flat start is a classic sign of too much work in progress rather than slow work. That points to techniques such as swarming and WIP limits rather than longer hours.

Velocity

Velocity is the amount of work, often in story points, that a team completes per Sprint. Averaged over several Sprints, it helps with Sprint Planning and release forecasts. It is a planning aid for one team, not a performance target, and it cannot be compared between teams. See our guide to Scrum velocity.

Delivery and quality techniques

These techniques help the Developers finish work to the Definition of Done and produce a usable Increment every Sprint.

Pair and mob programming

In pair programming, two people work on one task at one computer: one types while the other reviews and thinks ahead, and they swap regularly. In mob programming, also called ensemble programming, the whole team works on the same task together, rotating who types. Both spread knowledge, catch defects early and reduce hand-offs. They work for non-coding tasks too, such as writing a policy or designing a campaign together.

Test-driven development and continuous integration

Test-driven development (TDD) means writing a small automated test first, writing just enough code to make it pass, then improving the code while keeping tests green. Continuous integration (CI) means merging everyone's work into a shared codebase frequently, with automated builds and tests on every change. Together they make a demanding Definition of Done achievable within a Sprint, because problems surface within minutes rather than at the end.

Swarming

Swarming means several Developers working together on the highest-priority item to finish it, instead of each starting a separate item. It shortens the time each item spends in progress and reduces the risk of ending the Sprint with many items almost finished and none Done.

WIP limits within the Sprint

A work-in-progress limit caps the number of items allowed in a column of the Scrum board at once, for example no more than three items In progress for a team of six. When the limit is reached, people help finish existing work before starting anything new. WIP limits come from Kanban but fit naturally inside a Sprint.

Improvement and facilitation techniques

The Sprint Retrospective is where the team plans how to increase quality and effectiveness. These techniques help it find the real causes of problems and turn them into action.

Retrospective formats

Changing the format keeps Retrospectives fresh and helps quieter people contribute. Common formats include Start, Stop, Continue; Mad, Sad, Glad; the Sailboat; and the 4Ls (Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for). Our guide to Sprint Retrospective games explains when to use each, and the Sprint Retrospective template gives you a ready-made board.

Five Whys

The Five Whys technique, which comes from the Toyota Production System, asks why a problem happened, then asks why again of each answer until you reach a cause you can act on. Five is a guideline, not a rule. An illustrative example:

  1. Problem: two items failed the Definition of Done on the last day of the Sprint. Why? Regression tests failed.
  2. Why were failures found so late? The full regression suite only runs at the end of the Sprint.
  3. Why only then? It takes four hours and is run by hand by one tester.
  4. Why is it run by hand? It was never added to the continuous integration pipeline.
  5. Why not? The automation work was never visible on the Product Backlog, so it was never ordered against features.

Action: the Developers explain the cost to the Product Owner, who adds an item to automate the regression suite near the top of the Product Backlog. The 2020 Scrum Guide notes that the most impactful improvements may even be added to the Sprint Backlog for the next Sprint. When a problem probably has several causes rather than one chain, a fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, which groups causes under headings such as people, process and tools, works better. A free root cause analysis template helps you record either.

Working agreements

Working agreements are a short list of rules the team writes for itself, such as core collaboration hours, how to raise a blocker, or that nobody starts new work while a review request is waiting. Review them every few Sprints and change them when they stop reflecting how the team wants to work.

Walking the board at the Daily Scrum

Instead of each person reporting in turn, the team walks the Scrum board from right to left, starting with the items closest to Done, and asks what it will take to finish each one. It keeps the Daily Scrum focused on the Sprint Goal rather than on individual status updates.

How to choose and introduce a Scrum technique, step by step

Teams that adopt many techniques at once often end up with more process and no clear improvement. Treat each technique as an experiment.

  1. Name the problem. Write it in one sentence, with evidence. For example: in the last three Sprints, 30 to 40 per cent of selected items were not Done.
  2. Choose one technique that targets it. Use the at-a-glance table above. For unfinished work, WIP limits or swarming are good candidates.
  3. State a hypothesis. We believe that limiting work in progress to three items will mean at most one item is left unfinished per Sprint.
  4. Agree how you will measure it. Pick one simple measure you already have, such as items not Done or the shape of the burndown.
  5. Run it for two or three Sprints. One Sprint is rarely enough to judge a new habit.
  6. Inspect it in the Retrospective. Did the measure move? What did people notice? Was there a cost?
  7. Keep, adapt or drop it. Write down the decision, so the team does not reopen it every Sprint without new evidence.
Tip

If no one can name the problem a technique solves, it is probably ceremony. Drop it and see whether anyone misses it.

Worked example: choosing techniques for a struggling team

This example is illustrative. The team, numbers and outcomes are invented to show the process.

Context. A team of six works in two-week Sprints on an internal finance application. In the Retrospective, the team lists three problems: items regularly spill into the next Sprint, the Daily Scrum has become a 25-minute status report to the Product Owner, and the same complaint about late testing appears in every Retrospective.

ProblemEvidenceTechnique chosenMeasure
Items spill overFour of eleven items not Done in each of the last three SprintsWIP limit of three items, plus swarming on the top itemItems not Done per Sprint
Daily Scrum is a status reportAverages 25 minutes; Developers address the Product OwnerWalk the board, right to left, focused on the Sprint GoalDuration; team rating out of 5
Late testing keeps recurringMentioned in five consecutive RetrospectivesFive Whys, then one improvement item ordered in the Product BacklogDefects found after the Sprint

After three Sprints. Items not Done fall from four to one per Sprint. The Daily Scrum settles at about 12 minutes and the team rates it 4 out of 5, up from 2. The Five Whys exercise leads to automating the slowest manual tests; defects found after the Sprint fall, but the team agrees it needs more Sprints to be sure. The team keeps all three techniques, adds them to its working agreements and decides that its next experiment will be pairing on complex items.

Notice what the team did not do: it did not introduce story mapping, a Definition of Ready and a new estimation scale at the same time. Each change answered a problem the team could measure.

Common mistakes with Scrum techniques

  • Treating techniques as Scrum rules. Insisting that every item must be a user story or that velocity must rise each Sprint confuses the framework with optional practices.
  • Adopting too many at once. The team cannot tell which change helped, and the process becomes heavier than the work.
  • Using velocity as a performance target. Teams inflate estimates, and the number stops being useful for forecasting.
  • Comparing story points between teams. Points are relative to each team and mean nothing across teams.
  • Turning the Definition of Ready into a gate. Valuable work waits for paperwork, and refinement becomes a hand-off.
  • Splitting horizontally. Items split into layers deliver nothing usable until the last layer is finished.
  • Running the same retrospective format forever. Conversations become predictable and people disengage.
  • Keeping techniques nobody can justify. Review your practices regularly and remove those that no longer solve a problem.

Frequently asked questions

Are Scrum techniques part of the Scrum framework?

No. Scrum defines the accountabilities, events, artifacts and commitments. The 2020 Scrum Guide says the framework is purposefully incomplete and that various processes, techniques and methods can be employed within it. Techniques are the optional practices you choose.

Is planning poker part of Scrum?

No. Scrum only says that the Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing it. Planning poker is one popular way to do that, alongside T-shirt sizing, affinity estimation and simply splitting items to a similar size.

Are user stories required in Scrum?

No. Scrum requires Product Backlog items, not a particular format. User stories are a popular way to write them because they keep the focus on who needs something and why.

What is the INVEST checklist?

A checklist for good backlog items, attributed to Bill Wake: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small and Testable. Teams use it during refinement to check that items are ready to be discussed in Sprint Planning.

What is a Definition of Ready?

An optional checklist that a backlog item should meet before the team selects it, such as having acceptance criteria and a size. It is not in the Scrum Guide and works best as a guide rather than a strict gate.

What is the difference between acceptance criteria and the Definition of Done?

Acceptance criteria are specific to one item and describe what it must do. The Definition of Done is a quality standard that applies to every item in the Increment, such as being tested, reviewed and documented.

What is a spike in Scrum?

A timeboxed piece of research or prototyping that reduces uncertainty, so the team can split or size a backlog item. Its output is knowledge rather than a usable feature, and it should answer a specific question.

What is swarming in Scrum?

Several Developers working together on the highest-priority item to finish it quickly, instead of each starting a separate item. It reduces work in progress and the risk of ending the Sprint with many items almost done.

What is the difference between pair and mob programming?

Pair programming is two people working on one task at one computer. Mob, or ensemble, programming involves the whole team working on the same task together, rotating who types. Both spread knowledge and reduce hand-offs.

Is a burndown chart required in Scrum?

No. The Scrum Guide mentions burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flows as useful forecasting practices but says they do not replace the importance of empiricism. Many teams use one, but it is optional.

What techniques does a Scrum Master use?

Mostly facilitation and coaching techniques: retrospective formats, Five Whys and fishbone diagrams, working agreements, and coaching the team on practices such as WIP limits, swarming and walking the board at the Daily Scrum.

How many techniques should a new Scrum team adopt?

As few as possible at first. Get the Scrum events and artifacts working, then add one technique at a time to solve a problem you can name, and review it in the Retrospective after two or three Sprints.

Can non-software teams use Scrum techniques?

Yes. User stories, acceptance criteria, splitting, swarming, pairing, Scrum boards and Five Whys all work for marketing, HR, operations and other teams. Only a few, such as test-driven development, are specific to software.
