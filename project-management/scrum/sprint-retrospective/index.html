Sprint Retrospective: Agenda, Steps, Questions and Example

A Sprint Retrospective is the Scrum event in which the Scrum Team inspects how the last Sprint went and plans ways to increase quality and effectiveness. It concludes the Sprint, it is timeboxed to a maximum of three hours for a one-month Sprint, and its result is a small number of improvements the team acts on straight away. This guide covers what the 2020 Scrum Guide requires, a timed agenda built on the five stages of a retrospective, questions to ask, an illustrative worked example and the mistakes that make retros feel pointless.

Key takeaways

  • The purpose of the Sprint Retrospective is to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness. It is about how the team works, not about the product.
  • The whole Scrum Team attends: Developers, Product Owner and Scrum Master. Other people join only if the team invites them.
  • The maximum is three hours for a one-month Sprint. Many teams with two-week Sprints need 60 to 90 minutes.
  • The five stages (set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, close) give every retro a reliable shape.
  • Aim for one to three specific actions with an owner and a way to check the result. The most impactful can go straight into the next Sprint Backlog.
  • Start each retro by checking last Sprint's actions. Retros feel repetitive when actions are never finished.

What is a Sprint Retrospective?

A Sprint Retrospective is the last event of a Scrum Sprint, in which the Scrum Team reviews how it worked during that Sprint (its people, interactions, processes, tools and Definition of Done), agrees what to keep and what to change, and commits to a few concrete improvements for the next Sprint.

It follows the Sprint Review and comes just before the next Sprint Planning. It is one of the five Scrum events, alongside the Sprint itself, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum and the Sprint Review.

The Sprint Review asks whether the team is building the right product. The Retrospective asks whether the team is building it in the right way. Both rest on empiricism: inspect what really happened, then adapt. Teams can improve at any time, but the Retrospective gives that work a protected slot every Sprint so delivery pressure does not crowd it out.

What the Scrum Guide 2020 says about the Sprint Retrospective

The 2020 Scrum Guide describes the Sprint Retrospective in one short paragraph. In summary:

  • Purpose: plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness.
  • What is inspected: how the last Sprint went with regard to individuals, interactions, processes, tools and the team's Definition of Done.
  • What is discussed: what went well, what problems arose and how those problems were, or were not, solved.
  • Assumptions: the team identifies the assumptions that led it astray and explores where they came from.
  • Outcome: the team identifies the most helpful changes to improve its effectiveness. The most impactful are addressed as soon as possible and may even be added to the Sprint Backlog for the next Sprint.
  • Timing: it concludes the Sprint and is timeboxed to a maximum of three hours for a one-month Sprint, usually shorter for shorter Sprints.

The guide also makes the Scrum Master accountable for ensuring that all Scrum events take place and are positive, productive and kept within the timebox. It does not say who must facilitate, which format to use or how many actions to choose. Those are team decisions.

What changed from the 2017 Scrum Guide

  • The 2017 guide said the Sprint Backlog must include at least one high-priority process improvement from the previous Retrospective. The 2020 guide softened this: the most impactful improvements may be added to the Sprint Backlog.
  • The 2020 guide names the Definition of Done as something to inspect and adds the idea of examining assumptions that led the team astray.
  • The 2017 guide described the Scrum Master as participating as a peer team member. The 2020 version is shorter and less prescriptive throughout.
Tip

Scrum does not prescribe a format such as Start, Stop, Continue. Any technique that helps the team inspect and adapt honestly is valid, which is why it pays to vary formats.

Inputs and outputs of the Sprint Retrospective

Knowing what to bring and what should come out keeps the session focused. If nothing in the right-hand column exists at the end, the team had a conversation, not a Retrospective.

InputsOutputs
Actions agreed at the previous Retrospective and their statusOne to three improvement actions, each with an owner and a check
The Sprint Goal and whether it was metImprovement items added to the next Sprint Backlog where they need capacity
Observations from the Sprint ReviewUpdates to working agreements or the Definition of Done
Delivery data: burndown, velocity, cycle time, defects, interruptionsImpediments to escalate beyond the team, with a named person to raise them
Team members' experiences and feelingsA shared understanding of what to keep doing

Who attends the Sprint Retrospective and who facilitates

The Sprint Retrospective is an event for the Scrum Team: one Scrum Master, one Product Owner and the Developers. See our guide to Scrum team roles for how the accountabilities fit together.

AttendeeTypical role in the Retrospective
DevelopersShare observations, analyse causes, propose and own most actions
Product OwnerTakes part as a full team member; backlog clarity and stakeholder access are often the topics that matter most
Scrum MasterMakes sure the event happens and is positive, productive and timeboxed; often facilitates; also contributes views on how the team works
Guest facilitator (optional)Another team's Scrum Master or a coach, useful when the Scrum Master wants to participate fully
Managers and stakeholdersNot attendees by default; join only if the team invites them for a specific topic

Some articles say the Product Owner attends only sometimes. That is not Scrum: the Product Owner is part of the Scrum Team, and excluding them tends to split the team into 'us' and 'them'. Rotating the facilitator among team members is a good habit: it spreads skills, brings fresh formats and lets the Scrum Master take part as a participant.

How long should a Sprint Retrospective be?

The only rule is the maximum: three hours for a one-month Sprint, usually shorter for shorter Sprints. The typical durations below are common practice, not Scrum Guide rules. If the team keeps running out of time to decide on actions, the retro is too short.

Sprint lengthScrum Guide maximumTypical in practice
One weekShorter than three hours30 to 45 minutes
Two weeksShorter than three hours60 to 90 minutes
Three weeksShorter than three hours90 minutes to 2 hours
One monthThree hours2 to 3 hours

Hold it at the same time and place each Sprint where you can, usually on the last day after the Sprint Review. For more on Sprint lengths, see what a Sprint is.

The five stages of a retrospective

The most widely used structure comes from Esther Derby and Diana Larsen's book *Agile Retrospectives: Making Good Teams Great* (2006; a second edition with David Horowitz followed in 2024). Each stage has a job, and skipping one usually causes a predictable problem.

StagePurposeExample activitiesShare of time
1. Set the stageGet everyone talking early, state the focus and set a safe toneOne-word check-in, read the prime directive, review last Sprint's actionsAbout 10%
2. Gather dataBuild a shared picture of what actually happened, facts and feelingsSprint timeline, metrics, silent writing on cards, Mad/Sad/GladAbout 25%
3. Generate insightsUnderstand why things happened and spot patternsGroup cards into themes, dot vote, five whys, fishbone diagramAbout 30%
4. Decide what to doChoose a few experiments the team will actually runImpact/effort grid, draft actions, agree owners and checksAbout 25%
5. CloseConfirm commitments, appreciate each other and improve the retro itselfRead back actions, appreciations, quick feedback voteAbout 10%

The commonest failure is skipping stage three. Teams list problems and jump straight to fixes, so they treat symptoms. A few minutes asking 'why did that happen?' usually produces better actions than another round of sticky notes.

Sprint Retrospective agenda

This 60-minute agenda suits a two-week Sprint and maps to the five stages. Scale each slot proportionally for other Sprint lengths.

TimeStageWhat happensOutput
0:00–0:05Set the stageWelcome, state the focus, one-word check-in from everyoneEveryone has spoken once
0:05–0:10Set the stageReview last Retrospective's actions: done, not done, did it help?Keep, adapt or drop each one
0:10–0:25Gather dataShow key Sprint data, five minutes of silent writing, then read out cardsA wall of observations
0:25–0:40Generate insightsGroup into themes, dot vote, five whys on the top one or two themesRoot causes
0:40–0:52Decide what to doBrainstorm options, choose one to three actions, agree owners and checksAction list
0:52–1:00CloseRead back actions, decide which go into the Sprint Backlog, appreciations, feedback on the retroCommitments confirmed

How to prepare for a Sprint Retrospective

Preparation takes the facilitator 20 to 30 minutes and makes a large difference. The day before:

  1. Collect last Sprint's actions and check their status with the owners.
  2. Pull two or three pieces of data: whether the Sprint Goal was met, the burndown shape, unplanned work, cycle time or escaped defects. Our guide to Scrum metrics and KPIs explains which are useful.
  3. Note anything from the Sprint Review that points to a process issue rather than a product issue.
  4. Choose a focus and a format. If the last three retros used the same format, change it.
  5. Prepare the space: a private room with wall space, or an online whiteboard with columns set up and the prime directive at the top.
  6. Decide where actions will live, ideally the same tool as the Sprint Backlog.

How to run a Sprint Retrospective, step by step

Follow these steps with the agenda above. They work in person, remotely and in hybrid rooms.

  1. Open and set the tone. State the purpose and timebox, and read the prime directive if the team uses it.
  2. Check in. Ask everyone for one word or a number from 1 to 5 that sums up the Sprint. People who speak early are more likely to speak later.
  3. Review previous actions. Did we do it, and did it help? Keep, adapt or drop each one before raising anything new.
  4. Show the data. Facts anchor the conversation and reduce arguments about what happened.
  5. Write silently, then share. One idea per card, written before anyone speaks, so the loudest voices do not set the agenda.
  6. Group and vote. Cluster cards into named themes and give everyone three votes.
  7. Dig into causes. For the top theme, ask 'why?' until you reach something the team can influence.
  8. Choose actions. Brainstorm options, then pick one to three that are specific, small and owned by a named person.
  9. Agree how you will check whether each action helped, and when.
  10. Close. Read back the actions, add those needing capacity to the Sprint Backlog, offer appreciations and ask for feedback on the retro.
Tip

Keep the facilitator out of the content. If the Scrum Master has strong views on a topic, ask a team member or another Scrum Master to facilitate that part.

Sprint Retrospective questions

The three classic questions are: what went well, what did not go well, and what will we do differently? They are a fine start. The questions below go further; pick two or three per stage.

Set the stage

  • In one word, how was this Sprint for you?
  • From 1 to 5, how freely do you feel you can speak today?

Gather data

  • What happened this Sprint that we did not expect?
  • When were you most productive, and when were you most blocked?
  • Where did work wait the longest?
  • Did anything nearly slip past our Definition of Done?

Generate insights

  • Why did that happen? And why did that happen?
  • Is this a one-off or a pattern we have seen before?
  • Which assumption turned out to be wrong?
  • What is within our control, and what needs help from outside the team?

Decide and close

  • What is the smallest change that would make a noticeable difference?
  • Who will own it, and how will we know if it worked?
  • What will we stop doing to make room for it?
  • Was this retro worth the time? What would improve the next one?

Worked example: a Sprint Retrospective for a two-week Sprint

The following example is illustrative. The team, people and numbers are invented to show how the stages fit together.

Context. A seven-person Scrum Team builds an online booking app for a chain of physiotherapy clinics. Its Sprint Goal was patients can reschedule an appointment without phoning the clinic. The goal was partly met: rescheduling works, but the confirmation text message did not meet the Definition of Done. One of the Developers facilitates a 60-minute retro.

Set the stage and previous actions

Check-in words were mostly 'rushed', 'blocked' and 'OK'. Last retro's action, 'pair on every pull request over 300 lines', was done on four of five large changes and caught two defects early. The team kept it.

Gather data

The facilitator showed three facts: the burndown was flat for six days and then dropped steeply; three unplanned production fixes took about four person-days; and pull requests waited a median of 1.5 days for review. Silent writing produced 23 cards.

Generate insights

The dot vote put 'stories not ready at Sprint Planning' first and 'interruptions from production issues' second. Five whys on the first theme:

  1. Why was the burndown flat for six days? The two biggest stories were waiting for answers.
  2. Why? The message wording and cancellation rules had not been agreed.
  3. Why? The clinic operations lead, who decides them, had not seen the stories before Sprint Planning.
  4. Why? Backlog refinement only involved the Developers and the Product Owner.
  5. Why? No one asked which stakeholder decisions each upcoming story depended on.

Decide what to do

ActionOwnerHow we will checkWhen
Add a 'decisions needed from' field to stories; get those decisions before a story enters Sprint PlanningProduct OwnerStories waiting more than one day for a business decision (target: zero)Next retro
One Developer per day is the production support contact on a rota; others stay on the Sprint GoalScrum Master organises the rotaPerson-days of unplanned work by people not on rotaNext retro
(Parked) Reduce pull request wait timeNot assignedRevisit if the median wait stays above one dayNext retro

The rota needed capacity, so it went into the next Sprint Backlog. The new field was a working agreement and took ten minutes to set up.

What happened at the next retro

The team opened with the actions. One story still waited two days for a decision, so the first action was tightened: the Product Owner now books a 15-minute slot with the operations lead before each refinement session. The rota cut unplanned work for everyone else to under one person-day, so it became a permanent working agreement.

What made this retro useful: real data, one theme explored properly, actions small enough to finish and a check at the next retro. For deeper problems, a root cause analysis template gives the analysis more structure.

How to write retrospective action items that actually happen

Most retros generate good ideas; far fewer turn them into change. The difference is usually in how the action is written.

Weak actionWhy it failsStronger version
Communicate betterNo one knows what to do differentlyPost a two-line update in the team channel when a story is blocked for more than half a day
Improve testingToo big for one Sprint and no ownerSam writes automated tests for the three most-used booking endpoints this Sprint
Management should stop interrupting usOutside the team's control as writtenScrum Master agrees an interruption route with the operations manager by Friday
Everyone reviews code fasterOwned by everyone, so by no oneReview requests older than four hours are picked up first after each Daily Scrum; Priya tracks wait time

A good action is specific (anyone knows what will change), owned by one named person, small enough for one Sprint, checkable with agreed evidence and visible on the team board or in the Sprint Backlog. Treat actions as experiments: if one does not work, drop it at the next retro rather than carrying it forward out of guilt.

Psychological safety and the prime directive

A retrospective only works if people say what they really think. That depends on psychological safety: confidence that you can raise a problem or admit a mistake without being punished or embarrassed.

Many teams open with the Retrospective Prime Directive, written by Norm Kerth in *Project Retrospectives: A Handbook for Team Reviews* (2001). In short, it asks everyone to believe that, whatever the team discovers, each person did the best job they could, given what they knew at the time, their skills and abilities, the resources available and the situation at hand. It moves the conversation from who to blame towards what in the system to change.

  • Agree a confidentiality rule: details stay in the room; only agreed actions are shared.
  • Run an anonymous safety check from 1 to 5. If the average is low, talk about that first.
  • Discuss systems, not individuals: 'Alex broke the build' becomes 'one change can break our build; why?'.
  • Model it: facilitators and senior team members go first in admitting their own mistakes.

These habits reflect the Scrum values of openness, respect and courage.

Remote and hybrid Sprint Retrospectives

  • Set up an online whiteboard with columns, timer and voting before the session starts.
  • In hybrid sessions, ask everyone to join from their own device so the room does not dominate.
  • Use a round-robin speaking order, and collect cards asynchronously the day before if time zones are tight.
  • Keep it slightly shorter than in person, and paste actions into the team's tool before the call ends.

When the same problems keep coming back

Repetition is the most common complaint about retros. It usually has one of four causes.

CauseWhat it looks likeWhat to do
Actions are never finishedThe same theme every Sprint with similar actionsOpen with last Sprint's actions; fewer, smaller actions in the Sprint Backlog
The cause is outside the teamComplaints about other teams, environments or approvalsName an owner, usually the Scrum Master, to escalate it as an impediment and report back
Symptoms, not causesActions such as 'try harder' or 'be more careful'Spend longer on insights; use five whys or a fishbone diagram
The format has gone staleLow energy, the same people talkingChange format or facilitator, or run a retro on your retros

Common Sprint Retrospective mistakes and how to fix them

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
Skipping the retro when the Sprint went wellLessons about what to repeat are lost and the habit weakensKeep it, shorten it, and focus on what made the Sprint work
Too many actionsNothing gets finishedLimit to one to three; park the rest on a visible list
No owners or checksActions drift and are forgottenOne owner and one piece of evidence per action
Blaming individualsPeople stop speaking openlyDiscuss the system; keep performance conversations elsewhere
Uninvited managers or stakeholdersThe team self-censorsKeep it to the Scrum Team; share actions, not discussion
Turning it into a product reviewMixes up review and retrospectiveSend product feedback to the Product Backlog
Skipping the insights stageFixes treat symptomsProtect about a quarter of the time for understanding why

Frequently asked questions

What are the five steps of a Sprint Retrospective?

Set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, and close. The structure comes from Esther Derby and Diana Larsen's book Agile Retrospectives. It is not part of the Scrum Guide, but it is the most widely used way to organise the event.

What are the three Sprint Retrospective questions?

What went well, what did not go well, and what will we do differently next Sprint? Adding 'why did that happen?' before choosing actions usually produces better results.

Who attends the Sprint Retrospective?

The Scrum Team: the Developers, the Product Owner and the Scrum Master. Other people attend only if the team invites them for a specific reason.

Should the Product Owner attend the Sprint Retrospective?

Yes. The Product Owner is a member of the Scrum Team, and many process issues involve the backlog, stakeholders or the Sprint Goal. If the team cannot speak openly with the Product Owner present, treat that as an issue to resolve.

Who facilitates the Sprint Retrospective?

Often the Scrum Master, but the Scrum Guide does not require it. Any team member or a guest facilitator can lead it, and rotating the role builds skills.

How long is a Sprint Retrospective for a two-week Sprint?

The Scrum Guide sets a maximum of three hours for a one-month Sprint, usually shorter for shorter Sprints. Many two-week teams use 60 to 90 minutes, which is common practice rather than a rule.

What is the output of a Sprint Retrospective?

A small number of improvements to act on as soon as possible. The most impactful may be added to the next Sprint Backlog. Updated working agreements or a revised Definition of Done are also common outputs.

How many action items should a retrospective produce?

Usually one to three. A few actions that are finished and checked are worth far more than a long list that is forgotten.

Can we skip the retrospective if the Sprint went well?

No. It is a Scrum event, and good Sprints hold lessons about what to repeat. Keep it short if there is little to discuss, but keep the habit.

Should managers attend the Sprint Retrospective?

Not by default. Managers in the room can reduce openness. The team can invite a manager for a specific topic and share its actions afterwards.

What is the retrospective prime directive?

A statement by Norm Kerth, from his 2001 book Project Retrospectives, asking participants to believe that everyone did the best job they could given what they knew, their skills, the resources available and the situation. Many teams read it to set a blame-free tone.

What if retrospectives feel repetitive?

Check whether actions are being finished, whether the real cause sits outside the team, and whether you are treating symptoms. Then change the format, theme or facilitator.
