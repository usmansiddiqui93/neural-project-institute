Scrum Sprint Cycle: Stages, Length, Rules and Examples

The Scrum sprint cycle is the repeating loop of Sprint Planning, daily work with a Daily Scrum, a Sprint Review and a Sprint Retrospective, all inside a fixed-length Sprint of one month or less. When one Sprint ends, the next starts immediately, so each cycle builds on what the last one taught you. This guide shows each stage with its inputs and outputs, time-boxes for any Sprint length, the rules, a two-week calendar and worked examples you can copy.

Key takeaways

  • A Sprint is a fixed-length event of one month or less that contains Sprint Planning, Daily Scrums, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. Sprints run back to back with no gap.
  • Each stage has a clear output: a Sprint Backlog from planning, an updated plan each day, an adapted Product Backlog from the Review and improvements from the Retrospective.
  • The Scrum Guide sets maximum time-boxes for a one-month Sprint (8, 4 and 3 hours, plus 15 minutes daily); shorter Sprints usually need proportionally less.
  • Sprints are never extended. Scope can be renegotiated with the Product Owner, but changes that endanger the Sprint Goal or lower quality are not made.
  • Only the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint, and only when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.
  • Keep the length stable so the cycle becomes a reliable learning loop for forecasts and improvements.

What is the Scrum sprint cycle?

The Scrum sprint cycle is the fixed, repeating rhythm in which a Scrum Team plans, builds, inspects and improves its work. Each cycle is one Sprint of one month or less: it starts with Sprint Planning, continues with daily work and Daily Scrums, and ends with a Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective.

The 2020 Scrum Guide, still the current official version, calls Sprints the heartbeat of Scrum, where ideas are turned into value. The Sprint is a container: all the work needed to achieve the Product Goal, including every other Scrum event, happens inside it. The Guide also notes that each Sprint may be considered a short project.

The cycle matters because Scrum is empirical. Every few weeks the team and its stakeholders look at a real, usable Increment and decide what to do next, instead of following a plan made months earlier. The shorter and steadier the cycle, the faster you learn.

The stages of the Scrum sprint cycle, step by step

1. Sprint Planning: set the goal and the plan

Sprint Planning starts the Sprint. The whole Scrum Team answers three questions: why is this Sprint valuable, what can be Done this Sprint, and how will the chosen work get done.

  1. The Product Owner proposes how the product could increase its value, and the team agrees a Sprint Goal.
  2. The Developers select Product Backlog items that serve the goal, using past performance and upcoming capacity to judge how much fits.
  3. The Developers plan the work, often breaking items into tasks of a day or less.
  4. The Sprint Goal is final before the event ends. Goal, items and plan together form the Sprint Backlog.

2. The Sprint work and the Daily Scrum

Most of the cycle is the Developers building the Increment. Each working day they hold a 15-minute Daily Scrum to inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan. The Scrum Guide no longer prescribes the three questions; any structure that keeps the focus on the goal works.

  • Finish items before starting new ones, so Done work accumulates steadily rather than all at the end.
  • Update the Sprint Backlog as you learn; it is a living plan owned by the Developers.
  • Raise impediments early. The Scrum Master causes those the team cannot solve to be removed.
  • Release whenever it makes sense. An Increment can go to users at any point; the Sprint Review is never a gate to releasing value.

3. Product Backlog refinement: prepare the next cycles

Backlog refinement is an ongoing activity, not an event, so it has no fixed time-box. The team breaks down upcoming items and adds detail such as description, order and size, so that items for the next one or two Sprints can be Done within a single Sprint. Many teams book one or two short sessions per Sprint.

4. Sprint Review: inspect the product with stakeholders

The Sprint Review is the second-to-last event. The Scrum Team shows what it accomplished, discusses progress towards the Product Goal and agrees with stakeholders what to do next. It is a working session, not a slide presentation, and its output is an adjusted Product Backlog.

5. Sprint Retrospective: improve the way of working

The Sprint Retrospective concludes the Sprint. The team inspects individuals, interactions, processes, tools and its Definition of Done, then picks the most helpful changes. The most impactful improvements can go straight into the next Sprint Backlog so they actually happen.

6. The next Sprint starts immediately

There is no gap, "stabilisation week" or planning pause between Sprints. The next Sprint begins with Sprint Planning as soon as the Retrospective ends, carrying forward the updated Product Backlog, the agreed improvements and fresh data about capacity and pace.

The rules of the Sprint

The Scrum Guide sets only a few rules for the Sprint itself. Each one protects the cycle as a learning loop.

Scrum Guide ruleWhat it means in practice
Sprints are fixed-length events of one month or lessPick a length and keep it; never stretch a Sprint to finish work.
A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one endsNo gaps or buffer weeks between Sprints.
No changes are made that would endanger the Sprint GoalNew requests normally go to the Product Backlog, not into the current Sprint.
Quality does not decreaseNever relax the Definition of Done to hit a date.
The Product Backlog is refined as neededPreparation for future Sprints happens during the current one.
Scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as more is learnedIf an item proves bigger than expected, agree a smaller slice that still meets the goal.
Only the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint, if the Sprint Goal becomes obsoleteCancellation is a rare business decision, not a way to reset a bad Sprint.

The Guide adds that forecasting practices such as burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flows can be useful, but they do not replace empiricism. Inspecting the real Increment matters more than any chart.

How long is a sprint cycle?

The Scrum Guide's only length rule is one month or less. Within that limit the Scrum Team chooses, and in practice most teams use one to four weeks, with two weeks the most common choice.

LengthBest forBenefitsWatch out for
1 weekFast-changing products, early-stage start-ups, support-heavy teamsVery fast feedback; small risk per SprintEvents take a larger share of time; large items are hard to finish
2 weeksMost teams, and a sensible defaultBalances feedback speed with event overheadItems still need splitting to a few days each
3 weeksTeams with longer test or sign-off stepsMore room for complex itemsLess common, so harder to align with other teams
4 weeks / one monthStable environments, hardware or heavily regulated workFewer events; bigger IncrementsSlow feedback; the Sprint Goal may go stale; more risk per Sprint

How to choose the length

Think of the length as a range with a floor and a ceiling.

  • Floor: can you create a Done Increment? Team size and skills, your Definition of Done, the technology and the size of typical items set the shortest workable Sprint.
  • Ceiling: how long can feedback wait? The faster customer needs, the market or the technology change, the shorter the Sprint should be. The Scrum Guide warns that when a Sprint's horizon is too long, the goal may become invalid, complexity may rise and risk may increase.
  • Pick the shortest length that clears the floor, then hold it for several Sprints before judging.

The Scrum Guide does not say who decides the length. In practice the Scrum Team agrees it together: the Product Owner brings the need for feedback, and the Developers bring what it takes to get work Done.

Time-boxes for every Sprint length

The Scrum Guide gives maximum time-boxes for a one-month Sprint and says shorter Sprints usually have shorter events. Many teams scale the maximums in proportion to the Sprint length. Only the one-month column and the Daily Scrum are Scrum Guide figures; the rest are a common rule of thumb.

Event1-week Sprint2-week Sprint3-week SprintOne-month Sprint (Scrum Guide)
Sprint PlanningUp to 2 hoursUp to 4 hoursUp to 6 hoursUp to 8 hours
Daily Scrum15 minutes15 minutes15 minutes15 minutes
Sprint ReviewUp to 1 hourUp to 2 hoursUp to 3 hoursUp to 4 hours
Sprint RetrospectiveUp to 45 minutesUp to 1.5 hoursUp to 2.25 hoursUp to 3 hours
Tip

Time-boxes are maximums, not targets. A team with a well-refined backlog often finishes two-week Sprint Planning in 60 to 90 minutes. If events keep overrunning, look at refinement and item size before adding time.

Worked example: a two-week sprint cycle calendar

This illustrative calendar is for a six-person Scrum Team with a two-week Sprint that starts on a Wednesday. Mid-week boundaries avoid losing Sprint Planning or the Review to Monday bank holidays and Friday absences, a common practice rather than a rule.

DayDate patternWhat happens
Day 1Wednesday, week 1Sprint Planning 09:30–12:30; work starts after lunch
Days 2–5Thursday to TuesdayDaily Scrum 09:30–09:45; build, test and integrate
Day 6Wednesday, week 2Daily Scrum; refinement session 14:00–15:30 for the next Sprint
Days 7–9Thursday to MondayDaily Scrum; finish items to Done; release where useful
Day 10Tuesday, week 2Daily Scrum; Sprint Review 13:00–14:30; Sprint Retrospective 15:00–16:30
Day 11Wednesday, week 3Next Sprint starts with Sprint Planning

Calculating capacity for the cycle

Before Sprint Planning, the team works out how much time it really has. The numbers are illustrative.

  1. Nominal days: 6 Developers × 10 working days = 60 person-days.
  2. Holidays and leave: one public holiday for everyone (6 days) and one person on 3 days' leave leaves 51 person-days.
  3. Scrum events and refinement: about 12 hours each, or roughly 2 days each at 6 focused hours a day, removes 12 person-days, leaving 39.
  4. Support and unplanned work: the team reserves 10% for production support, leaving about 35 person-days.
  5. Compare with history: the team's last three Sprints at full strength averaged 24 story points with about 45 available person-days. Scaling by 35 ÷ 45 suggests about 19 points, so the Developers forecast 18 to 20 points and pick a Sprint Goal that fits.

How the cycle repeats: three feedback loops

The sprint cycle is really three loops running at different speeds:

LoopFrequencyInspectsAdapts
Daily loopEvery working dayProgress towards the Sprint GoalThe Sprint Backlog plan
Sprint loopEvery SprintThe Increment and the way of workingThe Product Backlog and team practices
Product loopOver many SprintsProgress towards the Product Goal and market responseThe Product Goal, roadmap and release plans

Worked example: forecasting across cycles

Stable cycles turn history into forecasts. An illustrative team completes 21, 26 and 24 story points in three two-week Sprints, an average of about 24 and a range of 21 to 26. About 120 points of work remain for the next release.

  • Optimistic: 120 ÷ 26 ≈ 4.6, so 5 Sprints (10 weeks).
  • Cautious: 120 ÷ 21 ≈ 5.7, so 6 Sprints (12 weeks).
  • Forecast to stakeholders: "5 to 6 Sprints, so 10 to 12 weeks, assuming scope stays the same." Update it after every Sprint Review.

This only works if the Sprint length and the Definition of Done stay stable; change either and the history no longer compares. See Scrum velocity and Agile release planning for more forecasting methods.

Changing work during a Sprint

The Sprint Goal is fixed; the detailed work is not. Use this table when something changes mid-cycle.

SituationAllowed?What to do
Developers discover extra tasks for a selected itemYesAdd them to the Sprint Backlog; it is the Developers' plan.
An item is much bigger than expectedYes, by renegotiationAgree with the Product Owner a smaller slice that still meets the Sprint Goal; return the rest to the Product Backlog.
A stakeholder asks for a new featureNot usuallyThe Product Owner adds it to the Product Backlog and orders it for a future Sprint.
An urgent production defect appearsYes, if the team decides it must be fixedFix it; if this threatens the Sprint Goal, the Product Owner decides what to drop or whether the goal still holds.
Someone wants to lower testing to finish on timeNoQuality does not decrease. Finish fewer items to the Definition of Done instead.
The Sprint Goal no longer makes senseOnly the Product Owner can actThe Product Owner may cancel the Sprint (see below).
Work is unfinished on the last dayThe Sprint still endsUnfinished items return to the Product Backlog to be re-ordered; they are not part of the Increment.

Cancelling a Sprint, step by step

A Sprint can be cancelled only if its Sprint Goal becomes obsolete, for example because a merger, a regulatory change or a cancelled contract makes the goal pointless. Only the Product Owner has the authority to cancel. The 2020 Scrum Guide says nothing more, so the steps below are common practice.

  1. Confirm the goal is truly obsolete. A difficult Sprint, a late item or a new idea is not a reason to cancel.
  2. The Product Owner decides and tells the Scrum Team and key stakeholders, explaining why.
  3. Inspect the work already Done. Done items still form a usable Increment and may be released.
  4. Return unfinished items to the Product Backlog, where the Product Owner re-orders them in light of the new situation.
  5. Hold a short Retrospective on what led to the cancellation and whether anything could have been spotted sooner.
  6. Start a new Sprint with Sprint Planning straight away, usually keeping the normal length so the cadence returns quickly.
Tip

Cancellation consumes resources and unsettles the team, so it should be rare. Frequent cancellations usually mean Sprints are too long for how fast priorities change.

Sprint cycle vs release cycle

Sprints and releases are separate decisions. Producing a Done Increment every Sprint means you could release; whether and when you do is a business choice.

CycleTypical lengthPurposeWho decides
SprintOne month or less, often two weeksProduce at least one Done Increment and inspect itScrum Team
ReleaseFrom several times a day to every few SprintsPut Increments in users' handsProduct Owner, with the organisation
Product roadmap horizonMonths to quartersSteer towards the Product GoalProduct Owner with stakeholders
Planning Interval (SAFe)Typically 8 to 12 weeksAlign many teams on shared objectivesAgile Release Train

Teams with continuous delivery may release many times per Sprint; teams with regulated or hardware products may release after several. Our release plan template helps map Sprints to releases.

Running sprint cycles across several teams

When several Scrum Teams work on one product, the Scrum Guide says they share the same Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product Owner, and must agree the same Definition of Done. Aligning their sprint cycles is the natural next step.

  • Share a cadence: the same Sprint length and start day, so integration, Reviews and forecasts line up.
  • Integrate continuously, so the combined Increment is Done every Sprint, not just each team's part.
  • Coordinate across teams through a Scrum of Scrums or a joint refinement session.
  • Use a scaling framework if you need more structure: Nexus and LeSS both run teams on a common Sprint. See Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS).

How to set up a sprint cycle for your team

  1. Agree a Product Goal and an ordered Product Backlog, with the top items refined small enough to finish in one Sprint.
  2. Write a Definition of Done that everyone understands; it defines what each cycle must produce. See the Definition of Done.
  3. Choose the Sprint length, usually two weeks to start, using the floor and ceiling questions above.
  4. Pick start and end days, often mid-week, and check them against public holidays and other teams' cycles.
  5. Book every event as a recurring series for the next three months: Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, refinement, Sprint Review and Retrospective, with time-boxes scaled to your length.
  6. Set up a visible board showing the Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog, in a tool or on a wall.
  7. Run three Sprints before changing anything structural, recording completed work, capacity and Retrospective actions.
  8. Review the cycle at a Retrospective. If feedback is too slow, shorten the Sprint; if nothing gets Done, split items or strengthen engineering practices before lengthening it.

Common sprint cycle problems and how to fix them

ProblemTypical signFix
OvercommittingHalf the items roll over every SprintPlan from real capacity and recent history; aim for a goal, not a full list.
Mini-waterfall inside the SprintTesting piles up in the last two daysSplit items smaller; test and integrate continuously; limit work in progress.
No real Sprint GoalThe goal is a list of ticket numbersWrite an outcome the Sprint achieves and let items flex around it.
Extending Sprints"Just two more days" to finishEnd on time; return unfinished work to the Product Backlog; discuss at the Retrospective.
Hardening SprintsA Sprint reserved for fixing before releaseStrengthen the Definition of Done so every Increment is releasable.
Changing length oftenVelocity jumps around and forecasts mean littleHold one length for several Sprints; change it only by Retrospective decision.
Retrospective actions never happenThe same issues come up every SprintPut one improvement into the next Sprint Backlog with an owner.

Tools and templates for the sprint cycle

Any board, physical or digital, can run a sprint cycle. Tools help once you want automatic burndowns, rollover of unfinished work and history across Sprints.

  • Jira, ranked #6 in our reviews and our pick for software and Agile teams, has mature backlogs, Sprints, boards and Sprint reports.
  • Wrike, our #1 overall (9.4/10), suits teams whose Sprints sit alongside cross-department projects, dashboards and portfolio reporting; its AI agents can handle Sprint operations such as rolling over work and writing summaries.
  • ClickUp (8.9/10) includes Sprints even on its Free Forever plan, which suits small teams starting out.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sprint cycle in Scrum?

The repeating rhythm of a fixed-length Sprint of one month or less: Sprint Planning, daily work with Daily Scrums, a Sprint Review and a Sprint Retrospective. The next Sprint starts immediately afterwards.

What are the stages of a sprint cycle?

Sprint Planning, the Sprint work with a Daily Scrum each day, ongoing backlog refinement, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. Then the next Sprint begins.

How long is a sprint cycle?

One month or less. Most teams use one to four weeks, and two weeks is the most common choice. Keep the length stable once chosen.

Who decides the Sprint length?

The Scrum Guide does not say. In practice the Scrum Team agrees it, balancing the Product Owner's need for feedback with the Developers' ability to finish Done work.

Can a Sprint be extended?

No. The Sprint ends on time even if work is unfinished. Incomplete items return to the Product Backlog and the next Sprint starts straight away.

Is there a break between Sprints?

No. A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one ends, beginning with Sprint Planning.

What happens to unfinished work at the end of a Sprint?

It returns to the Product Backlog, where the Product Owner re-orders it. It is not part of the Increment and cannot be shown as Done at the Sprint Review.

Can you add work to a Sprint in progress?

The Developers can add tasks to their plan, and scope can be renegotiated with the Product Owner, but no change should endanger the Sprint Goal. New requests normally go to the Product Backlog.

Who can cancel a Sprint?

Only the Product Owner, and only if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.

Is backlog refinement part of the sprint cycle?

Yes, but as an ongoing activity, not an event. The team refines upcoming items during the current Sprint so future Sprint Planning goes smoothly.

Can you release before the end of a Sprint?

Yes. An Increment can be released whenever it is Done and useful. The Sprint Review is never a gate to releasing value.

Should all teams on one product share the same sprint cycle?

It is strongly recommended. A shared length and start day make integration, Reviews and forecasting much easier, and frameworks such as Nexus and LeSS use a common Sprint.

What is the difference between a sprint cycle and a sprint cadence?

They are often used interchangeably. Cycle usually means the sequence of events in one Sprint; cadence means the regular rhythm of Sprints over time, such as every two weeks from Wednesday.

What are Sprint zero and hardening Sprints?

Neither is part of Scrum. Sprint zero is an informal set-up period before the first Sprint; a hardening Sprint is reserved for fixing before release and usually signals a weak Definition of Done.
