Increment in Scrum: Definition, Rules and Examples

An Increment in Scrum is a concrete, usable step towards the Product Goal: the work that meets the Definition of Done, added to everything Done before it. An Increment is born the moment a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done, a Sprint can produce several, and any of them can be released before the Sprint Review. This guide explains what the 2020 Scrum Guide requires, how an Increment differs from a release or an MVP, what does not count, and what unfinished "undone" work really costs, with a worked example.

Key takeaways

  • An Increment is one of Scrum's three artifacts. Its commitment is the Definition of Done: work that does not meet it is not part of the Increment.
  • Each Increment is additive to all prior Increments and thoroughly verified, so the whole product works together, and it must be usable to provide value.
  • The Developers create Increments; the whole Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint.
  • Done is not the same as released. The 2020 Scrum Guide says the Sprint Review should never be considered a gate to releasing value, but whether and when to release remains a business decision.
  • The 2020 Guide dropped "potentially releasable" and the quotation marks around "Done", made the Definition of Done a formal commitment and stated that several Increments can be created in one Sprint.
  • Work left outside the Definition of Done ("undone work") does not disappear; it accumulates until someone pays for it, usually in a stabilisation period just before release.

What is an Increment in Scrum?

An Increment is a concrete stepping stone towards the Product Goal. It is the sum of the product work that meets the Definition of Done, including everything completed in earlier Sprints, verified so that it all works together and usable enough to provide value. A new Increment exists the moment a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done.

The Increment is one of the three Scrum artifacts, alongside the Product Backlog and the Sprint Backlog. The other two describe work the team intends to do; the Increment is the only one that represents value already created. That is why it sits at the centre of empiricism in Scrum: stakeholders can inspect real, working product rather than a status report about it.

In everyday speech people say "product increment", "Sprint increment" or "shippable increment". The Scrum Guide simply says Increment, with a capital I, and that is the term used here.

What the 2020 Scrum Guide says about the Increment

The November 2020 Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland is still the current edition. Its Increment section is only a few sentences long, so each one carries weight. The left column paraphrases the Guide closely; the right column is our explanation.

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means in practice
An Increment is a concrete stepping stone toward the Product GoalEvery Increment should move the product measurably closer to the Product Goal. Work that is Done but unrelated to the goal is a question for the Product Owner
Each Increment is additive to all prior Increments and thoroughly verified, ensuring that all Increments work togetherA new feature that breaks an older one has not produced a valid Increment. Integration and regression checks are part of the job, not a later phase
In order to provide value, the Increment must be usableSomeone could use it for its intended purpose today. A design that exists only in a mock-up is not usable product
Multiple Increments may be created within a SprintNothing forces a team to batch its work until the last day. Teams practising continuous delivery may create and release many Increments per Sprint
The sum of the Increments is presented at the Sprint ReviewEverything Done during the Sprint is inspected together at the Sprint Review, even items already released
An Increment may be delivered to stakeholders before the end of the Sprint; the Sprint Review should never be considered a gate to releasing valueWaiting for the Sprint Review to release finished work is a choice, not a Scrum rule
Work cannot be considered part of an Increment unless it meets the Definition of Done"Nearly done", "done apart from testing" and "done on my machine" are not Done

The Definition of Done section adds two further rules. The moment a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done, an Increment is born. And an item that does not meet it cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review; it returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.

Who is accountable for the Increment

The Guide states that the entire Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint. Within that shared accountability, the Developers create the Increment and are accountable for instilling quality by adhering to the Definition of Done. The Product Owner is accountable for maximising the value of the product, which shapes which Increments are worth building and when they are released. The Scrum Master helps the team focus on creating high-value Increments that meet the Definition of Done. Our guide to Scrum team roles sets out the rest of each accountability.

The five properties of an Increment

Read together, the Guide's sentences give five properties. A piece of work that fails any of them is not yet part of the Increment. The test questions are our own, written to make each property checkable during a Sprint.

PropertyTest questionTypical failure
Meets the Definition of DoneHas every item on the Definition of Done been satisfied, with evidence?Testing or review skipped under deadline pressure
AdditiveDoes the product still do everything it did before this change?A new report breaks an existing export
Thoroughly verifiedHow do we know it works with all earlier Increments, not just on its own?Tested in isolation, never in an integrated environment
UsableCould an intended user, or an operator, use it for its purpose today?Back-end work with no way to reach it; a feature behind a login nobody has
A step towards the Product GoalWhich part of the Product Goal does this move forward?Work pulled in because it was easy, not because it mattered

Notice that usable and released are different tests. A usable Increment could go to users if the Product Owner and the organisation chose to release it. Whether it actually does is a separate decision, covered below.

Tip

A quick check before calling anything part of the Increment: if the Product Owner said "release it now", would anyone on the team object for a reason other than business timing? If yes, it is not Done.

How an Increment is created during a Sprint

An Increment is not produced in one step at the end of the Sprint. It grows item by item. The sequence below follows one Sprint; the rules come from the Scrum Guide, and the order of steps reflects common practice.

  1. Sprint Planning. The Product Owner proposes how the product could increase its value and utility in the current Sprint. The Scrum Team crafts a Sprint Goal, and the Developers select items they believe they can make Done. See Sprint Planning.
  2. Building. The Developers work on the selected items, using the Definition of Done as the quality bar from the start rather than as a final checklist.
  3. Verification and integration. Each item is tested, reviewed and integrated with the existing product so that it works with all prior Increments.
  4. An Increment is born. When an item meets the Definition of Done, it becomes part of the Increment. This can happen on any day of the Sprint.
  5. Release (optional and at any time). If the Product Owner and stakeholders decide it is worth releasing, and organisational rules allow, the Done work can go to users immediately.
  6. Sprint Review. The Scrum Team presents the sum of the Sprint's Increments to key stakeholders, who inspect them and discuss what to do next. The Product Backlog may be adjusted.
  7. Unfinished work. Items that did not meet the Definition of Done are not presented as Done. They return to the Product Backlog, where the Product Owner reorders them like any other item.
  8. Sprint Retrospective. The team may strengthen the Definition of Done, which raises the quality bar for every future Increment. See Sprint Retrospective.

Increment vs release, deliverable, MVP and Sprint

Several terms sit close to the Increment, and confusing them leads to poor decisions about release timing and progress reporting. Only the Increment and the Sprint are defined in the Scrum Guide; the other terms come from wider product and project management practice.

TermWhat it isRelationship to the Increment
IncrementUsable work that meets the Definition of Done, added to all prior Done workThe thing itself
Product Backlog itemA unit of work on the Product Backlog, such as a feature, fix or technical changeWhen an item meets the Definition of Done, it becomes part of the Increment
SprintA fixed-length event of one month or less in which the work happensThe container; one Sprint can produce one or many Increments
ReleaseMaking Done work available to users or customersA business decision about an Increment. One release can contain several Increments, or each can be released as soon as it is Done
DeliverableA project management term for any unique, verifiable product, result or capabilityAn Increment is a kind of deliverable, but many deliverables (a plan, a report) are not product Increments
Minimum viable product (MVP)The smallest version of a product used to test a business hypothesis with real usersAn MVP is usually reached after several Increments; an Increment does not need to be viable as a whole product
Planning Interval (SAFe)In SAFe 6.0, a fixed timebox of several iterations for an Agile Release Train (formerly Program Increment)Not a Scrum term. Despite the old name, a PI is a timebox, not a body of Done work

Two confusions are especially common. The first treats "Increment" and "release" as synonyms, so teams think they must ship at every Sprint Review or, conversely, that nothing counts until it ships. The second carries SAFe's former "Program Increment" into plain Scrum. If you work in SAFe, read our guide to the Scaled Agile Framework for how its Planning Intervals relate to team iterations.

Who decides whether to release an Increment

The 2020 Scrum Guide does not name who decides to release. The 2017 edition said the Increment must be in usable condition "regardless of whether the Product Owner decides to release it", and in practice the Product Owner, who is accountable for maximising value, decides with stakeholders when to release, within organisational rules such as change approval, regulatory sign-off or contractual acceptance. A useful decision rule: release when the value of users having the Increment now, plus the value of the feedback, outweighs the cost and risk of releasing now. Our guides to Agile release planning and continuous delivery cover the mechanics of planning and automating releases.

From potentially shippable to usable: how the wording changed

Older articles, courses and exam material still describe a "potentially shippable product increment". The phrase was common in earlier Scrum literature; the 2017 Scrum Guide used "potentially releasable". The 2020 edition uses neither. Comparing the two most recent editions on scrumguides.org shows what changed:

Topic2017 Scrum Guide2020 Scrum Guide
Core descriptionThe sum of all Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint plus the value of the increments of all previous SprintsA concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal
Quality wording"Done", useable and potentially releasable; each Increment thoroughly testedUsable; each Increment thoroughly verified; the word releasable does not appear
Definition of DoneThe definition of "Done" (in quotation marks), not linked to a commitmentDefinition of Done (no quotation marks) is the Increment's formal commitment
Who builds itThe Development TeamThe Developers; the whole Scrum Team is accountable
Number per SprintDescribed as one Increment at the end of the SprintMultiple Increments may be created within a Sprint
Release timingThe Product Owner may choose to release it immediatelyMay be delivered before the Sprint ends; the Sprint Review is never a gate to releasing value
DirectionA step toward a vision or goalA step toward the Product Goal, a concept added in 2020

The practical effect is small but real. "Thoroughly verified" is broader than "tested", which suits non-software work where verification may be an expert review or a pilot. Dropping "potentially releasable" separates two questions the old phrase blurred: is the work complete to our quality standard, and should we release it? The Definition of Done answers the first; the Product Owner and stakeholders answer the second.

Examples of Increments in and outside software

The examples below are illustrative. In each, the right-hand column shows a nearby piece of work that would not yet count, because it fails one of the five properties.

TeamIncrementHow it is verifiedNot yet an Increment
Software productA checkout option released to 10% of users behind a feature flagAutomated and exploratory tests, integrated with the existing checkout, monitored in productionCode merged but never run against the payment provider's test environment
MarketingA tested landing page with analytics recording visitsLinks, forms and tracking checked; brand and legal review doneFinal copy written but the page not built
HardwareA working prototype that passes defined bench testsTest results recorded against the agreed specificationCAD model approved but no physical unit assembled
EducationA complete course module, reviewed and published to learnersSubject-matter review, accessibility check, tested in the learning platformSlides finished but the assessment not written
Internal ITA migrated database table with reconciled record counts and a rollback planReconciliation report, performance check, operations sign-offData copied but not reconciled

Technical work can form part of an Increment when it meets the Definition of Done and leaves the product usable, for example a performance improvement or a security fix that users or operators benefit from. Paying down technical debt in Scrum is a common case. For how one larger product is split into increments over several cycles, see iterative and incremental development.

What does not count as an Increment

The Scrum Guide's rule is binary: work either meets the Definition of Done or it is not part of the Increment. The following are common borderline cases.

  • Partly finished items. A story that is 90% complete contributes nothing to the Increment. Splitting it into smaller items that can each be Done is usually better than reporting partial credit; see user stories for splitting techniques.
  • Work that passes acceptance criteria but not the Definition of Done. Acceptance criteria describe what one item must do; the Definition of Done describes quality standards for all work. An item needs both. Our Definition of Done guide explains the difference.
  • Documents about the product. A specification, wireframe or test plan may be valuable, but on its own it is not usable product. It becomes part of an Increment only if the team's Definition of Done treats it as part of the product, as user help text often is.
  • Spikes and experiments. A spike that answers a technical question produces knowledge, not usable product. Ken Rubin of Innolution argued in January 2021 that the 2020 definition fits such knowledge-acquisition work poorly and that teams should not worry when it does not produce an Increment. Keep the spike on the Product Backlog, record what was learned, and do not inflate the Increment with throwaway code.
  • Work Done to a weaker standard. If the Definition of Done has been quietly relaxed for one item, that item is not Done by the team's own rules.

Worked example: the hidden cost of undone work

This hypothetical example shows why a weak Definition of Done does not save effort; it moves it. A Scrum Team of seven Developers builds a customer self-service portal for a regional water utility. Sprints last two weeks (10 working days). The team estimates its available capacity at 60 person-days per Sprint: 7 Developers × 10 days = 70 person-days, minus leave, Scrum events and support duties.

Step 1: what was Done in Sprint 7

The Developers selected 11 Product Backlog items. By the end of the Sprint, 8 met the Definition of Done and became part of the Increment; 3 did not and returned to the Product Backlog. Proportion of selected items Done = 8 ÷ 11 = 0.727, or 72.7%. The Sprint Review inspected the 8 Done items only. The three unfinished items were not demonstrated, and the Product Owner reordered them alongside new feedback.

Step 2: the undone work behind the Done items

The team's Definition of Done excluded two activities, which it ran only before each release, every six Sprints:

  • Full regression testing in the integrated test environment: about 4 person-days of effort per Sprint's worth of change.
  • Security testing and fixing its findings: about 2 person-days per Sprint.

Undone work per Sprint (U) = 4 + 2 = 6 person-days. Over the number of Sprints between releases (n = 6), accumulated undone work = U × n = 6 × 6 = 36 person-days. Dividing by the Sprint capacity (C = 60 person-days) gives 36 ÷ 60 = 0.6: more than half a Sprint of effort that the release forecast did not show, usually spent in a so-called hardening or stabilisation Sprint. The Scrum Guide has no concept of a hardening Sprint; every Sprint is meant to end with Done, usable work.

The 8 items presented in Sprint 7 met the team's own Definition of Done, yet none could have been released that day, because they still needed regression and security testing. The Definition of Done was too weak to make the Increment genuinely usable, and the Sprint Review inspected work whose real state was unknown.

Step 3: compare bringing the work inside the Definition of Done

The team estimates that automating the regression suite and adding security scanning to the build would take a one-off investment (I) of 15 person-days, after which the remaining manual checks would cost about 1 person-day per Sprint (r). The cumulative effort of each option after k Sprints is:

  • Undone approach: U × k = 6k person-days.
  • Inside the Definition of Done: I + r × k = 15 + 1k person-days.
Sprints (k)Undone approach (person-days)Inside the Definition of Done (person-days)

Break-even occurs when 6k = 15 + k, so k = 15 ÷ (6 − 1) = 3 Sprints. Over the six-Sprint release cycle the second option costs 21 person-days instead of 36, a difference of 15 person-days, and every Increment becomes releasable on the day it is Done.

Interpreting the result

The calculation assumes undone work grows linearly. In practice it is usually worse, because defects found weeks after the change are harder to trace, and a single large stabilisation period is difficult to forecast. The estimates themselves are uncertain, so treat the break-even as a range rather than a precise figure. The decision it supports is clear: the Product Owner should order the automation work on the Product Backlog, and the team should agree at the next Sprint Retrospective which undone activities to move into the Definition of Done first. Large-scale frameworks treat this as a standing goal; see undone work in LeSS.

Increments with several Scrum Teams

When several Scrum Teams work on one product, they share one Product Backlog, one Product Goal and one Definition of Done, and the goal is a single integrated Increment rather than one per team. The 2020 Scrum Guide requires that Scrum Teams working on the same product mutually define and comply with the same Definition of Done.

Scrum.org's Nexus Guide (January 2021 edition) makes this explicit: its Integrated Increment represents the current sum of all integrated work completed by a Nexus towards the Product Goal, and it is inspected at the Nexus Sprint Review. Three teams that each finish their own features but cannot combine them have produced no usable Increment for the product. Integration testing therefore belongs inside the shared Definition of Done. For the scaling frameworks, see Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS) and Scrum@Scale.

What if a Sprint produces no Increment?

The Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint, so a Sprint with nothing Done is a strong signal to inspect, not a rule broken by one person. The Scrum Guide does not prescribe a response. In practice:

  • Hold the Sprint Review anyway. Discuss what was learned, what changed in the environment and what the Product Backlog should look like now. Do not demonstrate unfinished work as if it were Done.
  • Do not extend the Sprint. Sprints have a fixed length; extending one to finish work hides the problem.
  • Look for the cause at the Sprint Retrospective. Typical causes are items too large to finish in a Sprint, too much work started at once, dependencies on people outside the team, or a Definition of Done the team cannot yet meet with its current skills and tools.
  • Make items smaller. Refining items until several can be Done within a Sprint is usually the fastest fix; see backlog refinement.

Tracking Increments: templates and tools

The Increment itself is the working product, so it does not need its own document. What teams track is the status of the items that make it up and the plan for releasing them. Our free product backlog template records order, item, type, value, estimate, readiness, Sprint and status for each item, which is enough to see which items are Done. The release plan template maps features to releases and Sprints with story points, cumulative points and a forecast capacity check, which helps the Product Owner decide when a set of Increments is worth releasing.

If you track Done work as a measure, pair it with outcome measures such as usage or customer feedback; a team can produce many Increments that nobody uses. Frequently asked questions

What is an Increment in Scrum in simple terms?

It is the working product the Scrum Team has finished to its Definition of Done, including everything finished in earlier Sprints. It must be usable and move the product towards the Product Goal.

Is the Increment a Scrum artifact?

Yes. It is one of three, with the Product Backlog and the Sprint Backlog. Its commitment is the Definition of Done, which is not a separate artifact.

When is an Increment created?

According to the 2020 Scrum Guide, an Increment is born the moment a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done. That can happen on any day of the Sprint.

Can there be more than one Increment per Sprint?

Yes. The 2020 Scrum Guide says multiple Increments may be created within a Sprint. The sum of them is presented at the Sprint Review.

Does an Increment have to be released?

No. It must be usable, but releasing it is a separate business decision. It can be released at any time, including before the Sprint Review, or held and combined with later Increments.

Who creates the Increment?

The Developers create it and must adhere to the Definition of Done. The whole Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint.

What does potentially shippable increment mean?

It is an older phrase for an Increment in a releasable state. The 2017 Scrum Guide said "potentially releasable"; the 2020 Guide says usable and leaves the release decision separate.

What happens to work that does not meet the Definition of Done?

It is not part of the Increment, cannot be released or presented at the Sprint Review as Done, and returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.

Can unfinished work be shown at the Sprint Review?

Not as part of the Increment. Teams sometimes discuss ideas or designs to get direction, but they should make clear that this is not Done product and keep it separate from the inspection of the Increment.

Does an Increment have to be software?

No. A published course module, a tested landing page or a prototype that passes its bench tests can all be Increments if they meet the team's Definition of Done and are usable.

Is an Increment the same as an MVP?

No. An MVP is a product version used to test a business hypothesis with real users. It usually takes several Increments to reach one.
