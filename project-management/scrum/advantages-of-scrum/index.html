Advantages of Scrum: Benefits, Limitations and Evidence

The main advantages of Scrum are earlier usable results, a smaller amount of money and effort at risk between feedback points, clear priorities, built-in improvement and more ownership for the team. None of them is automatic: each comes from a specific part of the framework and disappears when the conditions behind it are missing. This guide explains where each benefit comes from, what the research evidence says, what Scrum costs in time and where it fits poorly.

Key takeaways

  • Scrum's benefits come from short, regular inspect-and-adapt cycles: Sprints of one month or less, each producing a usable Increment that real stakeholders can inspect.
  • Shorter Sprints limit how much cost and effort is spent before the next inspection; the 2020 Scrum Guide states this directly.
  • Every advantage has a precondition, such as an empowered Product Owner, stakeholders who attend the Sprint Review or a Definition of Done the team actually meets.
  • A large study of 1,978 Scrum teams (Verwijs and Russo, 2023) linked effectiveness most strongly to stakeholder concern and responsiveness, not to performing the events.
  • Scrum costs time: in an illustrative two-week Sprint, the events take about 13% of each person's hours, which is the price of the feedback loop.
  • Scrum fits poorly with interrupt-driven work, fixed-specification contracts and organisations that cannot give a team a dedicated Product Owner.

What are the advantages of Scrum?

The advantages of Scrum are the practical benefits a team and its organisation gain from working in short, fixed-length Sprints that each produce a usable Increment: earlier value, faster feedback, lower exposure to wasted effort, clearer priorities, visible problems and steady improvement. They arise from Scrum's empirical design, not from holding the events.

The 2020 Scrum Guide defines Scrum as a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organisations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. The word "complex" matters. Scrum's benefits are largest where more is unknown than known, because that is where learning from each Increment changes what you do next. On repetitive work with a known solution there is little to learn each Sprint, and the same mechanics add cost without adding much value.

The table below summarises each advantage, the element of Scrum that produces it and the condition it depends on. The final column is the warning sign that the benefit is not reaching you.

Why Scrum produces these benefits

Scrum is founded on empiricism: decisions are based on what is observed rather than on a fixed upfront plan. The Scrum Guide describes three pillars, transparency, inspection and adaptation, and builds an inspect-and-adapt point into every event. This section is about how those pillars turn into benefits. Read empiricism in Scrum for the pillars themselves.

Three design choices do most of the work:

  • A short, fixed horizon. Sprints last one month or less. The Scrum Guide notes that when a Sprint's horizon is too long, the Sprint Goal may become invalid, complexity may rise and risk may increase, and that shorter Sprints can be used to generate more learning cycles and limit the risk of cost and effort to a smaller time frame.
  • A usable result at the end of every cycle. An Increment must meet the Definition of Done, so each Sprint ends with something that can be inspected for real, not a progress percentage.
  • Clear accountabilities. One Product Owner orders the Product Backlog, the Developers own the plan for the Sprint and the Scrum Master is accountable for the team's effectiveness. Decisions have an owner, so they are made quickly.

The Guide also states that Scrum makes visible the relative efficacy of current management, environment and work techniques so that improvements can be made. That is an advantage and a source of discomfort at the same time: Scrum shows problems; it does not solve them by itself.

Tip

Scrum is purposefully incomplete. It defines the parts needed to implement Scrum theory and leaves practices such as estimation, technical practices and release management to the team. Benefits attributed to "Scrum" often come from these added practices, so name them when you report results.

The main advantages of Scrum explained

1. Usable results early and regularly

Because each Sprint produces a Done Increment, users can start benefiting from the most valuable features long before the whole product is complete. The Product Owner can release whenever an Increment is valuable enough; the Scrum Guide allows several Increments within one Sprint and does not tie release to the Sprint Review. This advantage depends on a Definition of Done that genuinely makes work usable. A team whose "Done" excludes integration or testing produces Increments that cannot be released, and the advantage disappears.

2. Less money and effort at risk between inspections

Every Sprint ends with an inspection of real work, so a misunderstanding can cost at most one Sprint of effort before it is found. The worked example below shows how large this effect is. The limitation is that inspection only reduces risk when the right people inspect: an Increment shown to no one, or to a proxy who does not represent users, leaves the risk where it was.

3. Building the right product, not just building the product right

The Sprint Review is a working session in which the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the Increment and decide what to do next, and the Product Backlog may be adjusted as a result. Over several Sprints, features that users ignore can be dropped and requests that matter can move to the top. The precondition is stakeholder time: if sponsors and users cannot attend regularly, the feedback loop exists on paper only.

4. Clear priorities and fewer conflicting instructions

Scrum gives one person, the Product Owner, accountability for ordering the Product Backlog. Anyone who wants to change it must persuade the Product Owner. For teams used to receiving requests from several managers at once, this is often the most immediately felt advantage. It fails when the Product Owner lacks the authority to say no, or when the role is split across a committee.

5. Problems become visible quickly

Transparent artifacts, a Daily Scrum focused on the Sprint Goal and a fixed end date make delays, dependencies and quality issues hard to hide. A slow approval process that would be absorbed into a long schedule shows up within a Sprint or two as a missed Sprint Goal. The benefit only materialises if someone with authority acts on what becomes visible.

6. Improvement built into the calendar

The Sprint Retrospective happens every Sprint, so improvement is a routine rather than a project. Small changes compound: a team that removes one recurring delay per Sprint is working very differently after six months. Retrospectives lose value when actions are not followed up or when the same format produces the same vague actions each time.

7. Ownership and morale

Developers decide how to turn Product Backlog items into a Done Increment, and the Sprint Backlog is their plan, made by and for them. Team autonomy is one of the five factors in the Verwijs and Russo study discussed below. This depends on managers resisting the urge to assign tasks or set velocity targets; self-management that can be overruled at any time is not experienced as ownership.

8. Consistent quality

The Scrum Guide states that quality does not decrease during the Sprint, and every Increment must meet the Definition of Done. Testing and integration therefore happen continuously rather than in a phase at the end, where they are usually squeezed. The limitation is that Scrum sets no quality standard of its own: a weak Definition of Done produces weak Increments on a regular schedule.

9. Predictability through regular inspection

The Guide says Sprints enable predictability by ensuring inspection and adaptation of progress toward a Product Goal at least every calendar month. This is predictability of learning, not a guarantee of fixed scope by a fixed date. Teams often add forecasting practices such as velocity or throughput, but these are complementary practices, not part of Scrum, and they become unreliable when used as targets.

Worked example: how much is at risk between feedback points

The following example is illustrative. The team, costs and durations are invented to show the arithmetic; replace them with your own figures.

Scenario. A team of 7 people is building an online appointment-booking system for a chain of dental clinics over 24 weeks. Each person costs the organisation £4,000 per week, fully loaded, and works 37.5 hours a week. The team compares four ways of getting feedback from real users.

Step 1: the team's weekly cost and total budget

  • Weekly team cost (C) = number of people × cost per person per week = 7 × £4,000 = £28,000 per week.
  • Budget (B) = C × duration in weeks = £28,000 × 24 = £672,000.

Step 2: maximum spend between inspections

Formula: maximum exposure (E) = C × I, where I is the interval in weeks between inspections of usable work by stakeholders. E is in pounds. Exposure as a share of budget = E ÷ B × 100%.

Feedback arrangementInterval I (weeks)Maximum exposure E = C × IShare of budget
One-week Sprints1£28,0004.2%
Two-week Sprints2£56,0008.3%
Four-week Sprints4£112,00016.7%
Single release; first user feedback in acceptance testing at week 2020£560,00083.3%

Interpretation. With two-week Sprints, a misunderstanding about what users need can consume at most £56,000 of effort before the next Sprint Review exposes it. If users first see the product in acceptance testing at week 20, up to £560,000 of work may rest on an assumption nobody has tested. This is a ceiling, not an expected loss: most work in either arrangement will be useful. The figure shows how much is spent on trust before evidence arrives.

Step 3: the cost of the feedback loop

Scrum's events take time. Assume the team uses half of the one-month maximum timeboxes, a common practice for two-week Sprints rather than a Scrum Guide rule: Sprint Planning 4 hours, Sprint Review 2 hours, Sprint Retrospective 1.5 hours, and a 15-minute Daily Scrum on each of the 10 working days (2.5 hours).

  • Event hours per person per Sprint = 4 + 2 + 1.5 + 2.5 = 10 hours.
  • Hours available per person per Sprint = 37.5 × 2 = 75 hours, so events take 10 ÷ 75 × 100% = 13.3% of each person's time.
  • Hourly cost per person = £4,000 ÷ 37.5 = £106.67. Event cost per Sprint = 10 hours × 7 people × £106.67 = £7,467, or about £89,600 across 12 Sprints (13.3% of the budget).

Backlog refinement comes on top. The 2017 Scrum Guide said refinement usually consumed no more than 10% of the Development Team's capacity (7.5 hours per person in this Sprint); the 2020 Guide removed that figure, so treat it as a historical rule of thumb only.

What the project manager or Product Owner should do with this. The event time is not all overhead: Sprint Planning replaces planning that any approach needs, and a Sprint Review replaces some status reporting and approval meetings. The useful question is whether each event earns its time. If Sprint Reviews are poorly attended, the team is paying for the feedback loop without receiving the reduction in exposure, and fixing attendance is worth more than shortening the events.

Limitations. The model treats all spend as equally exposed, ignores the value of releasing early and assumes inspections actually catch misunderstandings. It compares feedback intervals, not methods: a predictive project with prototypes and staged user reviews can also shorten its feedback interval.

What research says about Scrum's benefits

Claims about Scrum's benefits are easier to make than to test. The largest peer-reviewed study we found is *A Theory of Scrum Team Effectiveness* by Christiaan Verwijs and Daniel Russo, published in ACM Transactions on Software Engineering and Methodology (vol. 32, no. 3, 2023). After thirteen exploratory case studies, the authors tested their model with survey data from 4,940 respondents in 1,978 Scrum teams.

  • Effectiveness was defined as stakeholder satisfaction with the outcomes and team morale, not velocity or output.
  • Five factors explained effectiveness: responsiveness, stakeholder concern, continuous improvement, team autonomy and management support.
  • Stakeholder concern (the team's focus on stakeholder needs and value) had the largest direct effect on effectiveness, and responsiveness (how quickly the team can release and act on feedback) also had a significant direct effect.
  • Management support worked indirectly, by strengthening autonomy, continuous improvement and stakeholder concern. The authors conclude that management support in itself will not make teams more effective.

The practical reading is that Scrum's benefits come from the conditions around the events: close contact with stakeholders, the ability to release often, autonomy and a habit of improvement. A team that holds every event but never releases and never meets users should not expect the advantages in this guide.

The authors list important limitations. The data are cross-sectional, so they do not prove cause and effect; outcomes were rated by team members rather than by stakeholders; the sample was voluntary; and the findings apply to software teams. The study also does not compare Scrum with other approaches, so it cannot show that Scrum outperforms Kanban or a predictive plan.

Limitations and disadvantages of Scrum

Scrum's limitations are mostly the reverse side of its preconditions. Knowing them lets you decide whether to adapt the environment, add practices or choose another approach.

LimitationWhy it happensWhat to do
Needs an empowered Product OwnerOrdering the Product Backlog requires authority and timeAgree the Product Owner's mandate before starting; if no one can hold it, Scrum will struggle
Needs a dedicated, cross-functional teamSelf-management and a Done Increment depend on people who can finish the work togetherReduce shared specialists; make dependencies visible on a Scrum board
Poor fit for interrupt-driven workUrgent requests arrive daily, disrupting the Sprint GoalUse Kanban for operational flow, or split support from product work
Tension with fixed-scope, fixed-price contractsScope is meant to emerge from feedbackPrefer contracts that fix time and budget and let scope be re-ordered
Event timeAbout 13% of each person's time in the example aboveKeep events within their timeboxes and make each one end in a decision
Incomplete by designScrum says nothing about budgeting, architecture or portfolio governanceAdd complementary practices deliberately; see Scrum techniques
Partial adoptionThe Guide states that implementing only parts of Scrum is possible, but the result is not ScrumChange practices through Retrospectives, but keep the framework intact
Exposes uncomfortable problemsTransparency reveals organisational impediments quicklyPrepare sponsors to act on what becomes visible
Coordination across many teamsBeyond asking teams on one product to share a Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product Owner, the Guide says little about coordinating themUse a scaling approach such as a Scrum of Scrums only once single teams deliver Done Increments

The most common disadvantage in practice is not in the table: adopting the events and job titles without the values and empiricism behind them, sometimes called "zombie Scrum". The Scrum values of commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage are what make the transparency safe enough to be useful.

When Scrum's advantages outweigh its costs

Before adopting Scrum, check whether the conditions that create each benefit are in place. Each "no" below names the advantage you are likely to lose.

QuestionIf the answer is no, you lose
Is the work complex, with requirements or solutions likely to change?Most of the value of learning each Sprint
Can the product be delivered in usable pieces?Early value and meaningful Sprint Reviews
Is there one person with authority to order the Product Backlog?Clear priorities
Will users or their representatives attend a Sprint Review every few weeks?Reduced exposure and building the right thing
Can a team of about 10 or fewer people be dedicated to the product?Ownership, focus and a reliable Done Increment
Will managers act on impediments the team raises?The benefit of visible problems

Three or more "no" answers suggest fixing the environment first or choosing another approach. For a scored comparison with a predictive plan, use the checklist in Scrum vs Waterfall; for continuous, unpredictable demand, compare Kanban vs Scrum. How to check whether your team is getting the benefits

Benefits claimed but not measured tend to be assumed. Pick a few measures that map to the advantages you expect, review them in Retrospectives and look at trends rather than single Sprints.

AdvantageUseful measureHow to read it
Earlier valueTime from an item starting to it being usable (cycle time)Falling or stable cycle time suggests work is finished rather than parked
Reduced exposureWeeks between stakeholder inspections of usable workShould equal the Sprint length; longer gaps mean the feedback loop is broken
Building the right thingShare of Sprint Reviews that change the Product BacklogIf feedback never changes anything, either no one is listening or no one is attending
FocusSprint Goal success rateSee the formula below
ImprovementRetrospective actions completed by the next RetrospectiveA low rate means improvement is talked about, not done
QualityDefects found after an Increment is DoneA rising trend suggests the Definition of Done is too weak or not being met

Sprint Goal success rate = Sprints in which the Sprint Goal was met ÷ Sprints completed × 100%. For example, a team that met its Sprint Goal in 9 of its last 12 Sprints has a rate of 9 ÷ 12 × 100% = 75%. There is no official target. Use the trend and the reasons behind each miss; a team that meets every goal may be setting goals that are too safe. Our guide to Scrum metrics and KPIs covers more measures and their pitfalls.

Common mistakes that erase Scrum's benefits

  • Treating the events as the benefit. Holding a Sprint Review without users present produces the cost of the event without the feedback.
  • Lengthening Sprints to fit more work in. Longer Sprints increase the exposure calculated above and delay learning.
  • A Product Owner by title only. If someone else can overrule the order of the Product Backlog, priorities stay contested.
  • Using velocity as a target. Estimates inflate and the number stops supporting forecasts.
  • Weakening the Definition of Done under pressure. Work appears finished but cannot be released, so early value and quality both fall away.
  • Expecting Scrum to fix organisational problems. It exposes them; people with authority still have to act.
  • Comparing Scrum with a perfect plan. Compare it with how your current approach actually performs, including late changes and rework.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main advantages of Scrum?

Usable results every Sprint, a cap on how much is spent before the next inspection, clear priorities through one Product Owner and one ordered Product Backlog, problems that become visible quickly, built-in continuous improvement and more ownership for the team. Each depends on conditions such as stakeholder involvement and a meaningful Definition of Done.

What are the disadvantages of Scrum?

Scrum needs an empowered Product Owner, a dedicated cross-functional team and regular stakeholder time. It fits poorly with interrupt-driven work and fixed-specification contracts, its events take a noticeable share of the team's time, and it deliberately leaves practices such as budgeting and architecture to you.

Is there evidence that Scrum works?

A 2023 study by Verwijs and Russo of 1,978 Scrum teams found that effectiveness, measured as stakeholder satisfaction and team morale, was linked mainly to stakeholder concern and responsiveness, supported by continuous improvement, autonomy and management support. The data are cross-sectional and self-reported, and the study does not compare Scrum with other approaches.

Does Scrum reduce project risk?

It reduces the risk of building the wrong thing, because usable work is inspected at least every Sprint. The 2020 Scrum Guide says shorter Sprints limit the risk of cost and effort to a smaller time frame. It does not remove technical, commercial or supplier risks, which still need managing.

How much time do Scrum events take?

In a two-week Sprint using half of the one-month maximum timeboxes, the events take about 10 hours per person, roughly 13% of a 75-hour Sprint. Backlog refinement is extra. Much of this time replaces planning and reporting that other approaches also need.

Is better quality an advantage of Scrum?

It can be. Every Increment must meet the Definition of Done and the Scrum Guide states that quality does not decrease during the Sprint. The benefit depends on a strong Definition of Done; Scrum itself sets no quality standard.

Do the advantages of Scrum apply outside software?

Yes, where the work is complex and can be delivered in usable pieces, such as marketing, research or service design. Where output only has value when complete, such as a building, many advantages shrink.

Why do some teams never see the benefits of Scrum?

Usually because a precondition is missing: a Product Owner without authority, stakeholders who do not attend Sprint Reviews, managers who assign tasks, or Increments that never meet the Definition of Done.

Is Scrum better than Waterfall?

Not in every case. Scrum suits complex work with changing requirements; a predictive approach suits stable, well-defined work where late change is expensive. See Scrum vs Waterfall.

Does Scrum make delivery dates predictable?

Scrum makes progress inspectable at least every month, which supports evidence-based forecasting. It does not guarantee fixed scope by a fixed date; teams often add forecasting practices such as velocity or throughput, which are not part of Scrum.
