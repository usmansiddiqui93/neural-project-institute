Scrum Artifacts: The 3 Artifacts, Commitments and Examples

Scrum artifacts are the three things a Scrum Team uses to make work and value visible: the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. Under the 2020 Scrum Guide each has a commitment (the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done) that gives it focus and a way to measure progress. This guide explains what each artifact is, who owns it and how the three fit together, then follows them through a real-world Sprint with step-by-step setup advice and the mistakes to avoid.

Key takeaways

  • Scrum has exactly three artifacts: the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment.
  • Each artifact has a commitment: the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done. The commitments were added in the 2020 Scrum Guide.
  • The Product Owner is accountable for the Product Backlog, the Developers own the Sprint Backlog, and the whole Scrum Team is accountable for a valuable, useful Increment every Sprint.
  • The purpose of every artifact is transparency: everyone inspecting it should see the same reality, so decisions are based on facts.
  • Work that does not meet the Definition of Done is not part of the Increment and returns to the Product Backlog.
  • Burndown charts, roadmaps, velocity and Definitions of Ready are useful practices, but they are not Scrum artifacts.

What are Scrum artifacts?

Scrum artifacts are the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. According to the 2020 Scrum Guide, they represent work or value and are designed to maximise transparency of key information. Each artifact has a commitment (the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done) that provides focus and a measure of progress.

The word comes from software development, where an artifact is something produced or used while building a product. Scrum.org describes Scrum's artifacts as the information a Scrum Team and its stakeholders need to understand the product, the work being done and the value delivered.

  • The Product Backlog shows everything the team currently believes is needed to improve the product.
  • The Sprint Backlog shows what the Developers plan to do this Sprint, and why.
  • The Increment is the real, usable product that results.

The artifacts sit alongside Scrum's three accountabilities and five events. If you are new to the framework, start with what Scrum is, then read about the Scrum team roles and Scrum events.

What the Scrum Guide says about artifacts

The Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland is short, so it is worth knowing exactly what it says. The November 2020 edition is still the current official version (checked 26 October 2026).

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means for your team
Artifacts represent work or value and are designed to maximise transparencyIf an artifact is out of date or hidden, it is failing at its main job
Each artifact contains a commitment that enhances transparency and focus against which progress can be measuredEvery artifact answers "towards what?": the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal or the Definition of Done
Decisions are based on the perceived state of the artifacts; low transparency can diminish value and increase riskPoor artifacts lead to poor decisions, at every level
The Product Backlog is the single source of work undertaken by the Scrum TeamWork outside the backlog is invisible and should be brought in
The Sprint Backlog is a plan by and for the DevelopersManagers and the Product Owner do not dictate the plan
Work cannot be considered part of an Increment unless it meets the Definition of Done"Nearly done" is not done

What changed in the 2020 Scrum Guide

The 2017 Scrum Guide already had the same three artifacts. The 2020 edition added the idea of a commitment for each one, introduced the Product Goal as a new concept, and moved the Sprint Goal inside the Sprint Backlog. It also made the Definition of Done the formal commitment for the Increment. Many older articles and exam dumps predate these changes, so check the date of anything you study.

The three artifacts support Scrum's three pillars of empiricism: transparency makes inspection possible, and inspection makes adaptation possible.

How the Scrum artifacts fit together

The artifacts are linked through the Scrum events. In one Sprint, the flow looks like this:

  1. The Product Owner keeps the Product Backlog ordered against the Product Goal, with help from the Developers during refinement.
  2. In Sprint Planning, the Scrum Team agrees a Sprint Goal, and the Developers select items from the top of the Product Backlog and plan the work. Together these form the Sprint Backlog.
  3. Each day, the Developers inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal at the Daily Scrum and adapt the Sprint Backlog.
  4. Whenever an item meets the Definition of Done, it becomes part of the Increment. It can be released at any time, even before the Sprint ends.
  5. At the Sprint Review, the team and stakeholders inspect the Increment and progress towards the Product Goal, and the Product Backlog is adjusted.
  6. At the Sprint Retrospective, the team looks at how it worked, which can include strengthening the Definition of Done.
  7. Items not Done return to the Product Backlog, and the next Sprint begins.

Each artifact feeds the next: no Product Backlog, no Sprint Backlog; no Sprint Backlog, no plan for an Increment; no Increment, nothing real to inspect.

Product Backlog and the Product Goal

The Product Backlog is an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product, and the single source of work undertaken by the Scrum Team. Items at the top are small and clear enough to be Done within one Sprint; items further down stay coarse until they are needed. Product Backlog refinement is the ongoing activity of breaking items down and adding detail such as a description, order and size. The Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing.

The Product Goal is its commitment. The Scrum Guide describes it as a future state of the product that serves as a target for the Scrum Team to plan against. The team must fulfil or abandon one Product Goal before taking on the next.

Weak Product GoalStronger Product GoalWhy it is stronger
Improve the websiteMembers can reserve and renew books online without visiting a branchDescribes an outcome for users that can be tested
Build version 2Small retailers can set up their online shop in under an hour without helpStates who benefits and how you will know

The Product Owner is accountable for the Product Backlog, including developing and communicating the Product Goal, creating and ordering items and keeping the backlog transparent. Many teams write items as user stories, but the Scrum Guide does not require any format.

For ordering techniques, a worked example of building a backlog and how to keep it healthy, read our full guide to the Product Backlog.

Sprint Backlog and the Sprint Goal

The Sprint Backlog is the Developers' plan for the Sprint. The Scrum Guide describes it as a highly visible, real-time picture of the work the Developers plan to accomplish during the Sprint to achieve the Sprint Goal. It has three parts:

PartQuestion it answersExample
Sprint GoalWhy are we doing this Sprint?Members can renew a loan online and see the new due date
Selected Product Backlog itemsWhat will we deliver?Renew a single loan; show a message when the renewal limit is reached
The planHow will we deliver it?Tasks such as connect to the library system's renewal API, build the renew button, write automated tests

The Sprint Goal is the single objective for the Sprint. It is created by the whole Scrum Team during Sprint Planning and added to the Sprint Backlog. Although it is a commitment by the Developers, it allows flexibility in the exact work needed to achieve it.

The Sprint Backlog is updated throughout the Sprint as more is learned, and should have enough detail for the Developers to inspect their progress in the Daily Scrum. If the work turns out to be different from what they expected, the Developers collaborate with the Product Owner to negotiate the scope of the Sprint Backlog without affecting the Sprint Goal.

Change during the SprintAllowed?
Developers add, split or remove tasksYes, at any time; it is their plan
Developers and Product Owner swap or drop an item, keeping the Sprint GoalYes, by negotiation
A stakeholder adds work directlyNo; requests go to the Product Owner and the Product Backlog
Changes that would endanger the Sprint GoalNo; if the goal becomes obsolete, only the Product Owner can cancel the Sprint

Increment and the Definition of Done

An Increment is a concrete stepping stone towards the Product Goal. According to the Scrum Guide, each Increment is additive to all prior Increments and thoroughly verified, so that all Increments work together, and it must be usable in order to provide value.

Four rules from the Scrum Guide are often misunderstood:

  • Several Increments can be created within one Sprint. The sum of them is presented at the Sprint Review.
  • An Increment can be delivered before the Sprint ends. The Sprint Review should never be considered a gate to releasing value.
  • Only Done work counts. The moment a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done, an Increment is born.
  • Unfinished work goes back. An item that does not meet the Definition of Done cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review; it returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.

The Definition of Done

The Definition of Done is the commitment for the Increment: a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product. It creates transparency by giving everyone a shared understanding of what work was completed.

  • If the organisation has a Definition of Done as a standard, all Scrum Teams must follow it as a minimum.
  • If not, the Scrum Team must create one appropriate for the product.
  • The Developers are required to conform to it.
  • Several Scrum Teams working on the same product must mutually define and comply with the same Definition of Done.

A Definition of Done for a web product might read:

  • Code peer-reviewed and merged to the main branch.
  • Automated tests written and passing; no known critical defects.
  • Meets the organisation's accessibility standard.
  • Works on current mobile and desktop browsers.
  • Help text updated and deployed to production behind a feature switch if needed.

Who owns each Scrum artifact?

Ownership in Scrum means accountability, not exclusive access. Everyone can see every artifact, and several people contribute to each.

ArtifactAccountableContributesTypical update rhythm
Product BacklogProduct OwnerDevelopers size and clarify items; stakeholders suggest items and give feedback; the Scrum Master helps with techniquesContinuously; at least after every Sprint Review
Sprint BacklogDevelopersThe Product Owner helps clarify items and negotiate scope; the Scrum Master coachesDaily, or whenever the plan changes
IncrementWhole Scrum TeamDevelopers build it to the Definition of Done; the Product Owner decides with stakeholders when to release, within organisational rulesEvery time an item meets the Definition of Done

For how the three accountabilities divide the rest of the work, see Scrum team roles.

Worked example: the three artifacts through one Sprint

A city library service is building online services for its members. The team has a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and five Developers, and runs two-week Sprints. The library and its people are illustrative.

Product Backlog and Product Goal

Product Goal: "Members can reserve and renew books online without visiting a branch." Before Sprint 4, the top of the Product Backlog looks like this:

OrderItemSize (points)
1Renew a single loan online5
2Show a clear message when a loan cannot be renewed2
3Email confirmation of the new due date3
4Fix: due dates shown in the wrong date format1
5Renew all loans at once5
6Reserve a book that is on loan8

Sprint Backlog and Sprint Goal

In Sprint Planning, the Product Owner explains that renewals cause the most phone calls. The Developers' recent average is about 11 points per Sprint, so they select items 1 to 4 (11 points). The Scrum Team agrees the Sprint Goal: "Members can renew a loan online and see the new due date." The Developers break the items into tasks:

ItemTasks (the plan)
Renew a single loan onlineConnect to the library system's renewal API; build the renew button; update the loans page; automated tests
Message when a loan cannot be renewedList the refusal reasons; write the messages with the content designer; tests
Email confirmationEmail template; trigger on renewal; tests
Date format fixChange the date formatting; regression test

During the Sprint

  • Day 3: the date fix meets the Definition of Done and is released. That is the first Increment of the Sprint.
  • Day 6: the Developers discover that the library system rejects renewals for books another member has reserved, which needs extra handling. At the Daily Scrum they see that the email confirmation is now at risk. They talk to the Product Owner, who agrees to return the email item to the Product Backlog. The Sprint Goal is unchanged.
  • Day 8: online renewal and the refusal messages meet the Definition of Done. The Product Owner releases them straight away rather than waiting for the Sprint Review.

Sprint Review and the next Sprint

At the Sprint Review, branch staff and a group of members try the Increment. They ask for a way to renew everything at once and point out that the refusal message should say when the book will be free. The Product Owner moves "Renew all loans at once" up, adds a new item for the expected-availability date, and places the email confirmation third. The Sprint Retrospective adds "tested against the library system's test environment" to the Definition of Done, because the reservation problem would have been found earlier.

ArtifactStart of Sprint 4End of Sprint 4
Product BacklogSix top items ordered for the Product GoalReordered after feedback; two new items; email confirmation returned
Sprint BacklogSprint Goal, four items, 12 tasksSprint Goal met; three items Done, one returned
IncrementPrevious Increments: login and loans listPlus online renewal, refusal messages and the date fix, all released

How to set up Scrum artifacts, step by step

Use these steps for a new Scrum Team, or to reset artifacts that have drifted. The order is our recommendation; the rules come from the Scrum Guide.

  1. Write the Product Goal. The Product Owner drafts one sentence describing the next future state of the product and shares it with the team and stakeholders.
  2. Create the Product Backlog. Gather known requests, bugs and technical work in one ordered list. Write the top items clearly and keep the rest coarse.
  3. Refine and size the top items. The Developers ask questions, split large items and size them, until roughly one or two Sprints' worth of work is clear.
  4. Agree the Definition of Done. Start from any organisational standard, then add what your product needs. Make it visible on the team board.
  5. Plan the first Sprint. Agree the Sprint Goal, select items and break them into a plan. Record all three parts in one place: the Sprint Backlog.
  6. Make everything visible. Put the Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Definition of Done where the team and stakeholders can see them, on a wall or in a tool.
  7. Update the Sprint Backlog daily. Use the Daily Scrum to inspect it and adjust the plan.
  8. Inspect the Increment and adapt. Show only Done work at the Sprint Review, update the Product Backlog with what you learn, and improve the Definition of Done at the Retrospective.

A physical or digital Scrum board is the simplest way to make the Sprint Backlog visible. Our free product backlog template and sprint planning template cover steps 2 to 5.

Keeping Scrum artifacts transparent

Because transparency is the purpose of every artifact, check it regularly. These signs are our own suggestions rather than Scrum rules.

ArtifactHealthy signWarning sign
Product BacklogOrdered; the Product Owner can explain the top ten items; visible to stakeholdersHundreds of stale items; everything labelled high priority; side lists in email
Product GoalOne clear goal, shown above the backlog and discussed at the Sprint ReviewNo goal, or several competing goals
Sprint BacklogShows the Sprint Goal, items and tasks, and reflects today's realityNot updated for days; work happening that is not on the board
Sprint GoalDevelopers can state it from memory and use it to make trade-offsA list of item numbers rather than an objective
IncrementReleased or releasable; meets the Definition of Done"Done except testing"; demos of work that is not finished
Definition of DoneWritten down, visible and strengthened over timeExists only in people's heads, or is quietly skipped under pressure

Official Scrum artifacts vs other useful tools

Some popular articles list five or seven Scrum artifacts. The Scrum Guide defines three. The rest are complementary practices: often valuable, but optional and not part of Scrum itself.

ItemScrum artifact?What it is for
Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, IncrementYesThe three official artifacts
Product Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of DoneNo: they are commitments attached to the artifactsFocus and a measure of progress for each artifact
Burndown and burnup chartsNoVisualising remaining or completed work over time; see Scrum metrics
VelocityNoForecasting how much work fits in future Sprints; see Scrum velocity
Product visionNoLong-term direction, often a source for Product Goals
Product roadmap and release planNoCommunicating direction and timing to stakeholders; see Agile roadmaps
Definition of ReadyNoAn optional checklist some teams use before Sprint Planning
Scrum boardNoA way to display the Sprint Backlog
Working agreementNoHow the team agrees to work together
Tip

If an exam question asks for the Scrum artifacts, the answer is the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. The Definition of Done is the Increment's commitment, not a fourth artifact.

Common Scrum artifact mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Several backlogs for one productNobody can order work across the productOne Product Backlog per product, with filters or views per team
No Product GoalThe backlog becomes a list of requests rather than a plan for valueAgree one Product Goal and review it at every Sprint Review
Sprint Goal written as a list of itemsDevelopers lose the flexibility to trade scope and still succeedWrite an objective that explains why the Sprint matters
Sprint Backlog owned by a managerThe team stops self-managing and the plan goes staleThe Developers create and update their own plan
Calling unfinished work doneHidden work piles up and forecasts become fictionApply the Definition of Done strictly; return unfinished items to the Product Backlog
Treating the Sprint Review as a release gateValue waits needlesslyRelease Done Increments when it makes sense
Keeping artifacts in private tools or spreadsheetsStakeholders and other teams cannot inspect themMake every artifact visible to everyone who needs it
Technical work kept off the Product BacklogDebt grows unseen and slows every SprintOrder technical items alongside features; see technical debt in Scrum

Frequently asked questions

What are the three Scrum artifacts?

The Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. The Scrum Guide defines no others.

What are the commitments in Scrum?

Each artifact has one: the Product Goal for the Product Backlog, the Sprint Goal for the Sprint Backlog and the Definition of Done for the Increment. They were introduced in the 2020 Scrum Guide to add focus and a way to measure progress.

Why are they called artifacts?

The term comes from software development, where an artifact is something produced or used in building a product. In Scrum, the artifacts hold the information the team and stakeholders need to inspect the product and the work.

Is the Definition of Done a Scrum artifact?

No. It is the commitment attached to the Increment: the formal description of the quality the Increment must meet.

Is a burndown chart a Scrum artifact?

No. Burndown and burnup charts are popular practices for showing progress, but they are not among the Scrum Guide's three artifacts.

Is a product roadmap or product vision a Scrum artifact?

No. Many Scrum Teams use them to communicate direction, and a vision often informs the Product Goal, but the Scrum Guide does not define either.

Who owns the Sprint Backlog?

The Developers. It is a plan by and for them, and they update it throughout the Sprint.

Which Scrum artifact does the Product Owner own?

The Product Backlog. The Product Owner is accountable for its content, order and transparency and for the Product Goal, though others contribute items.

Which Scrum artifact is the most important?

They depend on each other, so none works alone. Many practitioners point to the Increment, because it is the only one that delivers value directly to users.

Can a team create more than one Increment in a Sprint?

Yes. The Scrum Guide says multiple Increments may be created within a Sprint, and an Increment can be delivered to stakeholders before the Sprint ends.

What happens to work that does not meet the Definition of Done?

It is not part of the Increment, cannot be released or presented at the Sprint Review, and returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.

Do multiple Scrum Teams share artifacts?

Teams working on the same product share one Product Backlog, one Product Goal and one Definition of Done. Each team has its own Sprint Backlog and Sprint Goal.

Are there 7 Scrum artifacts?

No. Lists of five or seven usually add items such as a product vision, burndown chart or Definition of Done. The Scrum Guide defines three artifacts, each with one commitment.
