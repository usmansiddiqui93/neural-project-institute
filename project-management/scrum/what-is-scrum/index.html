What Is Scrum? How the Scrum Framework Works, With Examples

Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organisations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. A small team works in fixed-length Sprints of one month or less, delivers a usable Increment each Sprint, then inspects the result with stakeholders and adapts its plan. This guide explains how the Scrum framework fits together according to the 2020 Scrum Guide, walks through a real-looking Sprint, and shows when Scrum fits and how to start.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 4 September 2026
  • 19 min read

Key takeaways

  • Scrum is a lightweight framework, not a full methodology. It defines a small set of accountabilities, events, artifacts and rules, and leaves the detailed practices to the team.
  • It is built on empiricism (transparency, inspection and adaptation) and lean thinking, supported by five values: commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage.
  • The structure is often summarised as 3-5-3: three accountabilities, five events and three artifacts, each artifact with its own commitment.
  • Work happens in Sprints of one month or less. Each Sprint has a Sprint Goal and should produce a usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done.
  • Scrum suits complex work where the solution is uncertain and feedback matters. For continuous, unpredictable request work, Kanban is often a better fit.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide is still the official definition (checked 26 October 2026). Story points, user stories and burndown charts are optional practices, not Scrum rules.

What is Scrum?

Scrum is a lightweight framework for developing and sustaining complex products. A small, self-managing Scrum Team works in short, fixed-length cycles called Sprints, delivers a usable piece of the product every Sprint, and uses regular events to inspect the result and adapt both the product and the plan. Its official definition is the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland.

The 2020 Scrum Guide summarises Scrum in four steps. In our words:

  1. A Product Owner orders the work for a complex problem into a Product Backlog.
  2. The Scrum Team turns a selection of that work into an Increment of value during a Sprint.
  3. The Scrum Team and its stakeholders inspect the results and adjust for the next Sprint.
  4. Repeat.

A Scrum Master fosters the environment that makes this possible.

Framework, not methodology

People often call Scrum a methodology, but the Scrum Guide deliberately calls it a framework and describes it as purposefully incomplete. It tells you which accountabilities, events and artifacts you need and the rules that bind them together. It does not tell you how to write requirements, estimate, test or design. Teams add their own practices inside the framework, which is why two Scrum Teams can look quite different.

The Scrum Guide also says that Scrum exists only in its entirety. You can use parts of it, but if you drop an event or an accountability, the result is not Scrum, and you usually lose the feedback loop that makes it work.

Tip

Scrum in one sentence: a small team builds something usable in short cycles, shows it to the people who care, and uses what it learns to decide what to do next.

Scrum in simple terms: an everyday example

Imagine a small team asked to rebuild a museum's website. A traditional plan would specify every page up front and launch everything after six months. With Scrum, the team agrees a goal for the first two weeks, such as "Visitors can see opening times and buy a ticket online." At the end of those two weeks there is a working page to show the museum's staff. The staff point out that most visitors are on phones and that school groups need a separate booking route. The team adjusts the plan, and the next two weeks start from what everyone has learned rather than from the original guess.

That is the core of Scrum: short cycles, a clear goal for each one, something real to inspect at the end and permission to change direction based on evidence.

The theory behind Scrum: empiricism and lean thinking

According to the Scrum Guide, Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking. Empiricism means knowledge comes from experience and decisions are based on what is observed. Lean thinking means reducing waste and focusing on the essentials. Scrum applies them through an iterative, incremental approach to improve predictability and control risk.

The three pillars

PillarWhat it meansWhat it looks like in Scrum
TransparencyThe work and the process are visible to those doing the work and those receiving itA visible Product Backlog and Sprint Backlog; an honest Definition of Done
InspectionArtifacts and progress toward goals are inspected frequently to spot problemsDaily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective
AdaptationIf something deviates outside acceptable limits, adjust as soon as possibleRe-plan the Sprint, re-order the Product Backlog, change how the team works

The five Scrum values

ValueWhat it meansIn practice
CommitmentThe team commits to its goals and to supporting each otherWorking together to reach the Sprint Goal rather than finishing individual tasks
FocusAttention on the work of the Sprint and progress toward the goalsFinishing items before starting new ones
OpennessBeing open about the work and the challengesRaising a risk at the Daily Scrum instead of hiding it until the Review
RespectRespecting each other as capable, independent peopleLetting the Developers decide how to do the work
CourageDoing the right thing and working on tough problemsTelling stakeholders that a feature is not Done yet

The pillars only work when people feel safe to share honest information, which is why the values matter.

The Scrum framework at a glance: 3-5-3

A popular memory aid summarises Scrum as 3-5-3: three accountabilities, five events and three artifacts. Add the three commitments and you have every formal element of the framework.

ElementPartsPurpose
3 accountabilitiesProduct Owner, Scrum Master, DevelopersMake clear who is accountable for value, effectiveness and the Increment
5 eventsThe Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint RetrospectiveCreate regular points to inspect and adapt
3 artifactsProduct Backlog, Sprint Backlog, IncrementMake the work and its progress transparent
3 commitmentsProduct Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of DoneGive each artifact a focus to measure progress against

The Scrum Team and its three accountabilities

The Scrum Team is a small team of one Product Owner, one Scrum Master and Developers. The Scrum Guide describes it as cross-functional (between them, members have all the skills needed to create value each Sprint) and self-managing (they decide internally who does what, when and how). It is typically 10 or fewer people and has no sub-teams or hierarchies. If a team grows too large, it should split into several cohesive Scrum Teams that share one Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product Owner.

AccountabilityAccountable forTypical workDoes not
Product OwnerMaximising the value of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's workSets the Product Goal, orders the Product Backlog, works with stakeholders, decides what to releaseTell the Developers how to do the work
Scrum MasterEstablishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide and the Scrum Team's effectivenessCoaches, facilitates, causes impediments to be removed, helps the organisation adopt ScrumAssign tasks or act as the team's manager
DevelopersCreating any aspect of a usable Increment each SprintPlan the Sprint, build and test the work, meet the Definition of Done, adapt their plan dailyWork from tasks handed down by others

The 2020 Scrum Guide uses the word accountabilities rather than roles, and "Developers" covers anyone doing the work, whether they are engineers, designers, writers or analysts. Scrum does not define a project manager; project management work is shared between the three accountabilities.

The five Scrum events

Scrum uses five events. Each is a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt, and holding them at regular times reduces the need for other meetings. Time-boxes below are maximums for a one-month Sprint; shorter Sprints usually have shorter events.

EventPurposeTime-boxMain output
The SprintContainer for all other events; where ideas are turned into valueOne month or less, fixed lengthA usable Increment
Sprint PlanningDecide why the Sprint is valuable, what can be Done and howUp to 8 hoursSprint Goal and Sprint Backlog
Daily ScrumDevelopers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan15 minutes, every working dayAn actionable plan for the next day
Sprint ReviewInspect the outcome with stakeholders and decide what to do nextUp to 4 hoursA revised Product Backlog
Sprint RetrospectiveInspect how the last Sprint went and plan improvementsUp to 3 hoursImprovements, the most important often added to the next Sprint Backlog

Backlog refinement is an ongoing activity, not a sixth event. Many people call the events "Scrum ceremonies"; the Scrum Guide does not use that term.

The three Scrum artifacts and their commitments

Scrum's artifacts represent work or value, and are designed to make key information transparent. Since 2020, each artifact has a commitment that gives it focus.

ArtifactWhat it isAccountableCommitment
Product BacklogAn emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product; the single source of workProduct OwnerProduct Goal: the long-term objective the team plans against
Sprint BacklogThe Sprint Goal, the items selected for the Sprint and the Developers' planDevelopersSprint Goal: the single objective for the Sprint
IncrementA concrete, usable stepping stone toward the Product GoalDevelopers create itDefinition of Done: the quality standard the Increment must meet

Work that does not meet the Definition of Done is not part of the Increment and is not shown at the Sprint Review as finished. Several Increments can be created and released within one Sprint.

How Scrum works: a Sprint step by step

Every Sprint follows the same rhythm, and the next Sprint starts as soon as the previous one ends.

  1. Prepare the Product Backlog. The Product Owner keeps it ordered toward the Product Goal, and the team refines the top items until they are small and clear enough to be Done within a Sprint.
  2. Plan the Sprint. In Sprint Planning the Scrum Team agrees a Sprint Goal, the Developers select items they forecast they can finish, and they plan the work. Output: the Sprint Backlog.
  3. Build and inspect daily. The Developers work toward the Sprint Goal and hold a 15-minute Daily Scrum each working day to inspect progress and adapt their plan.
  4. Finish to the Definition of Done. Each item becomes part of the Increment only when it meets the Definition of Done. It can be released to users as soon as it is ready.
  5. Review the outcome. At the Sprint Review the Scrum Team shows stakeholders what was achieved, discusses what changed in the market or organisation, and updates the Product Backlog.
  6. Improve the way of working. In the Sprint Retrospective the team decides on the most helpful changes to its process, tools, relationships or Definition of Done.
  7. Start the next Sprint straight away, from the updated backlog.

For timings across a whole Sprint, see the worked example below.

Worked example: one Sprint in a real-looking team

A regional chain of gyms has a Scrum Team building its class-booking app: a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and five Developers (three engineers, a designer and a tester). Sprints last two weeks. The Product Goal is "Members can book, cancel and join waiting lists for classes from their phone."

Sprint Planning (day 1, about two hours). Popular classes fill up within minutes, and members phone the front desk to ask about cancellations. The Product Owner proposes tackling this. The team agrees the Sprint Goal "Members can join a waiting list for a full class and are told when a place becomes free." Based on recent Sprints and one Developer's leave, the Developers select four items and plan the first few days in detail.

DayWhat happenedScrum in action
1Sprint Planning; work starts on "Join a waiting list"Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog created
3"Join a waiting list" meets the Definition of DoneFirst part of the Increment finished
5Developers find that push notifications need an app store review that will take longer than the SprintRaised at the Daily Scrum
5Developers and Product Owner agree to notify members by email this Sprint and move push notifications back to the Product BacklogScope renegotiated; Sprint Goal unchanged
8Waiting lists released to one pilot gymIncrement released before the Sprint ends
10Sprint Review with two gym managers and the membership leadFeedback: cap waiting lists at ten people; add "leave the waiting list"
10Sprint RetrospectiveAction: automate the release checklist that caused a late-night fix on day 8

Result. The Sprint Goal was met, members at the pilot gym can join waiting lists, and the Product Backlog now has two new items near the top and push notifications re-ordered. The team did not deliver everything it originally selected, but it delivered what mattered and learned something that changed the plan. That is a successful Sprint.

What Scrum requires and what it leaves to you

Much of what people associate with Scrum is not in the Scrum Guide. These complementary practices can be very useful, but they are choices you can change, not rules.

Defined by the Scrum GuideCommon complementary practices
Product Backlog itemsUser stories, epics, acceptance criteria
Sizing by the DevelopersStory points, planning poker, T-shirt sizes
Monitoring progress toward goalsVelocity, burndown and burnup charts
Transparency of the Sprint BacklogPhysical or digital Scrum boards
Daily Scrum focused on the Sprint GoalThe three stand-up questions, walking the board
Items ready for selection when they can be Done within one SprintA Definition of Ready

Use the Retrospective to decide which ones earn their place in your team.

Benefits and limitations of Scrum

Scrum's benefits come from short feedback loops and clear accountability. Its limitations usually appear when an organisation adopts the events without the conditions Scrum needs.

BenefitsLimitations and challenges
Usable results every Sprint, so value and risk are visible earlyNeeds a Product Owner with real authority and time to order the backlog
Regular stakeholder feedback reduces the risk of building the wrong thingNeeds a stable, dedicated team; part-time members weaken focus
Short planning horizons make change cheaperFixed-date, fixed-scope contracts sit uneasily with emergent scope
Clear accountabilities and a visible backlog improve transparencyEvents can feel like overhead if they are run as status meetings
Retrospectives build continuous improvement into the workSays little about scaling, budgeting or architecture; you must add practices
Self-managing teams often report higher ownershipExposes organisational problems quickly, which can be uncomfortable

When to use Scrum (and when another approach fits better)

Scrum was designed for complex work, where more is unknown than known and the best solution emerges through experiment. Use the table as a quick fit check. These are our suggestions, not rules from the Scrum Guide.

SituationScrum fitWhy
New product or major feature with uncertain requirementsStrongShort cycles and reviews let you learn what users really need
Digital, marketing or research work that can be delivered in usable piecesStrongEach Sprint produces something to test or release
A dedicated, cross-functional team of up to about 10 peopleStrongMatches the Scrum Team design
Support or operations work with unpredictable incoming requestsWeakSprint commitments are disrupted daily; Kanban usually fits better
Repeatable work with a known solutionWeakLittle to learn each Sprint; a simple plan or checklist is enough
Construction or manufacturing with fixed sequences and long lead timesMixedUse Scrum for design and planning work, a predictive plan for physical delivery
Very large programmes with many teamsPossibleNeeds a scaling approach and one Product Backlog per product

Scrum vs Agile, Kanban and Waterfall

Scrum is often confused with Agile and compared with Kanban and Waterfall. The short version:

ScrumAgileKanbanWaterfall
What it isA lightweight frameworkA set of values and principles (2001 Manifesto)A method for managing flowA sequential, plan-driven approach
CadenceFixed Sprints of one month or lessNot prescribedContinuous flowPhases, one after another
RolesProduct Owner, Scrum Master, DevelopersNot prescribedNone requiredTypically a project manager and functional teams
ChangeWelcomed between Sprints; scope negotiable within a SprintWelcomedAny time, as capacity frees upThrough formal change control
Key measureA Done Increment toward the Product GoalWorking software or outcomesCycle time, throughput, WIPProgress against the baseline plan

Scrum is one way to be Agile: both Scrum co-creators signed the Agile Manifesto. For detail, see Agile vs Scrum, Kanban vs Scrum and Scrum vs Waterfall.

How to start using Scrum, step by step

A practical sequence for a team adopting Scrum for the first time:

  1. Read the Scrum Guide together. It is free and short. Agree that you will try Scrum as written before changing it.
  2. Fill the three accountabilities. Choose one Product Owner with authority over the backlog, one Scrum Master and a dedicated group of Developers with the skills to finish the work.
  3. Agree a Product Goal that describes the outcome you are working toward next.
  4. Build an initial Product Backlog, ordered, with the top items small enough to finish within a Sprint. Our product backlog template helps.
  5. Write a first Definition of Done. Keep it realistic: what must be true for work to be usable?
  6. Choose a Sprint length. Two weeks is a common starting point. Keep it consistent.
  7. Schedule the events for the first few Sprints so they become a habit: Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective.
  8. Run the first Sprint and expect it to be imperfect. Invite real stakeholders to the Sprint Review.
  9. Use Retrospectives to improve, one or two changes at a time. Add complementary practices only when they solve a problem you have seen.
Tip

Start with one team and one product. Scaling Scrum before a single team can deliver a Done Increment every Sprint usually multiplies the problems.

Common Scrum myths and mistakes

Myth or mistakeReality
Scrum is a project management methodologyIt is a framework for developing products; it leaves most practices to the team
The Scrum Master is the team's managerThe Scrum Master serves the team and organisation and has no authority to assign work
Scrum means no planningPlanning happens at every level: Product Goal, Sprint Planning and every Daily Scrum
Scrum means no documentationWrite what is needed for the Increment to be usable; include it in the Definition of Done
The Daily Scrum is a status report to the managerIt is the Developers' planning event, focused on the Sprint Goal
Story points and velocity are part of ScrumThey are optional practices; the Scrum Guide does not mention them
A Sprint can be extended to finish the workSprints have a fixed length; unfinished work returns to the Product Backlog
Scrum is only for softwareIt is used for marketing, research, product design, education and more

Scaling Scrum and getting certified

When several teams work on one product, they share one Product Backlog, one Product Goal and one Product Owner, and coordinate through practices such as a Scrum of Scrums or frameworks like Nexus, LeSS, Scrum@Scale and SAFe. Start with our guide to Scrum at scale.

Certification is not required to use Scrum, but it can help in a job search. The most common entry certificates are Professional Scrum Master (PSM I) from Scrum.org and Certified ScrumMaster (CSM) from the Scrum Alliance, with equivalents for Product Owners.

Frequently asked questions

What is Scrum in simple terms?

Scrum is a way for a small team to tackle complex work in short, fixed-length cycles called Sprints. Each Sprint the team delivers something usable, gets feedback and uses what it learned to plan the next Sprint.

Is Scrum a methodology or a framework?

The Scrum Guide calls it a framework. It is deliberately incomplete: it defines the accountabilities, events, artifacts and rules, and leaves the detailed practices to the team.

What are the 3-5-3 of Scrum?

A memory aid for three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), five events (the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) and three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment).

What are the three pillars of Scrum?

Transparency, inspection and adaptation. Together they put empiricism into practice.

What are the five Scrum values?

Commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage.

Who created Scrum?

Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. They presented Scrum together in 1995 and co-author the official Scrum Guide.

Why is it called Scrum?

The name comes from the scrum in rugby, where the whole team works together to move the ball forward. Takeuchi and Nonaka used the analogy in their 1986 Harvard Business Review article on product development.

How long is a Sprint?

One month or less, and the length stays consistent. Two weeks is a common choice.

How big is a Scrum Team?

The Scrum Guide says typically 10 or fewer people, including the Product Owner and Scrum Master. Larger groups should split into several Scrum Teams working on the same product.

Is Scrum only for software?

No. Scrum was designed for complex product work and is used in marketing, research, education, hardware and public services as well as software.

Does Scrum have a project manager?

Scrum does not define a project manager. Project management work is shared between the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the self-managing Developers.

What is the difference between Agile and Scrum?

Agile is a set of values and principles for working adaptively. Scrum is one specific framework for putting them into practice, with defined accountabilities, events and artifacts.

Is there a newer Scrum Guide than 2020?

No. The November 2020 Scrum Guide is still the official version (checked 26 October 2026). The Scrum Guide Expansion Pack published in June 2025 is a supplement and does not replace it.

What are the disadvantages of Scrum?

It needs a committed Product Owner and a stable, dedicated team, it can feel heavy if events become status meetings, and it fits poorly with unpredictable support work or fixed-scope contracts.
