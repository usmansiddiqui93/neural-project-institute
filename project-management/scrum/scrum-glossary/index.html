Scrum Glossary: 120+ Scrum Terms Defined A to Z

This Scrum glossary defines more than 120 Scrum terms in plain English, from accountability to working agreement, and marks the source of each one. Official terms follow the 2020 Scrum Guide, which is still the current version, so you can tell Scrum rules from common practices, related frameworks and retired wording. Find a term in the A to Z tables, then use the commonly confused terms and the worked Sprint example to see how the vocabulary fits together.

Key takeaways

  • Official Scrum vocabulary comes from the 2020 Scrum Guide: three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts and three commitments, built on three pillars and five values.
  • Story points, velocity, user stories, epics, burndown charts and the Definition of Ready are widely used practices, not Scrum Guide rules.
  • Several terms have been retired: "Development Team" became Developers, "roles" became accountabilities, "self-organising" became self-managing and "grooming" became refinement.
  • The most commonly confused pairs are Sprint Goal and Product Goal, Definition of Done and acceptance criteria, Done and released, and forecast and commitment.
  • The 2025 Scrum Guide Expansion Pack is an optional supplement; it does not replace the 2020 Scrum Guide.
  • Agree a short team glossary, especially for "Done", "ready" and how you size and forecast work.

What is a Scrum glossary?

A Scrum glossary is a reference list of the terms used in Scrum, each with a short, plain definition and its source. It covers the framework's accountabilities, events, artifacts, commitments, pillars and values, plus the practices teams commonly add, so that everyone on a Scrum Team and its stakeholders use the same words in the same way.

Shared vocabulary matters because Scrum depends on transparency: if one person's "Done" means coded and another's means deployable, every forecast becomes unreliable.

Every table below has a Source column:

  • Scrum Guide: defined or used in the 2020 Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. Scrum Guide terms are usually capitalised, such as Sprint, Product Owner and Increment.
  • Practice: widely used with Scrum but not required by it. Use, adapt or drop these as your team sees fit.
  • Scrum.org, Kanban, XP or Scaling: terms from Scrum.org's own glossary, from Kanban or Extreme Programming, or from frameworks for many teams.
  • Retired: older Scrum wording you will still hear but which the current Scrum Guide no longer uses.

For wider Agile, Kanban, XP and SAFe vocabulary, use our Agile glossary. See what Scrum is for how the framework fits together.

Scrum terms A to B

A

TermDefinitionSource
Acceptance criteriaConditions one specific backlog item must meet to be accepted. Unlike the Definition of Done, they differ for each item.Practice
AccountabilityThe 2020 Scrum Guide's word for the three areas of responsibility in a Scrum Team: Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. See Scrum team roles.Scrum Guide
AdaptationAdjusting the process or the product as soon as possible when inspection shows it has drifted outside acceptable limits. One of the three pillars of empiricism.Scrum Guide
AgileA way of working based on the 2001 Agile Manifesto: short cycles, frequent feedback, adaptable plans. Scrum is one Agile framework.Agile Manifesto
ArtifactSomething that represents work or value. Scrum has three: the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. See Scrum artifacts.Scrum Guide

B

TermDefinitionSource
Backlog groomingThe older name for Product Backlog refinement, replaced in the 2013 Scrum Guide.Retired
BlockerAnything that stops one item progressing, such as a missing decision. Scrum's wider term is impediment.Practice
Bug (defect)A flaw in the product, usually added to the Product Backlog and ordered like any other item.Practice
Burndown chartA chart of work remaining against time, sloping towards zero. The Scrum Guide names burn-downs as one forecasting practice but does not require them.Practice
Burnup chartA chart of work completed rising over time against a separate total-scope line, so scope changes stay visible. Also named in the Scrum Guide as an optional practice.Practice

Scrum terms C

C

TermDefinitionSource
Cancellation (Sprint)Ending a Sprint early because its Sprint Goal has become obsolete. Only the Product Owner has the authority to cancel a Sprint.Scrum Guide
CapacityHow much time the Developers realistically have in a Sprint after leave, holidays, events and support work.Practice
CeremonyAn informal name for a Scrum event. The Scrum Guide says event, not ceremony. See Scrum ceremonies.Practice
Chickens and pigsAn old fable separating people committed to the work from those merely involved. No longer part of the Scrum Guide.Retired
CoherenceProduct Backlog items that together form a meaningful whole, such as items serving one Sprint Goal.Scrum.org
Commitment (artifact)The element that gives each artifact focus: the Product Goal for the Product Backlog, the Sprint Goal for the Sprint Backlog and the Definition of Done for the Increment. Added in 2020.Scrum Guide
Commitment (value)Scrum Team members commit to achieving their goals and to supporting each other. One of the five Scrum values.Scrum Guide
CourageDoing the right thing and working on tough problems. A Scrum value.Scrum Guide
Cross-functionalHaving all the skills needed to create value each Sprint without depending on people outside the team.Scrum Guide
Cumulative flow diagram (CFD)A stacked chart of items in each workflow state over time; named in the Scrum Guide as an optional forecasting practice.Kanban
Cycle timeElapsed time from starting work on an item to finishing it.Kanban

Scrum terms D to E

D

TermDefinitionSource
Daily ScrumA 15-minute event, at the same time and place each working day, in which the Developers inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan. See the Daily Scrum.Scrum Guide
Daily stand-upA general name for a short daily team check-in. Scrum's version is the Daily Scrum.Practice
Definition of Done (DoD)A formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product. It is the commitment for the Increment. See the Definition of Done.Scrum Guide
Definition of Ready (DoR)An optional checklist for deciding when an item is clear enough to bring into Sprint Planning. Not part of the Scrum Guide.Practice
DemoInformal name for showing work at the Sprint Review, which the Scrum Guide says should be a working session, not just a presentation.Practice
DependencyA relationship where one item or team needs something from another before work can finish.Practice
DevelopersScrum Team members who create any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint, including testers, designers, analysts and writers.Scrum Guide
Development TeamThe pre-2020 name for the Developers as a team inside the Scrum Team. Replaced in 2020 by one Scrum Team with Developers.Retired
DoneMeeting the Definition of Done. Work that is not Done cannot be part of an Increment, released or presented at the Sprint Review.Scrum Guide

E

TermDefinitionSource
EmergenceNew facts or requirements becoming visible unexpectedly as work progresses.Scrum.org
EmpiricismMaking decisions based on what is known and observed. Scrum's pillars of empiricism are transparency, inspection and adaptation. See empiricism in Scrum.Scrum Guide
EpicA large body of work that is split into smaller Product Backlog items.Practice
EventA formal opportunity to inspect and adapt. Scrum has five: the Sprint, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective.Scrum Guide
Evidence-Based Management (EBM)Scrum.org's framework for measuring value across current value, unrealised value, time to market and ability to innovate.Scrum.org
Expansion PackThe Scrum Guide Expansion Pack, published in June 2025 by Ralph Jocham, John Coleman and Jeff Sutherland as an optional supplement. It does not replace the 2020 Scrum Guide.Supplement

Scrum terms F to O

F

TermDefinitionSource
FocusConcentrating on the work of the Sprint to make the best possible progress towards the goals. A Scrum value.Scrum Guide
Focus factorVelocity divided by available person-days; a planning aid, never a performance score.Practice
ForecastThe Developers' prediction of what they can get Done in a Sprint. The 2011 Scrum Guide replaced "commit" with "forecast" for selected work; the commitment is the Sprint Goal.Scrum Guide

H to I

TermDefinitionSource
Hardening SprintA Sprint reserved for testing and fixing before release. Not part of Scrum; it usually signals a weak Definition of Done.Practice
ImpedimentAnything slowing the Scrum Team's progress. The Scrum Master is accountable for causing impediments to be removed.Scrum Guide
IncrementA concrete, usable stepping stone towards the Product Goal that meets the Definition of Done. A Sprint can create several. See Increment in Scrum.Scrum Guide
InspectionFrequently and diligently checking artifacts and progress to spot undesirable variances. A pillar of empiricism.Scrum Guide
INVESTBill Wake's checklist for good user stories: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.Practice
IterationThe general Agile word for a fixed, repeated development cycle. In Scrum, the Sprint.Practice

K to L

TermDefinitionSource
KanbanA strategy for optimising flow by visualising work, limiting work in progress and managing flow. Often combined with Scrum. See Kanban vs Scrum.Kanban
Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS)A framework for two to eight teams working on one product with one Product Owner and one Product Backlog; LeSS Huge covers more.Scaling
Lean thinkingReducing waste and focusing on the essentials. The Scrum Guide names it, with empiricism, as a foundation of Scrum.Scrum Guide

M to O

TermDefinitionSource
Minimum viable product (MVP)The smallest version of a product that lets a team test a key assumption with real users.Practice
Monte Carlo forecastingSimulating many futures from past throughput to give a probabilistic forecast.Practice
NexusScrum.org's framework for three to nine Scrum Teams working on a single product, with a Nexus Integration Team.Scaling
OpennessBeing open about the work and its challenges. A Scrum value.Scrum Guide
OrderingArranging the Product Backlog in sequence. The Scrum Guide says ordered, not prioritised, because value, risk and dependencies all matter.Scrum Guide

Scrum terms P to R

P

TermDefinitionSource
PillarsThe three pillars of empiricism: transparency, inspection and adaptation.Scrum Guide
Planning PokerConsensus estimation in which everyone reveals an estimate card at once, then the highest and lowest explain their reasoning.Practice
ProductA vehicle to deliver value, with a clear boundary, known stakeholders and well-defined users or customers.Scrum Guide
Product BacklogAn emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product; the single source of work for the Scrum Team. See the Product Backlog.Scrum Guide
Product Backlog item (PBI)A single entry in the Product Backlog, such as a feature, fix, technical improvement or experiment.Scrum Guide
Product Backlog refinementBreaking down and adding detail, such as description, order and size, to Product Backlog items. An ongoing activity, not an event. See backlog refinement.Scrum Guide
Product GoalThe future state of the product the Scrum Team plans against; the commitment for the Product Backlog. Added in 2020.Scrum Guide
Product OwnerOne person, not a committee, accountable for maximising the value of the product and for effective Product Backlog management. See the Product Owner.Scrum Guide
Product Owner proxySomeone standing in for an absent Product Owner; it weakens decisions if the proxy cannot actually decide.Practice

R

TermDefinitionSource
ReadyIn the Scrum Guide, items that can be Done within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint Planning. Stricter checklists are the Definition of Ready.Scrum Guide
RefinementShort for Product Backlog refinement.Scrum Guide
ReleaseMaking an Increment available to users. It can happen at any time in a Sprint; the Sprint Review is never a gate to releasing value.Scrum Guide
Release planA forecast of when groups of features are likely to be ready, usually shown as a range.Practice
RespectRespecting each other as capable, independent people. A Scrum value.Scrum Guide
Retrospective formatsStructured Retrospective activities, such as Start, Stop, Continue.Practice
RolesThe pre-2020 word for Product Owner, Scrum Master and Development Team. Replaced by accountabilities.Retired

S

ScrumA lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organisations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems.Scrum Guide
Scrum AllianceA membership and certification body that issues the CSM, CSPO and related certifications.Certification
Scrum boardA visual board showing Sprint Backlog items and their status, physical or in a tool.Practice
Scrum GuideThe official definition of Scrum by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, free online. The current version was published in November 2020.Scrum Guide
Scrum MasterAccountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. See the Scrum Master role.Scrum Guide
Scrum of ScrumsA coordination meeting for representatives of several Scrum Teams working on related products.Scaling

Scrum valuesCommitment, focus, openness, respect and courage; added to the Scrum Guide in 2016. See Scrum principles and values.Scrum Guide
Scrum@ScaleJeff Sutherland's framework for scaling Scrum through networks of Scrum Teams.Scaling

Self-organisingThe pre-2020 description of how the Development Team chose how to do its work.Retired
Servant-leaderThe 2017 description of the Scrum Master. The 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum Masters are true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organisation.Retired
SpikeA time-boxed piece of research to reduce uncertainty before estimating or building. Originally an XP term.Practice
SprintA fixed-length event of one month or less that contains all the other events. A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one ends. See the Scrum Sprint cycle.Scrum Guide
Sprint BacklogThe Sprint Goal (why), the selected Product Backlog items (what) and an actionable plan (how). A plan by and for the Developers. See the Sprint Backlog.Scrum Guide
Sprint GoalThe single objective for the Sprint, created during Sprint Planning; the commitment for the Sprint Backlog.Scrum Guide
Sprint lengthThe fixed duration of a Sprint: one month or less, kept consistent. Two weeks is a common choice.Scrum Guide
Sprint PlanningThe event that starts the Sprint and addresses why it is valuable, what can be Done and how. See Sprint Planning.Scrum Guide
Sprint RetrospectiveThe event that concludes the Sprint, used to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness. See the Sprint Retrospective.Scrum Guide
Sprint ReviewThe second-to-last event, in which the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the outcome of the Sprint and decide future adaptations. See the Sprint Review.Scrum Guide
Sprint zeroAn informal set-up period before the first Sprint. Not a Scrum term.Practice
StakeholderAnyone outside the Scrum Team with an interest in the product, such as customers, users, sponsors and managers.Scrum Guide
Story mappingArranging user stories along a user journey to plan releases; associated with Jeff Patton.Practice
Story pointsRelative units for sizing backlog items. Not mentioned in the Scrum Guide. See story points in Scrum.Practice
SwarmingSeveral team members working together to finish one item before starting another.Practice

The Scrum Guide at a glance

The 2020 Scrum Guide is deliberately short, and most of its vocabulary fits in one table.

ElementScrum Guide termsCommitment or time-box
TheoryEmpiricism and lean thinking; pillars of transparency, inspection and adaptation—
ValuesCommitment, focus, openness, respect, courage—
AccountabilitiesProduct Owner, Scrum Master, Developers (one Scrum Team, typically 10 or fewer)—
EventsSprint; Sprint Planning; Daily Scrum; Sprint Review; Sprint RetrospectiveOne month or less; 8 hours; 15 minutes; 4 hours; 3 hours (maximums for a one-month Sprint)

Tip

If a term is not in the Scrum Guide, it is not a Scrum rule. Story points and burndown charts help many teams, but nobody is doing Scrum "wrong" without them.

Commonly confused Scrum terms

These pairs cause the most misunderstandings in Scrum Teams. The numbers are illustrative.

Sprint Goal vs Product Goal

A team building a patient booking service has the Product Goal "Patients can manage all their appointments online by spring". It will take many Sprints. The Sprint Goal for one two-week Sprint is narrower: "Patients can cancel a booking online". The Product Goal guides ordering of the Product Backlog; the Sprint Goal guides daily decisions.

Definition of Done vs acceptance criteria

TermApplies toExampleIn the Scrum Guide?
Definition of DoneEvery item and the whole IncrementCode reviewed, automated tests passing, accessibility checked, deployed to stagingYes
Acceptance criteriaOne itemCancellation is blocked within 24 hours of the appointmentNo
Definition of ReadyItems before Sprint PlanningHas acceptance criteria and fits in one SprintNo

Done vs released

Done means the work meets the Definition of Done and is usable. Released means users can actually get it. A team may finish five Done items in a Sprint, release two mid-Sprint and hold three behind a feature flag until marketing is ready. All five are part of the Increment.

Forecast vs commitment

The Developers forecast that eight items, about 24 story points, fit in the Sprint. They commit to the Sprint Goal. If one item proves bigger than expected, they renegotiate scope with the Product Owner rather than abandoning the goal or cutting quality.

Worked example: Scrum terms in one Sprint

Follow one illustrative two-week Sprint for a seven-person Scrum Team building that booking service. Every term in bold is in the glossary above.

  1. Product Goal. The Product Owner keeps the Product Backlog ordered towards the Product Goal: patients manage their appointments online.
  2. Refinement. Mid-Sprint, the team splits an epic into user stories that are vertical slices, adds acceptance criteria in a Three Amigos conversation and sizes them with Planning Poker in story points.
  3. Sprint Planning. Using recent velocity of 22–26 points and reduced capacity for a public holiday, the whole Scrum Team agrees the Sprint Goal. The Developers forecast eight items and break them into tasks, forming the Sprint Backlog.
  4. During the Sprint. At each 15-minute Daily Scrum, the Developers check progress towards the Sprint Goal. One item's work item age is unusually high, so two people swarm on it. A third-party API change is an impediment the Scrum Master helps remove.
  5. Done. Items meet the Definition of Done, including TDD and code review, and together form a usable Increment. Two items are released mid-Sprint.
  6. Review and Retrospective. At the Sprint Review, stakeholders try the cancellation flow and their feedback reorders the Product Backlog. At the Sprint Retrospective, the team adds accessibility checks to the Definition of Done. The next Sprint starts immediately.

How to build a Scrum glossary for your team

A one-page team glossary prevents most terminology arguments. Build it in a 30-minute session, then keep it alive.

  1. List the terms people actually use, including tool labels, abbreviations and local jargon from your board and events.
  2. Choose a source of truth. Use the 2020 Scrum Guide for Scrum terms and this glossary or the Agile glossary for practices.
  3. Agree the high-risk definitions first: your Definition of Done, what "ready" means, how you size work and what counts towards velocity.
  4. Mark local terms so new joiners do not mistake team conventions for Scrum rules.
  5. Publish it next to your working agreement and Product Backlog, and link it from onboarding notes.
  6. Review it at a Sprint Retrospective every few months, or whenever a misunderstanding causes rework.

Common mistakes with Scrum terminology

  • Using retired terms such as "Development Team", "grooming", "roles" or "self-organising" in new training or tools.
  • Treating practices as rules. Story points, velocity, user stories and the Definition of Ready are optional.
  • Calling the Sprint Review a demo. It is a working session about what to do next, not a presentation.
  • Saying "prioritised" instead of "ordered", then arguing over which items are "all priority one".
  • Confusing Done with accepted or released, which hides unfinished work inside the Increment.
  • Comparing velocity between teams. Story points are relative to each team, so the numbers do not compare.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important Scrum terms for beginners?

Start with Scrum Team, Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers, Sprint, Sprint Goal, Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment, Definition of Done, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective.

What is 3-5-3 in Scrum?

A shorthand for Scrum's three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), five events (Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) and three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment).

Where are official Scrum terms defined?

In the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. The current version was published in November 2020 and is free online.

What changed in the 2020 Scrum Guide terminology?

It added the Product Goal and the three commitments, replaced roles with accountabilities and the Development Team with Developers, replaced self-organising with self-managing, and dropped the three Daily Scrum questions.

Is the Scrum Guide Expansion Pack a new Scrum Guide?

No. Published in June 2025 by Ralph Jocham, John Coleman and Jeff Sutherland, it is an optional supplement. The 2020 Scrum Guide remains the official definition of Scrum.

What is a time-box in Scrum?

A fixed maximum duration for an event. The Sprint is one month or less and the Daily Scrum 15 minutes. For a one-month Sprint, Sprint Planning is up to 8 hours, the Sprint Review 4 and the Retrospective 3; shorter Sprints usually need less.

What is the difference between the Sprint Goal and the Product Goal?

The Sprint Goal is the single objective for one Sprint. The Product Goal is the longer-term objective the Scrum Team works towards across many Sprints.

Are story points and velocity official Scrum terms?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide mentions neither. They are popular practices for sizing and forecasting work.

Is the Definition of Ready part of Scrum?

No. The Scrum Guide only says items that can be Done within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection. A Definition of Ready is an optional team checklist.

What is the difference between a Scrum Master and a project manager?

A Scrum Master coaches the team and organisation in Scrum and removes impediments; they do not plan or assign the Developers' work. Scrum has no project manager accountability.

Which Scrum terms matter most for PSM I and CSM exams?

Both are based on the Scrum Guide, so focus on its terms: accountabilities, events, artifacts, commitments, pillars, values and time-boxes. Practices such as story points are rarely what the questions test.
