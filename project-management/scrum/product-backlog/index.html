Product Backlog: What It Is and How to Manage It in Scrum

The product backlog is an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve a product, and the single source of work for the Scrum Team. The Product Owner is accountable for it, and its commitment is the Product Goal. This guide explains what the Scrum Guide says, what goes into a backlog, and how to build, order and refine one step by step, with worked examples, prioritisation techniques and the mistakes to avoid.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • Last updated 25 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • The product backlog is an emergent, ordered list and the single source of work for the Scrum Team. There is one backlog per product.
  • Its commitment is the Product Goal: a future state of the product that the team plans against, one goal at a time.
  • The Product Owner is accountable for the content and order; the Developers who will do the work size the items.
  • Items near the top are small, clear and ready for Sprint Planning; items lower down stay coarse until they are needed.
  • Order by value, risk, dependencies and learning, using techniques such as MoSCoW or WSJF as inputs, not as a replacement for judgement.
  • Keep the backlog healthy: refine continuously, delete items that no longer serve the Product Goal and avoid a list that only grows.

What is a product backlog?

A product backlog is an ordered list of everything the Scrum Team currently believes is needed to improve a product: features, fixes, technical work, research and experiments. The 2020 Scrum Guide calls it "an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product" and the single source of work undertaken by the Scrum Team.

The product backlog is one of the three Scrum artifacts, alongside the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. Each artifact has a commitment that gives it focus; for the product backlog, that commitment is the Product Goal.

Four characteristics set it apart from an ordinary to-do list:

  • Emergent. It is never complete. Items are added, changed, split and removed as the team, users and stakeholders learn.
  • Ordered. Every item has a position relative to every other item, so everyone can see what comes next.
  • The single source of work. If the Scrum Team is going to work on something, it should be visible in the product backlog, not hidden in emails or side lists.
  • One per product. Several Scrum Teams working on the same product share one product backlog and one Product Owner.
Tip

The product backlog describes the product, not a project. It lives as long as the product does.

What the Scrum Guide says about the product backlog

The Scrum Guide is short, and it says less about the product backlog than many people assume. The 2020 edition is still the current official version (checked 25 October 2026). The table summarises its main points and what each one means in practice.

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means for your team
An emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the productExpect constant change, and keep one clear sequence rather than a pile of labels
The single source of work undertaken by the Scrum TeamWork outside the backlog is invisible; bring it in
Its commitment is the Product GoalEvery item should help reach the current Product Goal, or be questioned
Refinement breaks items down and adds detail such as a description, order and sizeRefinement is an ongoing activity, not a formal Scrum event
The Developers who will be doing the work are responsible for the sizingThe Product Owner can explain trade-offs but should not dictate estimates
Items that can be Done within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint PlanningLarge items must be split before they are pulled into a Sprint
Those wanting to change the backlog do so by trying to convince the Product OwnerStakeholders influence the order through the Product Owner, not around them
Teams on the same product share one Product Goal, Product Backlog and Product OwnerNo separate competing backlogs for one product

Notice what is missing: the Scrum Guide does not require user stories, story points, epics, a Definition of Ready or any prioritisation technique. These are optional complementary practices.

The Product Goal

The Product Goal describes a future state of the product that the Scrum Team plans against. According to the Scrum Guide it is the long-term objective for the Scrum Team, it sits in the product backlog, and the rest of the backlog emerges to define what will fulfil it. The team must fulfil or abandon one Product Goal before taking on the next.

ArtifactCommitmentExample
Product BacklogProduct GoalPatients can book, change and pay for appointments online without phoning the clinic
Sprint BacklogSprint GoalA patient can book an initial appointment online and receive a confirmation
IncrementDefinition of DoneCode reviewed, tested on mobile and desktop, accessible and deployed to production

How to write a useful Product Goal

  1. Start from the product vision or strategy and the problem you most need to solve next.
  2. Describe an outcome for users or the business, not a list of features.
  3. Make it reachable within months rather than years, so the team can see progress.
  4. Make it testable: you should be able to tell when it has been met.
  5. Show it above the backlog and review it at every Sprint Review.

For example, "Improve reporting" is a weak Product Goal; "Finance managers can produce the month-end report without exporting to spreadsheets" is a strong one.

Who owns the product backlog?

The Product Owner is accountable for effective product backlog management. Under the 2020 Scrum Guide this includes:

  • Developing and explicitly communicating the Product Goal.
  • Creating and clearly communicating product backlog items.
  • Ordering product backlog items.
  • Ensuring that the product backlog is transparent, visible and understood.

The Product Owner may do this work or delegate it to others, but remains accountable. The Product Owner is one person, not a committee.

WhoWhat they do with the product backlog
Product OwnerOwns the Product Goal, decides the order, makes sure items are understood
DevelopersSize items, raise technical work and risks, help split and clarify items during refinement
Scrum MasterHelps the Product Owner find techniques for Product Goal definition and backlog management
Stakeholders and usersSuggest items, give feedback at the Sprint Review, and try to convince the Product Owner when they want the order to change

In many teams anyone can add an item to the backlog. That is fine, as long as the Product Owner decides where it goes and whether it stays.

What goes into a product backlog?

Types of product backlog items

Product backlog items (PBIs) can describe any work that improves the product. Keeping it all in one list makes honest ordering possible.

Item typeWhat it isExample
Feature or user storyNew capability or change that users will noticeAs a patient, I want to reschedule my appointment online so that I do not have to phone
BugA defect in something already releasedOpening hours show the wrong day on mobile
Technical debtWork that keeps the product maintainable and safe to changeUpgrade the logging framework before support ends
Spike or researchTime-boxed investigation to reduce uncertaintySpike: can the diary system accept bookings via its API?
ExperimentA small change to test an assumption with real usersShow prices on the booking page to half of visitors and compare completed bookings
Non-functional or compliance workQuality attributes such as accessibility, security or performanceFix the issues found in the accessibility audit
Epic (coarse item)A large item that will be split laterInsurance claims handled online

Many teams write features as user stories and group them under epics. Technical work deserves its own place in the order; our guide to technical debt in Scrum explains why.

Product backlog item attributes

The Scrum Guide says attributes vary with the domain of work. Most teams record something like this:

AttributePurpose
DescriptionWhat is needed and why, often as a user story
OrderIts position relative to other items (Product Owner)
SizeRelative effort, for example in story points (Developers)
ValueThe expected benefit or the outcome it supports
Acceptance criteriaConditions that show the item does what it should
Type, links and statusFeature, bug, spike or debt; related epic or designs; progress

How detail changes from top to bottom

A healthy backlog looks like an iceberg: small and detailed at the top, larger and vaguer further down. Detail added months early is often wasted.

Part of the backlogTypical horizonLevel of detail
TopNext one to two SprintsSmall items, sized, with acceptance criteria; can be Done within one Sprint
MiddleThe following few SprintsMedium items or small epics, roughly sized, some open questions
BottomLaterLarge epics and ideas, a single line each, not yet sized

The DEEP product backlog

A popular checklist for backlog quality is DEEP, an acronym usually credited to Roman Pichler and Mike Cohn. It is not part of the Scrum Guide, but it fits it well.

LetterMeaningQuick check
DDetailed appropriatelyCan the team explain the top five items without guessing?
EEstimatedAre the next two Sprints' items sized by the Developers?
EEmergentDid anything change at the last Sprint Review?
PPrioritised (ordered)If two items tie, does the Product Owner know which goes first?

If a check fails, fix it at the next refinement session.

How to create a product backlog, step by step

Use this process for a new product, or to rebuild a backlog that has become unmanageable.

  1. Agree the Product Goal. Write one sentence describing the outcome the team will work towards next. Everything else hangs from it.
  2. Collect what you already know. Gather requests, ideas, known bugs, technical work and stakeholder commitments, without worrying about detail yet.
  3. Remove duplicates and unrelated items, noting who asked for each one.
  4. Group items into epics or themes to show the shape of the work and reveal gaps.
  5. Write the top items properly. For the items most likely to come first, add a clear description, the reason it matters and draft acceptance criteria. A user story template helps keep them consistent.
  6. Ask the Developers to size them. Use relative estimates such as story points or T-shirt sizes; see Agile estimation. Split anything too large to finish within one Sprint.
  7. Order the backlog. Weigh value, risk, dependencies, cost of delay and what you need to learn. Use a technique from the section below if it helps the conversation.
  8. Check readiness and plan the first Sprint. Make sure roughly one to two Sprints' worth of items at the top are clear and sized, then hold Sprint Planning.

After that first pass, the backlog is maintained through ongoing refinement, Sprint Reviews and regular pruning. Our free product backlog template gives you a ready-made sheet for steps 2 to 8.

Worked example: building a backlog from scratch

A physiotherapy clinic wants patients to stop phoning reception to book. The Product Owner sets the Product Goal: "Patients can book, change and pay for appointments online without phoning the clinic."

Steps 1 and 2: collect, group and filter. A workshop with reception staff, physiotherapists and the IT supplier produces 18 raw ideas. The Product Owner groups them into epics (booking, managing bookings, payments, reminders, staff tools) and moves exercise videos and insurance claims to the bottom, because they do not serve the current Product Goal.

Step 3: write, size and order. After a refinement session with the Developers, the top of the backlog looks like this:

OrderItemTypeSize (points)Why it is here
1Spike: confirm the diary system can accept bookings via its APISpike2Online booking depends on it; reduces the biggest risk first
2Book an initial appointment online (choose clinician, date and time)Story8Core to the Product Goal; nothing else works without it
3Fix opening hours shown on the wrong day on mobileBug1Tiny, visible to every patient, and confuses bookings
4Email confirmation after bookingStory3High value, small, reassures patients
5Cancel a booking onlineStory3Directly reduces phone calls
6Reschedule a booking onlineStory5Directly reduces phone calls
7SMS reminder 24 hours before the appointmentStory5Clinic reports no-shows as a cost
8Pay a deposit by card when bookingStory8Part of the Product Goal; needs a payment provider decision
9Fix issues from the accessibility auditNon-functional3Clinic has committed to fix these this quarter
10Staff view of today's online bookingsStory5Useful for reception, less urgent while volumes are low
LaterInsurance claims onlineEpicNot sizedNext Product Goal, not this one
LaterExercise video libraryEpicNot sizedPossible future goal; may be deleted

Step 4: forecast the first Sprint. The team is new, so it has no velocity yet. In Sprint Planning the Developers look at the top items and judge that items 1 to 4 (14 points) are achievable. The Sprint Goal becomes "A patient can book an initial appointment online and receive a confirmation."

Step 5: adapt. Over the next three Sprints the team completes 13, 16 and 14 points, an average of about 14. Items 5 to 10 total 29 points, so the Product Owner forecasts two to three more Sprints for the remaining Product Goal work and updates that forecast after each Sprint Review.

How to order and prioritise a product backlog

The Scrum Guide uses the word ordered, not prioritised. In practice that means every item has a unique position in one sequence. Labels such as high, medium and low still leave you to decide which high item goes first; an ordered list makes that decision explicit.

Order is not only about value. The Product Owner weighs several factors:

FactorQuestion to ask
ValueHow much does this help users or the business reach the Product Goal?
Cost of delayWhat do we lose for each Sprint we wait? Is there a deadline?
RiskWould doing this early reveal a problem we cannot afford to find late?
DependenciesDoes something else need this first?
SizeHow much effort does it take? Small, valuable items are cheap wins.
LearningWill this give us feedback that changes what we do next?

Prioritisation techniques compared

Techniques give structure to the conversation. None of them replaces the Product Owner's judgement, and none is required by Scrum.

TechniqueHow it worksBest forWatch out for
MoSCoWSort items into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have (this time); originated in DSDMAgreeing scope for a release or Product Goal with stakeholdersToo many Musts; it groups items rather than ordering them
Value vs effortPlot items on a two-by-two grid of value against sizeQuick visual sorting in a workshopIgnores risk and dependencies
WSJF (weighted shortest job first)Cost of delay divided by job size; widely used in SAFe and based on Don Reinertsen's workLarge backlogs where timing mattersScores can look precise when they are guesses
RICE(Reach x Impact x Confidence) / Effort; popularised by IntercomProduct teams with usage dataNeeds reasonable reach estimates
Kano modelClassifies features as basic expectations, performance features or delightersUnderstanding what users really expectNeeds user research to be reliable

Worked example: scoring and ordering six items

The clinic's Product Owner uses a simple WSJF-style score for six middle-of-the-backlog items. Value, time criticality and risk reduction are each scored from 1 to 10 in a short session with stakeholders; size comes from the Developers. Score = (value + time criticality + risk reduction) / size.

ItemValueTime criticalityRisk reductionSizeScore
Spike: choose a payment provider13826.0
Fix accessibility audit issues58335.3
Reschedule a booking online85152.8
SMS reminder 24 hours before76152.8
Staff view of today's bookings62151.8
Pay a deposit by card63281.4

The scores suggest an order; the Product Owner decides. Here rescheduling goes above reminders because reception gets more calls about changes than about forgotten appointments. Card deposits stay low until the payment spike reports back.

Refining the product backlog

Product backlog refinement is the ongoing activity of breaking down and adding detail to items, such as a description, order and size. It is not one of the five Scrum events; teams fit it in wherever it works, often as one or two short sessions per Sprint plus informal conversations. The 2020 Scrum Guide sets no time limit, so inspect and adapt the amount you spend.

A typical session:

  1. The Product Owner brings the next few items and explains the need and the context.
  2. The Developers ask questions, raise risks and suggest simpler options.
  3. Together they draft or improve acceptance criteria.
  4. Items that are too large are split into thin slices that still deliver value.
  5. The Developers size the items.
  6. The Product Owner adjusts the order if anything new was learned.

Splitting example. "Pay a deposit by card when booking" (8 points) is split into "Pay a deposit by debit or credit card" (5), "Receive a deposit receipt by email" (2) and "Refund the deposit when cancelling more than 24 hours ahead" (3). The parts add up to more than the original estimate, which is common: splitting often reveals work nobody had noticed.

For techniques, agendas and timings, see our full guide to backlog refinement.

Definition of Ready: optional, not Scrum

Some teams agree a Definition of Ready, a checklist an item must meet before it can enter Sprint Planning. The Scrum Guide does not include one; it only says items that can be Done within one Sprint are ready for selection. A light checklist (clear description, drafted acceptance criteria, sized, fits in one Sprint, dependencies visible) can help. Keep it short: a long one becomes a stage gate that delays learning.

From product backlog to Sprint Planning

Items leave the product backlog when they are selected in Sprint Planning. The Scrum Team agrees a Sprint Goal, and the Developers select the items they forecast they can complete. Those items, the Sprint Goal and the Developers' plan form the Sprint Backlog.

Use recent velocity as a guide to how much to select, adjusted for holidays and other capacity changes. If an item is not Done at the end of the Sprint, it returns to the product backlog for the Product Owner to reorder.

The Sprint Review closes the loop: the team shows what was done, stakeholders give feedback, and the product backlog is adjusted to meet new opportunities. A sprint planning template helps you record capacity, the Sprint Goal and the selected items.

Product backlog vs sprint backlog

Keeping the product backlog healthy

Backlogs tend to grow faster than teams can deliver. These checks, which are our own suggestions rather than Scrum rules, help you spot problems early.

CheckHealthy signWarning sign
Ready itemsAbout one to two Sprints' worth of refined, sized items at the topSprint Planning keeps stalling on unclear items
Size of the listThe Product Owner can explain why each item is thereHundreds of items nobody has read in months
AgeFew items older than a yearMany items untouched since they were created
Link to the Product GoalTop items clearly serve the current goalThe top is full of unrelated requests
Mix of workFeatures, bugs and technical work all appear in the orderTechnical debt never rises near the top

How to prune an overgrown backlog

  1. Export or list every item with its creation date and last update.
  2. Delete duplicates and items that no longer fit the product.
  3. Archive items older than an agreed age (for example 12 months) that nobody has championed; good ideas tend to come back.
  4. Re-order what remains against the current Product Goal.
  5. Tell stakeholders what was removed and how to raise it again if it matters.

Clear ordering against a Product Goal is one of the best defences against scope creep.

Common product backlog mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
A backlog that only growsThe important items are buried and refinement takes longer each SprintPrune regularly and delete items that no longer serve the Product Goal
Several backlogs for one productTeams optimise locally and nobody can order work across the productOne product backlog and one Product Owner, with views or filters per team
Everything is high priorityLabels hide the real decisionsKeep one strict order
Too much detail too earlySpecifications for distant items are rewritten or thrown awayDetail the top; keep the rest coarse
The Product Owner works aloneItems are unclear and sizes are guessesRefine with the Developers and involve stakeholders
No Product GoalThe backlog becomes a list of requests rather than a plan for valueAgree and display a Product Goal
Technical work kept off the backlogDebt accumulates invisibly and slows every SprintMake technical items visible and order them alongside features

Frequently asked questions

What is a product backlog in Scrum?

It is an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product, and the single source of work for the Scrum Team. Its commitment is the Product Goal, and the Product Owner is accountable for it.

Who owns the product backlog?

The Product Owner is accountable for it, including the Product Goal, the items and their order. Others can help, but the accountability stays with the Product Owner.

Who can add items to the product backlog?

Anyone can suggest items, and in many teams Developers and stakeholders add them directly. The Product Owner decides whether an item stays and where it goes in the order.

What is the difference between a product backlog and a sprint backlog?

The product backlog covers everything that might improve the product for its whole life and belongs to the Product Owner. The sprint backlog is the Sprint Goal, the items selected for one Sprint and the Developers' plan, and belongs to the Developers.

Why does Scrum say ordered rather than prioritised?

Priority labels such as high, medium and low leave ties. Ordering puts every item in one sequence that can reflect value, risk, dependencies and learning.

How big should a product backlog be?

There is no rule. A useful test is whether the Product Owner can explain why each item is there. Keep detailed items to a few Sprints' worth and hold the rest as coarse epics.

How often should the product backlog be refined?

Continuously. Most teams hold one or two short refinement sessions per Sprint. The 2020 Scrum Guide sets no time limit; the old guidance of no more than 10% of capacity was removed.

Do product backlog items have to be user stories?

No. The Scrum Guide does not prescribe a format. User stories are popular for features, but bugs, technical tasks and spikes are often clearer as plain descriptions.

Who estimates product backlog items?

The Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing. The Product Owner helps them understand items and trade-offs but should not set the estimates.

When is a product backlog item ready?

The Scrum Guide says items that can be Done within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint Planning. Some teams add a short Definition of Ready, which is optional.

Can multiple teams share one product backlog?

Yes. Teams working on the same product share one Product Goal, product backlog and Product Owner.

Is the product backlog the same as a product roadmap?

No. A roadmap shows direction and themes over quarters or years. The product backlog lists the specific work needed next, in order, and changes far more often.

Do bugs and technical debt belong in the product backlog?

Yes. Putting them in the same list as features lets the Product Owner order all work honestly, and stops important maintenance from being forgotten.
