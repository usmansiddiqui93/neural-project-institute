Scrum Metrics and KPIs: What to Track and How

Scrum metrics are the measures a Scrum Team uses to inspect its progress, quality, flow and the value it delivers, so it can adapt its plans and ways of working. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not require any metric, but a small, well-chosen set makes empiricism easier. This guide explains which Scrum metrics and KPIs are worth tracking, how to calculate each one with worked examples, which to review at each event and how to avoid the mistakes that turn metrics into targets.

Key takeaways

  • The Scrum Guide mandates no metrics; it names burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flows as useful forecasting practices that do not replace empiricism.
  • Cover six areas with a handful of measures: goals, delivery, flow, quality, value and team health.
  • Sprint Goal success rate and a value measure usually tell you more than velocity alone.
  • Use velocity and throughput for forecasting ranges, never as targets or to compare teams.
  • Evidence-Based Management from Scrum.org gives a value-focused frame: current value, unrealised value, time to market and ability to innovate.
  • Review metrics with the team at the Sprint Review and Retrospective, and always look at several together.

What are Scrum metrics?

Scrum metrics are quantitative measures that a Scrum Team uses to make its work transparent and to support inspection and adaptation. They cover whether the team meets its Sprint Goals, how much work it completes, how smoothly work flows, the quality of each Increment, the value delivered to users and the health of the team itself.

People often use "Scrum metrics" and "Scrum KPIs" interchangeably, but there is a useful distinction. A metric is any measure the team finds informative. A key performance indicator (KPI) is a measure an organisation has chosen as a critical indicator of progress towards an intended result, usually with a target attached.

Scrum metricKPI
PurposeHelp the team inspect and adaptShow progress towards an organisational result
Typical ownerThe Scrum TeamLeadership, product or portfolio management
Typical examplesBurndown, velocity, cycle time, retro actions completedCustomer satisfaction, adoption, revenue per user, release frequency
Review cadenceAt least once per SprintMonthly, quarterly or per release
Target attached?Usually not; trends matter moreOften yes
Risk if misusedGaming, such as inflated estimatesLocal optimisation at the expense of the whole

The best Scrum KPIs are outcome measures, such as product usage or customer satisfaction, that the team can influence and that link to the Product Goal. Output measures such as velocity make poor KPIs because they are easy to inflate and say nothing about value.

What the Scrum Guide says about metrics

The Scrum Guide (2020 edition, still the current official version) does not define any metric. It does not mention velocity, story points or KPIs at all. What it does say is relevant to every metric you choose:

  • Scrum is founded on empiricism and Lean thinking. Knowledge comes from experience and decisions are based on what is observed. Metrics are one way to observe. See empiricism in Scrum.
  • Forecasting practices are optional. In the Sprint section the Guide says: "Various practices exist to forecast progress, like burn-downs, burn-ups, or cumulative flows. While proven useful, these do not replace the importance of empiricism."
  • Only what has happened can be used for decisions. The Guide notes that in complex environments what will happen is unknown, so only what has already happened may be used for forward-looking decision making. Every forecast should rest on actual past data.
  • Done work is the evidence. Work cannot count as part of an Increment unless it meets the Definition of Done. Any metric that counts unfinished work weakens transparency.
  • The Developers forecast their own Sprint. In Sprint Planning, the Guide says the more the Developers know about their past performance, their upcoming capacity and their Definition of Done, the more confident they will be in their Sprint forecasts.

So metrics are a complementary practice, like story points or user stories. The team chooses them, owns them and changes them when they stop helping. Our guide to Scrum artifacts explains the commitments (Product Goal, Sprint Goal and Definition of Done) that most good metrics are anchored to.

The six categories of Scrum metrics

A balanced set covers six areas. You do not need every metric below; pick one or two per category that answer questions your team is actually asking.

CategoryQuestion it answersExample metricsMain users
GoalsAre we achieving what we set out to do?Sprint Goal success rate, Product Goal progressWhole Scrum Team, stakeholders
DeliveryHow much can we complete, and when will it be ready?Velocity, Sprint burndown, release burnupDevelopers, Product Owner
FlowHow smoothly and predictably does work move?Cycle time, throughput, WIP, work item ageDevelopers, Scrum Master
QualityIs the Increment really done and sustainable?Escaped defects, Definition of Done adherence, technical debt, change failure rateDevelopers
ValueAre we building the right thing?Usage, customer satisfaction, business outcomesProduct Owner, stakeholders
Team healthCan we keep working this way?Happiness or engagement, retrospective actions completedScrum Master, whole team

The categories balance each other. Velocity can rise while quality falls; cycle time can drop because the team only picks easy items; happiness can be high while users are ignored. Looking across categories is what makes the picture trustworthy.

Goal and delivery metrics

These are the most familiar Scrum metrics. They help the team plan Sprints and answer the stakeholder question "when will it be ready?".

Sprint Goal success rate

The Sprint Goal success rate is the percentage of Sprints in which the team met its Sprint Goal. Because the Sprint Goal is the single objective for the Sprint, this is the most direct measure of whether the team delivers what it intends.

Formula: Sprint Goal success rate = Sprints where the Sprint Goal was met ÷ total Sprints × 100.

Tip

Worked example: over the last ten Sprints, the team met its Sprint Goal in seven. Success rate = 7 ÷ 10 × 100 = 70%. In the Retrospective the team notices that two of the misses came from unplanned production support, so it agrees to reserve some capacity for support in Sprint Planning.

Do not chase 100%. A team that always meets its goal may be setting goals that are too safe. The goal also needs to be outcome-focused ("customers can reset their own password") rather than a list of tickets, or the metric becomes a count of items done.

Velocity

Velocity is the amount of work, usually in story points, that meets the Definition of Done in a Sprint. Partly finished items count as zero. It is a planning aid for the team, not a productivity score. Our guide to velocity in Scrum covers how to calculate, chart and use it in detail.

Sprint burndown chart

A Sprint burndown plots the work remaining in the Sprint Backlog against the days of the Sprint. The Developers use it, often at the Daily Scrum, to see whether they are on track for the Sprint Goal. A flat line for several days usually means work is blocked or items are too big to finish in a day or two; a line that jumps up means work was added.

Release or Product Goal burnup

A burnup chart shows completed work rising over time against a separate line for total scope. Because scope has its own line, it makes scope changes visible, which a burndown hides. It is the most useful chart for longer-range forecasts towards a release or the Product Goal.

Worked example of a burnup forecast:

  1. The Product Owner estimates that the remaining work for the next release is 180 points in the Product Backlog.
  2. Velocity over the last five Sprints was 24, 21, 27, 23 and 25, an average of 24.
  3. Middle forecast: 180 ÷ 24 = 7.5, so about 8 Sprints.
  4. Pessimistic forecast using the lowest velocity: 180 ÷ 21 ≈ 8.6, so 9 Sprints.
  5. Optimistic forecast using the highest velocity: 180 ÷ 27 ≈ 6.7, so 7 Sprints.
  6. The forecast is shared as a range: "7 to 9 Sprints, most likely 8, if scope stays as it is". It is updated at every Sprint Review as velocity and scope change.

Giving a range, rather than a single date, is the honest way to reflect uncertainty. Our free release plan template has space to record the forecast.

Flow metrics for Scrum teams

Flow metrics come from Kanban and Lean but work well inside Scrum, especially for teams whose backlog items are small and similar. They measure how work moves rather than how big it was estimated to be.

MetricDefinitionHow to calculateWhat it tells you
Cycle timeTime from starting an item to finishing itDone date minus start date for each item; report the median or 85th percentileHow long a typical item takes; how predictable delivery is
ThroughputNumber of items finished per periodCount items that meet the Definition of Done each Sprint or weekDelivery rate without needing estimates
Work in progress (WIP)Number of items started but not finishedCount items in active columns on the boardOverload and multitasking; high WIP lengthens cycle time
Work item ageHow long an unfinished item has been in progressToday minus start date for each open itemItems at risk of stalling, while there is still time to act

A practical example: the team's 85th percentile cycle time is six working days. In the middle of a Sprint, one item has been in progress for nine days. Its age flags it at the Daily Scrum before it becomes a missed Sprint Goal, and the Developers swarm on it.

Throughput can also replace velocity for forecasting. If the team finishes 10 to 12 items per Sprint and 60 items remain, the forecast is 5 to 6 Sprints, without estimating any item in points.

For definitions, charts and percentile forecasting in more depth, read our guides to Kanban metrics, cycle time and the cumulative flow diagram, which is one of the forecasting practices the Scrum Guide names.

Quality metrics

Quality metrics check that "done" really means done. Without them, velocity and throughput can look healthy while problems pile up for later Sprints.

  • Escaped defects. Defects found after an item met the Definition of Done, often by users in production. Track the count per Sprint and look at the trend.
  • Defect escape rate. Escaped defects ÷ (escaped defects + defects caught before release) × 100. Example: 4 defects reached production and 36 were caught in the Sprint, so 4 ÷ 40 = 10%.
  • Definition of Done adherence. The share of completed items that genuinely met every Definition of Done criterion. Some teams check a sample at the Retrospective.
  • Technical debt indicators. Items such as known shortcuts logged in the backlog, test coverage trends or static analysis warnings. See technical debt in Scrum.
  • Change failure rate. The share of releases or deployments that cause a failure needing a fix or rollback. It is one of the DORA software delivery metrics and appears among the example measures in the Evidence-Based Management Guide.

A rising escaped-defect trend is usually a signal to strengthen the Definition of Done, for example by adding automated regression tests, rather than a reason to blame individuals.

Value and outcome metrics

Value metrics answer the question the other categories cannot: are we building the right thing? They are the Product Owner's main evidence for ordering the Product Backlog and the best candidates for KPIs.

Output measure (what we produced)Outcome measure (what changed)
Number of features releasedShare of active users who use the new feature
Story points completedReduction in support tickets about the old process
Reports deliveredTime saved per user per week
Campaign pages launchedConversion rate from page visit to sign-up

Choose outcome measures that the team can influence within a few Sprints and that relate to the Product Goal. Customer satisfaction scores such as CSAT or NPS, product usage, task completion rates and revenue or cost per user are common choices. Review them at the Sprint Review, where stakeholders and the Scrum Team inspect the outcome of the Sprint and decide what to do next.

Outcome data often lags. A feature released this Sprint may only show its effect on usage after a month, so treat value metrics as trends across several Sprints, not as a score for one Sprint.

Team health metrics

Scrum depends on a self-managing team working at a sustainable pace. Team health metrics give early warning before burnout or disengagement shows up in delivery.

  • Happiness or engagement score. A quick anonymous rating, such as 1 to 5, collected at each Retrospective, with a short reason.
  • Retrospective actions completed. The share of improvement actions agreed at the last Sprint Retrospective that were actually done. A low rate suggests the team agrees too many actions or does not add them to the Sprint Backlog.
  • Unplanned work. The share of capacity spent on work that was not in the Sprint Backlog at Sprint Planning, such as urgent support.

Keep these inside the team. Sharing an individual's happiness score, or ranking teams by it, destroys the honesty the metric depends on.

Evidence-Based Management

Evidence-Based Management (EBM) is a framework from Scrum.org for measuring and improving the value an organisation delivers. The EBM Guide was first released in 2015 and has been updated several times, most recently in 2024. It is not part of the Scrum Guide, but it fits naturally with Scrum's focus on goals and empiricism.

EBM groups measures into four Key Value Areas:

Key Value AreaQuestionExample measures named in the EBM Guide
Current ValueHow much value does the product deliver today?Customer satisfaction, employee satisfaction, customer usage index, revenue per employee
Unrealised ValueHow much more value could the product deliver?Market share, customer or user satisfaction gap
Time to MarketHow quickly can we deliver and learn?Release frequency, lead time, customer cycle time, time to learn, mean time to repair
Ability to InnovateHow effectively can we deliver new capabilities?Innovation rate, defect trends, technical debt, change failure rate, time spent context-switching

EBM also describes three levels of goals: a long-term Strategic Goal, nearer-term Intermediate Goals, and Immediate Tactical Goals that teams pursue through small experiments. In Scrum terms, the Product Goal often sits at the intermediate level and each Sprint Goal is an immediate tactical step.

For most teams the practical lesson is simple: make sure at least one of your metrics sits in each value area, so you are not measuring speed alone. The OKR template is a convenient way to record goals and the measures you will use to judge them.

Which metrics to review at each Scrum event

Metrics are only useful if someone looks at them at the moment a decision is made. Scrum's events give natural points to do that.

EventMetrics to look atDecision they support
Sprint PlanningAverage velocity or throughput, capacity, Sprint Goal success trendHow much to select and how ambitious the Sprint Goal should be
Daily ScrumSprint burndown, WIP, work item ageWhat to swarm on today; what is blocked
Sprint ReviewProduct Goal or release burnup, value and outcome measures, forecast rangeWhat to do next; changes to the Product Backlog and release forecast
Sprint RetrospectiveSprint Goal met, escaped defects, cycle time trend, happiness, retro actions completedWhich one or two improvements to make next Sprint
Backlog refinement (ongoing)Item size distribution, age of top backlog itemsWhich items to split or clarify before Sprint Planning

Scrum metrics for leadership and stakeholders

Managers often ask for "Scrum KPIs" to report upwards. That is reasonable, but the metrics a team uses to improve are not always the right ones to report.

Share widelyKeep mostly inside the team
Product Goal progress and forecast rangesVelocity in story points
Value and outcome measuresSprint burndown
Release frequency and lead timeIndividual workload or task counts
Escaped defects trendHappiness scores
Sprint Goal success rateRetrospective details

Story points are relative to one team, so a stakeholder who sees team A's velocity of 40 and team B's of 20 will draw the wrong conclusion. Forecast ranges, outcomes and quality trends answer the questions leaders actually have: when, what value and how reliably. For metrics across several teams or a whole programme, see our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs.

How to choose and introduce Scrum metrics

A new metric works best when the team chooses it to answer a real question. This step-by-step process keeps the set small and useful.

  1. Start from questions, not dashboards. In a Retrospective, ask what the team, Product Owner and stakeholders most need to know. For example: "Will we hit the release?", "Why do items take so long?", "Are users adopting what we ship?"
  2. Pick one measure per question. Match each question to a metric from the six categories. Aim for four to six metrics in total.
  3. Agree definitions in writing. Decide exactly what counts: when does an item "start", which defects count as escaped, what is the scale for happiness. Write it in the team's working agreement.
  4. Automate collection where possible. Most Scrum tools produce burndown, velocity and cycle time automatically if the board reflects reality.
  5. Collect a baseline for three to five Sprints before drawing conclusions. Single Sprints are noisy.
  6. Review at the right event using the event table above, and ask "what will we do differently because of this?" each time.
  7. Pair every speed metric with a quality metric. For example velocity with escaped defects, or throughput with Definition of Done adherence.
  8. Retire metrics that nobody acts on. Every few months, drop any measure that has not changed a decision.

Worked example: a Scrum team dashboard

Here is a one-page dashboard for a fictional six-person team working in two-week Sprints. It shows six Sprints and one metric from most categories.

SprintSprint Goal met?Velocity (points)Throughput (items)Cycle time, 85th pct (days)Escaped defectsHappiness (1–5)
14Yes2411613.8
15Yes2612603.9
16No229823.4
17No2710943.1
18Yes2311733.3
19Yes2412613.7

How the team read it at the Retrospective after Sprint 17:

  • Velocity alone looked good. Sprint 17 had the highest velocity of the period, 27 points.
  • But the goal was missed twice in a row, cycle time rose from 6 to 9 days and escaped defects jumped to 4.
  • Happiness fell from 3.9 to 3.1. Team comments mentioned pressure to finish a large stakeholder request and skipping some testing.
  • Diagnosis: the team had pulled in more work than usual, started too many items at once and let the Definition of Done slip to show progress.

The team agreed two actions: a WIP limit of four items in progress, and adding automated regression tests to the Definition of Done. By Sprint 19, cycle time and escaped defects were back to normal and both Sprint Goals had been met, at a slightly lower but steadier velocity.

Tip

Look at metrics together and as trends. Any one of the columns above, read on its own, would have told the wrong story about Sprint 17.

Common mistakes with Scrum metrics

Most metric problems come from how numbers are used, not from the numbers themselves. The idea often summarised as Goodhart's law applies: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

  • Setting velocity targets. Teams respond by inflating estimates, so velocity rises while real output does not.
  • Comparing or ranking teams. Story points and even cycle times depend on each team's context and definitions.
  • Using team metrics in individual performance reviews. People stop reporting problems honestly, which undermines transparency, the first pillar of empiricism.
  • Counting unfinished work. Giving partial credit for items that do not meet the Definition of Done makes forecasts unreliable.
  • Tracking too many metrics. A dashboard of twenty charts that nobody discusses adds work and no insight.
  • Measuring activity instead of outcomes. Hours logged, tickets closed or lines of code say little about value.
  • Optimising one metric at the expense of others, such as speed over quality or throughput over customer value.
  • Collecting data nobody acts on. If a metric never changes a decision, drop it.

The Scrum Master can help by coaching the team and stakeholders on what each metric does and does not show, and by keeping the conversation focused on improvement rather than judgement.

Tools for tracking Scrum metrics

You can track every metric on this page in a spreadsheet, but most teams let their Scrum tool collect the data. Jira Software is widely used by software teams for its Sprint reports, burndown and velocity charts. ClickUp offers sprint reporting with burndown, burnup, velocity and cumulative flow. Wrike, our top-ranked tool overall, suits organisations that need configurable dashboards across many teams and projects, including Agile and non-software work.

Whatever you use, the tool can only report what the board shows, so keep item states up to date and make sure "done" on the board means the Definition of Done.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important Scrum metrics?

There is no official list, but a strong starter set is Sprint Goal success rate, velocity or throughput for forecasting, cycle time, escaped defects, one value or outcome measure and a team health measure. Together they cover goals, delivery, flow, quality, value and sustainability.

Which metrics does the Scrum Guide require?

None. The 2020 Scrum Guide says various practices exist to forecast progress, such as burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flows, and that while proven useful, these do not replace the importance of empiricism. Velocity and story points are not mentioned.

Is velocity a KPI?

It should not be. Velocity is a team-specific planning aid based on relative estimates, so it is easy to inflate and cannot be compared between teams. Use it to forecast, and choose outcome measures such as usage or customer satisfaction as KPIs.

What is the difference between a Scrum metric and a KPI?

A Scrum metric is any measure the team uses to inspect and adapt, such as a burndown or cycle time. A KPI is a measure an organisation has selected as a key indicator of progress towards a result, usually with a target, such as customer satisfaction or release frequency.

How do you calculate Sprint Goal success rate?

Divide the number of Sprints in which the team met its Sprint Goal by the total number of Sprints, then multiply by 100. For example, seven goals met in ten Sprints gives a 70% success rate.

What is the difference between a burndown and a burnup chart?

A burndown shows work remaining falling towards zero. A burnup shows completed work rising towards a separate total scope line, so it makes scope changes visible and is better for release and Product Goal forecasts.

What are escaped defects?

Escaped defects are defects found after an item was considered done, often by users in production. A rising trend usually means the Definition of Done needs strengthening, for example with more automated testing.

What metrics should a Scrum Master track?

Scrum Masters usually focus on metrics that reveal how well Scrum is working: Sprint Goal success, cycle time and work item age, WIP, retrospective actions completed and team happiness. The team, not the Scrum Master alone, should own and interpret them.

What metrics should a Product Owner track?

Product Owners need forecast and value measures: release or Product Goal burnup, forecast ranges from velocity or throughput, and outcome measures such as usage, customer satisfaction or the business result the Product Goal targets.

Can flow metrics be used in Scrum?

Yes. Cycle time, throughput, WIP and work item age work well alongside Scrum, and cumulative flow is one of the forecasting practices the Scrum Guide names. Some teams forecast from throughput instead of story points.

Should Scrum metrics be used for performance reviews?

Generally no. Using team metrics to judge individuals encourages gaming and hiding problems, which undermines transparency and empiricism. Review metrics as a team to decide improvements.

How does Evidence-Based Management relate to Scrum metrics?

Evidence-Based Management is a Scrum.org framework that groups value measures into four Key Value Areas: current value, unrealised value, time to market and ability to innovate. It helps teams and leaders balance delivery metrics with measures of value.

How do you measure the success of a Scrum team?

Look at whether it reliably meets Sprint Goals, delivers Increments that meet the Definition of Done with few escaped defects, moves measurably towards the Product Goal and creates value users notice, while keeping a sustainable pace.
