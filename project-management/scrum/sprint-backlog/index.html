Sprint Backlog: What It Is, How to Create It, Examples

The sprint backlog is the Developers' plan for the Sprint: the Sprint Goal, the Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint and an actionable plan for delivering them. It is created in Sprint Planning and updated every day as the team learns. This guide explains what the 2020 Scrum Guide says, who owns the sprint backlog, how to build and update one step by step, what can and cannot change during a Sprint, and the mistakes to avoid, with a full worked example.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 14 September 2026
  • 20 min read

Key takeaways

  • The sprint backlog has three parts: the Sprint Goal (why), the selected Product Backlog items (what) and the Developers' plan (how).
  • It is a plan by and for the Developers. They create it in Sprint Planning and own it for the whole Sprint.
  • Its commitment is the Sprint Goal. The goal stays fixed; the items and the plan can change as the team learns.
  • If the work turns out different from expected, the Developers renegotiate scope with the Product Owner without endangering the Sprint Goal.
  • Update it at least daily so progress can be inspected at the Daily Scrum. Tasks, hours and burndown charts are optional practices, not Scrum rules.
  • At the end of the Sprint, Done items join the Increment and unfinished items go back to the Product Backlog.

What is a sprint backlog?

A sprint backlog is the Developers' plan for one Sprint. It contains the Sprint Goal, the Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint and an actionable plan for turning them into a usable Increment. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes it as a highly visible, real-time picture of the work the Developers plan to accomplish to achieve the Sprint Goal.

The sprint backlog is one of the three Scrum artifacts, alongside the Product Backlog and the Increment. Each artifact has a commitment that gives it focus. For the sprint backlog, that commitment is the Sprint Goal.

Four features set it apart from an ordinary task list:

  • It has a purpose. The Sprint Goal explains why the Sprint is worth doing, so the team can make sensible trade-offs.
  • It belongs to the Developers. Nobody outside the Developers decides how the work is done or who does which task.
  • It is live. It changes throughout the Sprint as the team learns, not only at the start.
  • It lasts one Sprint. A new sprint backlog is created at every Sprint Planning.

What the Scrum Guide 2020 says about the sprint backlog

The 2020 Scrum Guide is still the current official version (checked 26 October 2026). The Scrum Guide Expansion Pack published in June 2025 by Ralph Jocham, John Coleman and Jeff Sutherland adds context and examples, but it is a supplement and does not change the rules. The table summarises what the Scrum Guide says about the sprint backlog and what each point means for your team.

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means in practice
The sprint backlog is composed of the Sprint Goal (why), the Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint (what) and an actionable plan for delivering the Increment (how)A list of tickets without a goal or a plan is not a complete sprint backlog
It is a plan by and for the DevelopersThe Developers create it, own it and change it; managers do not assign its tasks
It is a highly visible, real-time picture of the workKeep it where everyone can see it and keep it current
It is updated throughout the Sprint as more is learnedThe plan is expected to change; that is not a failure
It should have enough detail that the Developers can inspect their progress in the Daily ScrumIf you cannot tell at the Daily Scrum whether the Sprint Goal is on track, add detail
The Sprint Goal is the commitment; it is created in Sprint Planning and added to the sprint backlogWrite the goal at the top of the board, not in a separate document
If the work turns out different from expected, the Developers collaborate with the Product Owner to negotiate the scope of the sprint backlog without affecting the Sprint GoalItems can be added, removed or changed mid-Sprint; the goal is what you protect
Planning is often done by decomposing items into smaller work items of one day or less; how is at the sole discretion of the DevelopersTasks of a day or less are common, but the format is the team's choice
The most impactful improvements from the Sprint Retrospective may be added to the sprint backlog for the next SprintMake improvement work visible next to product work

Notice what is missing. The Scrum Guide does not require tasks, hour estimates, story points, assignees, due dates or a burndown chart. These are complementary practices that many teams find useful. Treat them as choices you can change, not as rules.

The three parts of a sprint backlog

Why: the Sprint Goal

The Sprint Goal is the single objective for the Sprint. The whole Scrum Team agrees it during Sprint Planning, starting from the Product Owner's proposal of how the Sprint could increase the product's value. It gives the Developers focus and flexibility: if one item turns out harder than expected, they can find another way to meet the goal.

Weak Sprint GoalWhy it is weakStronger Sprint Goal
Complete stories 101 to 108A list of items, not an objective; gives no room to adaptCustomers can choose a delivery slot at checkout
Work on the reporting moduleNot testable; nobody can tell when it is metFinance managers can export the monthly sales report without manual edits
Deliver as much as possibleEncourages overloading the SprintNew users can sign up and verify their email address on mobile

A good Sprint Goal is short, describes an outcome, can be checked at the Sprint Review and moves the team toward the Product Goal.

What: the selected Product Backlog items

The Developers select Product Backlog items from the top of the ordered Product Backlog, in discussion with the Product Owner, to form the forecast for the Sprint. Most items should directly support the Sprint Goal. It is common to include a small amount of other work, such as an urgent bug or an improvement action, as long as everyone can still see what the goal is.

Items should be small enough to be Done within the Sprint. That is the job of backlog refinement before Sprint Planning, not of the first days of the Sprint.

How: the plan

The plan describes how the Developers will turn the selected items into a usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done. Many teams break each item into tasks of a day or less, because small tasks make progress visible at the Daily Scrum. Others plan only the first few days in detail and add tasks as they go. Both are valid; the plan only needs to be actionable and inspectable.

Good plans include everything needed to meet the Definition of Done, such as testing, documentation and deployment steps, not just the build work.

Who owns the sprint backlog?

The Developers own the sprint backlog. The Scrum Guide makes them accountable for creating a plan for the Sprint, and describes the sprint backlog as a plan by and for the Developers. In practice, that means:

WhoTheir part in the sprint backlog
DevelopersSelect items with the Product Owner, create the plan, choose who does what, update it daily, raise risks to the Sprint Goal
Product OwnerProposes how the Sprint could add value, clarifies items, agrees the Sprint Goal, negotiates scope when the work changes, decides where unfinished items go
Scrum MasterHelps the team plan well, coaches self-management, makes sure impediments are removed; does not assign tasks
Stakeholders and managersCan see the sprint backlog for transparency; raise new requests with the Product Owner rather than adding them directly

The Product Owner and Scrum Master take part as Developers only when they are actively working on sprint backlog items. For the full picture of who does what, see our guide to Scrum team roles.

When and how the sprint backlog is created

The sprint backlog is created in Sprint Planning, the event that starts every Sprint. Sprint Planning addresses three topics, and each one produces one part of the sprint backlog.

Sprint Planning topicQuestionPart of the sprint backlog it creates
Topic oneWhy is this Sprint valuable?The Sprint Goal
Topic twoWhat can be Done this Sprint?The selected Product Backlog items
Topic threeHow will the chosen work get done?The plan

Sprint Planning is time-boxed to a maximum of eight hours for a one-month Sprint and is usually shorter for shorter Sprints. Its main inputs are the ordered Product Backlog, the latest Increment, the Developers' projected capacity and their past performance.

How to create a sprint backlog, step by step

Use these steps in Sprint Planning. Steps 1 and 2 can be prepared beforehand so the event stays focused.

  1. Check the top of the Product Backlog. Make sure roughly one to two Sprints' worth of items are clear, sized and small enough to be Done within a Sprint. If not, the team will spend Sprint Planning refining instead of planning.
  2. Work out capacity. List each Developer's available days, subtracting leave, training, support duties and public holidays. See Agile capacity planning for methods.
  3. Agree the Sprint Goal. The Product Owner proposes how this Sprint could add value; the whole Scrum Team shapes it into one clear objective.
  4. Select items. Starting at the top of the Product Backlog, the Developers pull in items that serve the goal until they judge the forecast is realistic. Use recent velocity as a guide, adjusted for capacity, not as a target.
  5. Plan the work. For each item, the Developers decide how they will build, test and finish it. Break items into tasks of a day or less where that helps. Plan the first few days in the most detail.
  6. Add an improvement action. Bring in one of the most important actions from the last Sprint Retrospective so it is planned, not forgotten.
  7. Sense-check against the Definition of Done. Ask: if we finish these tasks, will every item meet the Definition of Done? Add missing testing, review or release work.
  8. Make it visible. Put the Sprint Goal at the top of the board, the items in order and the tasks underneath. Do not pre-assign every task; let people pull work as they become free.
Tip

If the team finishes Sprint Planning and cannot explain in one sentence what success looks like, the Sprint Goal needs more work before anyone writes code.

Worked example: building a two-week sprint backlog

A team of five Developers builds the online shop for a garden centre. Sprints last two weeks (ten working days). The current Product Goal is "Customers can order plants for home delivery on a day that suits them."

Step 1: capacity. Before Sprint Planning, the Developers list their availability.

DeveloperAvailable daysNotes
Priya10
Tom8Two days' leave
Aisha10
Marek9One training day
Lena10Also on the support rota
Total47 of 50About 94% of normal capacity

Step 2: forecast. The team completed 26, 30 and 28 points in its last three Sprints, an average of 28. At 94% capacity that suggests about 26 points. Because Lena is on support, the Developers decide to aim a little lower, at around 23.

Step 3: Sprint Goal. The Product Owner explains that customers are abandoning orders because they cannot choose a delivery day. The Scrum Team agrees the Sprint Goal: "Customers can choose a delivery slot at checkout."

Step 4: selected items.

Total forecast23

Step 5: the plan. The Developers break each item into tasks of a day or less. Here is the plan for item 2:

TaskSizeNotes
Add slot picker to the checkout page1 dayUse existing date component
API endpoint to reserve a slot with the delivery partner1 dayPartner API documented
Save the reserved slot against the orderHalf a day
Error messages when a slot is takenHalf a dayAgree wording with the Product Owner
Accessibility check of the picker (keyboard and screen reader)Half a dayRequired by the Definition of Done

Only items 1 and 2 are broken down in full on day one. Items 3 to 6 get a few starter tasks and are planned in detail when the team reaches them. Nobody is assigned to item 4 yet; whoever finishes first will pull it.

The result is a sprint backlog with a clear goal, a realistic forecast and a plan detailed enough to inspect at tomorrow's Daily Scrum.

Updating the sprint backlog during the Sprint

The sprint backlog should always show the current plan. Most teams update it continuously as tasks move, then check it together at the Daily Scrum. The Daily Scrum's job is to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the sprint backlog as necessary.

Not everything in the sprint backlog is equally open to change. This table shows what usually changes and who decides.

ElementCan it change during the Sprint?Who decides
Sprint GoalNo. If it becomes obsolete, the Product Owner may cancel the SprintProduct Owner (cancellation only)
Selected itemsYes, scope can be clarified and renegotiated as long as the Sprint Goal is not endangeredDevelopers with the Product Owner
The plan and tasksYes, at any timeDevelopers
Order of workYesDevelopers
Who does whatYesDevelopers
Quality standardsNo. Quality does not decrease during a SprintNobody

Worked example: renegotiating scope on day 6

On day 6 of the garden centre Sprint, Marek finds that the delivery partner's API does not support releasing a reserved slot automatically. Item 4 ("Release a reserved slot if checkout is abandoned") now looks like 13 points instead of 5, because the team would need a scheduled job and a manual fallback.

  1. At the Daily Scrum, Marek raises the risk. The Developers agree that items 1 to 3 are still on track and that the Sprint Goal does not depend on item 4.
  2. Straight after the Daily Scrum, two Developers meet the Product Owner and explain the options.
  3. The Product Owner agrees to remove item 4 from the Sprint. It returns to the Product Backlog with a note about the API limit and will be re-refined.
  4. The Developers update the board, and the freed capacity goes into finishing item 2 and the regression tests.
  5. The Sprint Goal is unchanged and is met by the end of the Sprint.

This is Scrum working as intended: the plan changed, the commitment did not.

Handling unplanned work and urgent bugs

Urgent work will arrive. Deal with it openly rather than hiding it:

  • Make it visible. Add the item to the sprint backlog, marked as unplanned, so the team can see its effect.
  • Check the Sprint Goal. If the new work threatens the goal, the Developers and the Product Owner decide together what comes out.
  • Route requests through the Product Owner. Stakeholders should not add items directly; the Product Owner decides whether something is urgent enough to interrupt the Sprint.
  • Discuss patterns in the Retrospective. If interruptions happen every Sprint, keep some capacity free or rotate a support role.

If the Sprint Goal itself becomes obsolete, for example because the business changes direction, only the Product Owner can cancel the Sprint.

How to track sprint backlog progress

Most teams show the sprint backlog on a Scrum board, physical or digital. A simple board has the Sprint Goal at the top, one row per selected item and columns such as To do, In progress, Review and Done. Our suggestions, which go beyond the Scrum Guide:

  • Keep columns about state, not job titles. Columns such as Analysis, Development and Testing encourage hand-offs and mini-waterfalls inside the Sprint.
  • Limit work in progress. Finishing two items is better than having five half-done. Many teams borrow WIP limits from Kanban or swarm on the top item.
  • Show blocked work clearly, for example with a red marker and the date it became blocked.
  • Track items, not hours. An item is either Done or not. Counting hours spent says little about whether the Sprint Goal will be met.

The Scrum Guide mentions burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flow as practices for forecasting progress, but does not require any of them. A sprint burndown chart plots the work remaining each day against an ideal straight line.

What happens to the sprint backlog at the end of the Sprint

A sprint backlog lasts exactly one Sprint. When the Sprint ends:

  1. Done items become part of the Increment. They may already have been released during the Sprint.
  2. The Sprint Review inspects the Increment and progress toward the Product Goal with stakeholders, and the Product Backlog is adjusted. It reviews the outcome, not the task list. See Sprint Review.
  3. Unfinished items go back to the Product Backlog. They do not roll over automatically; the Product Owner re-orders them like any other item.
  4. The Sprint Retrospective uses what happened in the sprint backlog (interruptions, blocked items, scope changes) as evidence for improvements.
  5. A new sprint backlog is created at the next Sprint Planning, which starts straight away.
Tip

Never mark an item as partly Done to keep a velocity number looking good. Undone work is not part of the Increment and its points do not count.

Common sprint backlog mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
No Sprint Goal, just a list of ticketsThe team cannot make trade-offs and every item feels equally urgentAgree one clear Sprint Goal and show it at the top of the board
Filling the Sprint to 100% of capacityNo room for surprises, so the Sprint Goal is missedForecast from recent velocity adjusted for capacity, and leave some slack
Treating the sprint backlog as fixedThe team follows an outdated plan instead of learningUpdate it daily; renegotiate scope with the Product Owner when needed
Managers or stakeholders adding work directlyUndermines the Developers' ownership and the Sprint GoalSend new requests to the Product Owner
Pre-assigning every task in Sprint PlanningCreates bottlenecks and discourages collaborationLet Developers pull the next task when they are free
Starting everything at onceMany items are 90% done at the end and none is usableLimit work in progress and finish items in order
Tasks that only cover buildingTesting and release work appear late and push items past the SprintPlan every step needed to meet the Definition of Done
Board not updatedThe Daily Scrum becomes guessworkMove cards as work happens; check the board at every Daily Scrum
Hidden workSupport, meetings and side requests eat capacity invisiblyPut all significant work on the board, marked as unplanned if necessary
Using the sprint backlog as a management reportPeople optimise for looking busy rather than for the goalReport progress toward goals at the Sprint Review instead

Scope that keeps growing inside a Sprint is a form of scope creep. The Sprint Goal is your best defence.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sprint backlog in Scrum?

It is the Developers' plan for the Sprint: the Sprint Goal (why), the Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint (what) and an actionable plan for delivering the Increment (how). Its commitment is the Sprint Goal.

Who owns the sprint backlog?

The Developers. The Scrum Guide describes it as a plan by and for the Developers, and they are accountable for creating it and keeping it up to date.

When is the sprint backlog created?

In Sprint Planning at the start of each Sprint. It is then updated throughout the Sprint as the Developers learn more.

What are the three parts of the sprint backlog?

The Sprint Goal, the selected Product Backlog items and the Developers' plan for delivering them.

Can the sprint backlog change during the Sprint?

Yes. The plan and tasks change as the Developers learn, and scope can be renegotiated with the Product Owner. The Sprint Goal stays fixed and quality does not decrease.

Can the Product Owner change the sprint backlog?

Not directly. The Product Owner can clarify items and negotiate scope with the Developers, but the Developers own the sprint backlog. If the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete, only the Product Owner can cancel the Sprint.

What is the difference between the sprint backlog and the product backlog?

The product backlog lists everything that might improve the product, belongs to the Product Owner and lasts as long as the product. The sprint backlog covers one Sprint, belongs to the Developers and is committed to the Sprint Goal.

Should the sprint backlog include tasks?

It is optional. The Scrum Guide only requires an actionable plan. Many teams break items into tasks of a day or less so progress is easy to inspect at the Daily Scrum.

How many items should be in a sprint backlog?

There is no fixed number. Select as many items as the Developers forecast they can finish, using recent velocity and capacity as a guide. Many teams find several smaller items work better than one or two large ones.

How often should the sprint backlog be updated?

Continuously, and at least once a day. It should be current enough to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal at every Daily Scrum.

What happens to unfinished sprint backlog items?

They return to the Product Backlog, where the Product Owner re-orders them. They do not carry over to the next Sprint automatically, and their points do not count toward velocity.

Do retrospective improvements go in the sprint backlog?

They can. The Scrum Guide says the most impactful improvements may be added to the sprint backlog for the next Sprint.

Is a sprint burndown chart required?

No. The Scrum Guide mentions burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flow as possible practices but requires none of them. Use one if it helps the team see whether the Sprint Goal is on track.
