Empiricism in Scrum: The 3 Pillars Explained with Examples

Empiricism in Scrum means making decisions based on what has actually been observed, not on what was predicted, using three pillars: transparency, inspection and adaptation. The 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on empiricism and Lean thinking, and every Scrum event exists to inspect and adapt. This guide explains what that means in practice, why complex work needs it, how each pillar works, and how to strengthen empiricism in your own team, with worked examples and a self-assessment.

Key takeaways

  • Empiricism asserts that knowledge comes from experience and making decisions based on what is observed. Scrum is founded on it, together with Lean thinking.
  • Its three pillars depend on each other: transparency enables inspection, and inspection enables adaptation. Remove one and the others stop working.
  • Empirical process control suits complex work where outcomes cannot be predicted reliably; defined process control suits simple, repeatable work.
  • Every Scrum event is a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt, and the Sprint is the container that sets the rhythm.
  • The three artifacts and their commitments (Product Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of Done) make the work transparent enough to inspect.
  • Adaptation is hardest when people are not empowered or self-managing, so empiricism depends on how the organisation treats the team, not just on the events.

What is empiricism in Scrum?

Empiricism in Scrum is the principle that knowledge comes from experience and that decisions should be based on what is observed. Instead of trying to predict everything up front, a Scrum Team makes work visible, inspects real results frequently and adapts its product, plan and process as soon as it learns something new.

Scrum puts this into practice through short, fixed-length Sprints. Each Sprint produces a usable Increment that people can see and use, and each one ends with evidence: what was built, what users and stakeholders think of it, and how well the team worked. That evidence shapes the next Sprint. The Scrum Guide describes this as an iterative, incremental approach used to optimise predictability and to control risk.

A simple analogy is driving at night. You cannot see the whole road, so you do not plan every turn of the steering wheel before you set off. You look at what the headlights show you, check where you are, and correct your course continuously. The destination stays fixed; the route is adjusted to what you see. In Scrum the destination is the Product Goal, the headlights are the events and artifacts, and the corrections are adaptations to the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the way the team works.

What the Scrum Guide 2020 says about empiricism

The 2020 Scrum Guide, still the current official version (checked 26 October 2026), covers empiricism in its 'Scrum Theory' section. The table sums up each statement and what it means for your team.

The Guide also makes a point about forecasting: practices such as burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flow diagrams are useful, but they do not replace the importance of empiricism. In complex environments, only what has already happened may be used for forward-looking decisions.

Why complex work needs empiricism

Some work is predictable. Baking the same cake from the same recipe in the same oven gives the same result every time, so a detailed plan works. Other work, such as building a new product for users whose needs are only partly known, has more unknowns than knowns. Requirements change as people see results, technology behaves unexpectedly and the market moves. A detailed plan made at the start soon goes out of date.

Empirical vs defined process control

Defined process controlEmpirical process control
SuitsSimple, repeatable work with known inputs and outputsComplex work where inputs, methods and outcomes vary
How you control itFollow a detailed plan and procedureInspect results frequently and adjust
Where knowledge comes fromUp-front analysis and past repetitionsExperience gained while doing the work
Planning horizonLong and detailedShort and detailed, long and coarse
Response to varianceTreated as an error to be corrected back to planTreated as information that may change the plan
ExamplesManufacturing a standard part; payroll runsNew product development; research; marketing experiments

Where the idea came from

In his 1995 paper 'SCRUM Development Process', Ken Schwaber drew on industrial process control, which distinguishes processes that are fully defined from those that must be treated as empirical 'black boxes'. He argued that systems development behaves like the second kind and described the Sprint as an empirical process. The idea has stayed at the centre of every edition of the Scrum Guide since 2010.

Recognising complex work

Many coaches use Dave Snowden's Cynefin framework to explain when empiricism is needed. In its 'complex' domain, cause and effect can only be understood in hindsight, so the recommended approach is to probe, sense and respond: run small experiments and learn from them. The Scrum Guide does not mention Cynefin, but the logic is the same. If your work is genuinely simple and repeatable, a defined approach may suit it better; see Scrum vs Waterfall.

The three pillars of empiricism

Scrum implements empiricism through three pillars. They are often listed separately, but each one only works with the others.

Transparency

Transparency means the process and the work are visible to everyone who does the work or receives it. In Scrum, important decisions are based on the perceived state of the three artifacts, so the Scrum Guide warns that artifacts with low transparency can lead to decisions that diminish value and increase risk.

What needs to be transparent:

  • The Product Backlog and Product Goal, so stakeholders can see what is planned and why.
  • The Sprint Backlog and Sprint Goal, so the Developers and anyone interested can see the real state of the Sprint.
  • The Increment and the Definition of Done, so everyone knows what 'Done' means and what was actually delivered.
  • Problems and impediments, including bad news. Transparency is as much about honesty as about boards and tools.

Signs of low transparency: work that is '90% done' for days, a board updated only before meetings, items marked Done that still need testing, and risks that first appear at the Sprint Review.

Inspection

Inspection means the artifacts and progress toward agreed goals are inspected frequently and diligently to detect undesirable variances or problems. Scrum provides a cadence for inspection through its five events, but the team can inspect whenever it needs to.

Inspection is done by the people who do and receive the work: the Developers at the Daily Scrum, the whole Scrum Team and stakeholders at the Sprint Review, and the Scrum Team at the Retrospective. It is not an audit or a reason for managers to check on individuals. Inspecting too often or too intrusively can get in the way of the work, so the events are time-boxed.

Adaptation

Adaptation means changing the process or the product when inspection shows something has gone outside acceptable limits. The Scrum Guide says the adjustment must be made as soon as possible to minimise further deviation, and that a Scrum Team is expected to adapt the moment it learns anything new.

Typical adaptations include re-ordering the Product Backlog, changing the plan in the Sprint Backlog, renegotiating scope with the Product Owner, changing a working practice or strengthening the Definition of Done. Adaptation depends on authority: the Guide notes that it becomes more difficult when the people involved are not empowered or self-managing.

Tip

A quick test after any Scrum event: what is different now because of it? If the honest answer is 'nothing', the team inspected but did not adapt.

How the pillars depend on each other

The pillars form a chain. If one link is weak, the whole chain fails.

If this is missingWhat happensExample
TransparencyInspection is based on a false picture, so it is misleading and wastefulThe Sprint Review shows a feature as finished, but it has not been tested; stakeholders approve the wrong next step
InspectionProblems stay hidden until they are expensiveThe team skips Daily Scrums in a busy week and discovers on day 9 that two items are blocked
AdaptationThe team learns but nothing improves; inspection becomes pointlessThe same impediment is raised in three Retrospectives and nobody acts on it

This is why Scrum's events, artifacts and accountabilities are designed together. Remove any one of them and you lose part of the feedback loop.

Lean thinking: the second foundation

The 2020 Scrum Guide added Lean thinking alongside empiricism: an approach that reduces waste and focuses on the essentials. The two support each other. Short Sprints and small items reduce the amount of work at risk, and reducing waste (half-finished work, unused features, waiting and hand-offs) makes the evidence clearer and faster to collect.

In practice, Lean thinking shows up in Scrum as finishing work rather than starting more, keeping the Product Backlog free of items that no longer serve the Product Goal, and building only what is needed to learn the next thing. For a fuller comparison, read our guide to Scrum vs Lean.

Empiricism in every Scrum event

The Scrum Guide describes each event as a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt Scrum artifacts. The table shows what each event inspects and adapts.

EventWhat is inspectedWhat is adaptedHow often
The SprintProgress toward the Product Goal, one Sprint at a timeEverything below; the Sprint itself can be cancelled by the Product Owner if its goal becomes obsoleteContinuously; one month or less
Sprint PlanningProduct Backlog, latest Increment, capacity and past performanceSprint Goal and Sprint BacklogStart of each Sprint
Daily ScrumProgress toward the Sprint GoalThe Sprint Backlog and the plan for the next dayEvery working day
Sprint ReviewThe Increment, progress toward the Product Goal and changes in the environmentThe Product BacklogEnd of each Sprint
Sprint RetrospectiveIndividuals, interactions, processes, tools and the Definition of DoneHow the team works; improvements may go into the next Sprint BacklogEnd of each Sprint

Backlog refinement is not an event, but it is also a form of inspection and adaptation: the team looks at upcoming items, learns more about them and changes them. For the order in which all of this happens, see the Scrum process step by step.

Why are the events time-boxed and regular? Because empiricism needs a dependable rhythm. The Scrum Guide says events are used to create regularity and minimise the need for meetings not defined in Scrum. It also notes that when a Sprint's horizon is too long, the Sprint Goal may become invalid, complexity may rise and risk may increase. Shorter Sprints give more learning cycles; our guide to the Sprint cycle helps you choose a length.

Artifacts and commitments: the basis for transparency

The Scrum Guide says the artifacts are designed to maximise transparency of key information, so that everyone inspecting them has the same basis for adaptation. Each artifact has a commitment that gives inspection a reference point.

ArtifactCommitmentWhat it makes transparentInspected at
Product BacklogProduct GoalWhat the team believes is needed next, in order, and the long-term objectiveSprint Planning, Sprint Review, refinement
Sprint BacklogSprint GoalThe single objective of the Sprint and the Developers' current planDaily Scrum, throughout the Sprint
IncrementDefinition of DoneExactly what has been finished to the required qualitySprint Review; whenever it is released

The Definition of Done deserves special mention. Without it, 'Done' means different things to different people, and the Sprint Review inspects an illusion. A strong, shared Definition of Done is one of the most effective ways to improve transparency.

How the Scrum values make empiricism work

The Scrum Guide links the five values (commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage) directly to empiricism: when they are embodied by the Scrum Team and the people they work with, the pillars of transparency, inspection and adaptation 'come to life', building trust.

  • Openness makes transparency real: people share bad news, not just good news.
  • Courage makes adaptation possible: telling a stakeholder that a feature is not ready, or dropping an item that no longer makes sense.
  • Respect keeps inspection safe: the work is inspected, not the people.
  • Focus and commitment keep inspection pointed at the Sprint Goal and Product Goal rather than at activity.

Worked examples of empiricism in Scrum

The examples below are illustrative. Each one shows the three pillars in sequence.

Example 1: a product decision at the Sprint Review

PillarWhat happened
TransparencyThe team released a new two-page sign-up flow and added a simple funnel report to the Increment, so everyone could see how many people finished each page
InspectionAt the Sprint Review, the report showed that most people who started the sign-up abandoned it on the second page
AdaptationThe Product Owner moved 'shorten the second page' to the top of the Product Backlog, above two new features that had been planned for the next Sprint

The original plan said to build the new features. The evidence said to fix the sign-up first. Empiricism means following the evidence.

Example 2: re-planning at the Daily Scrum

PillarWhat happened
TransparencyOn day 6 of a ten-day Sprint, the board showed an integration item still in progress after three days, with a note that the supplier's test system was down
InspectionAt the Daily Scrum, the Developers agreed the Sprint Goal was at risk if they waited
AdaptationTwo Developers built a temporary stub so testing could continue; the Scrum Master took the supplier issue away to get it resolved; the team agreed with the Product Owner to drop a low-priority item to protect the Sprint Goal

Example 3: a process change at the Retrospective

PillarWhat happened
TransparencyThe team tracked how long items waited for code review; the chart was visible on the board
InspectionAt the Retrospective, they saw items waited about two days on average for review, and two items had missed the Sprint because of it
AdaptationThey agreed to review before starting new work each morning and added the change to the next Sprint Backlog; at the following Retrospective the waiting time was checked again

A simple way to keep this loop visible is to record each improvement and its result in a sprint retrospective template.

Example 4: forecasting from actual data

A team was asked when a set of features worth about 120 story points would be ready. Before the first Sprint, a manager had assumed 30 points a Sprint, which suggested four Sprints. After three Sprints, the team had actually completed 18, 22 and 20 points.

ApproachAssumed rate per SprintForecast for 120 points
Original plan30 points4 Sprints
Empirical forecast from the last three SprintsAbout 20 points (range 18 to 22)About 6 Sprints (between 5.5 and 6.7)

The Product Owner shared the range with stakeholders and re-ordered the backlog so the most valuable features would be in the first release whatever happened. The forecast was updated every Sprint as new data arrived. This is the Scrum Guide's point about forecasting: past performance informs the forecast, but in complex work it is still only a forecast.

How to strengthen empiricism in your team, step by step

Most teams that struggle with Scrum have the events in their calendar but weak empiricism. The steps below are our recommended way to improve it, one Sprint at a time.

  1. Check the state of transparency. Walk the Scrum board with the team. Does it match reality? Is the Definition of Done written down, and does every Done item meet it?
  2. Make the goals visible. Put the Product Goal and the current Sprint Goal at the top of the board or the start of every event.
  3. Tighten the Definition of Done. If work regularly needs 'finishing' after a Sprint, add those steps to the Definition of Done.
  4. Shorten feedback loops. Split items so they finish in a few days, get users or stakeholders to the Sprint Review, and consider shorter Sprints if feedback arrives too late.
  5. End every event with a decision. Close the Daily Scrum with the plan for the day, the Sprint Review with changes to the Product Backlog and the Retrospective with one or two concrete improvements.
  6. Follow up adaptations. Track improvements from the Retrospective in the Sprint Backlog and check at the next Retrospective whether they worked.
  7. Remove barriers to adaptation. Work with managers so the team can change its own plan and practices; the Scrum Master leads this with the wider organisation.

Self-assessment: how empirical is your team?

QuestionHealthy answer
Could a stakeholder understand the state of the Sprint from the board without asking?Yes, at any time
Does every item marked Done meet the Definition of Done?Yes, without exceptions
Did the last Sprint Review change the Product Backlog?Yes, based on feedback or data
Did the team change its plan during the last Sprint because of something it learned?Yes, when needed, without waiting for permission
Can you name a Retrospective improvement that is now normal practice?Yes, and the team checked whether it helped
Do forecasts use the team's actual results?Yes, and they are updated every Sprint

If you answer 'no' to two or more, start with transparency: it is the pillar the others depend on.

Common pitfalls that undermine empiricism

PitfallPillar weakenedWhat to do instead
Updating the board just before meetingsTransparencyUpdate as work moves; walk the board in the Daily Scrum
Calling work Done when testing or review is outstandingTransparencyApply the Definition of Done strictly and return unfinished items to the Product Backlog
Hiding bad news until the Sprint ReviewTransparencyRaise risks as soon as they are known; make it safe to do so
Skipping events when the team is busyInspectionKeep the events and shorten them; busy times are when feedback matters most
Sprint Reviews with no real stakeholdersInspectionInvite the people who use or pay for the product; show working software, not slides
Using inspection to judge individualsInspectionInspect the work and the process; keep the focus on the Sprint Goal
Retrospective actions that are never doneAdaptationPick one or two improvements and add them to the next Sprint Backlog
Plans that cannot change without approval from outside the teamAdaptationAgree with management what the team can decide for itself
Treating the initial release plan as a commitmentAdaptationForecast from actual data and update the forecast every Sprint

Teams that keep Scrum's events but lose its empiricism, and with it the agile mindset, are sometimes described as doing 'zombie Scrum' or 'mechanical Scrum': the meetings happen, but nothing changes as a result.

Empiricism beyond the Scrum Team

Empiricism works best when the wider organisation uses it too. If managers demand fixed scope, dates and budgets agreed a year in advance, the team can inspect all it likes but has little room to adapt.

  • Leaders can ask for evidence (Increments, user data, actual delivery rates) rather than status reports and percentage-complete estimates.
  • Stakeholders can attend Sprint Reviews and accept that the Product Backlog will change as a result.
  • Metrics should measure outcomes and value, not activity. Our guide to Scrum metrics and KPIs suggests which to use.
  • Funding and governance can be set per Product Goal or per quarter, with regular reviews, rather than locked to a detailed plan.

Scrum.org's Evidence-Based Management guide applies the same thinking at organisational level, using four key value areas: current value, unrealised value, ability to innovate and time to market. It is a complementary framework, not part of the Scrum Guide.

If you are introducing Scrum in an organisation used to predictive planning, explaining empiricism first often makes the rest of the framework easier to accept.

Frequently asked questions

What is empiricism in Scrum?

The principle that knowledge comes from experience and decisions should be based on what is observed. A Scrum Team makes work transparent, inspects real results frequently and adapts its product, plan and process.

What are the three pillars of empiricism in Scrum?

Transparency, inspection and adaptation. Transparency enables inspection, and inspection enables adaptation, so the three only work together.

Why is Scrum based on empiricism?

Because Scrum is designed for complex work, where there are more unknowns than knowns and long-range predictions soon become inaccurate. Short cycles of inspecting real results and adapting keep mistakes small and learning fast.

What is empirical process control?

A way of controlling work by observing actual results and adjusting, rather than following a detailed plan made in advance. Scrum applies empirical process control through Sprints, events and artifacts.

What is the difference between empirical and defined process control?

Defined process control follows a fixed procedure that gives predictable results, which suits simple, repeatable work. Empirical process control relies on frequent inspection and adaptation, which suits complex work where outcomes vary.

Are the Scrum pillars the same as the Scrum values?

No. The pillars (transparency, inspection, adaptation) describe how empiricism works. The values (commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage) describe the behaviours that make the pillars work in practice.

Which Scrum event is not an opportunity for inspection and adaptation?

None. The Scrum Guide describes every event as a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt, with the Sprint acting as the container for the other four.

Who inspects in Scrum?

The people who do and receive the work. The Developers inspect progress at the Daily Scrum, the Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the Increment at the Sprint Review, and the Scrum Team inspects its process at the Retrospective.

What does 'inspection without transparency is misleading' mean?

If the artifacts do not show the real state of the work, for example items marked Done that are not finished, any inspection is based on a false picture and leads to poor decisions.

How does the Definition of Done support empiricism?

It makes the state of the Increment transparent, so everyone knows exactly what Done means. Without it, the Sprint Review inspects work that may not really be finished.

How does Lean thinking relate to empiricism in the Scrum Guide?

The 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on both. Empiricism bases decisions on observation; Lean thinking reduces waste and focuses on the essentials, which makes feedback faster and clearer.

Can empiricism work with long Sprints?

It can, but more slowly. The Scrum Guide warns that when a Sprint's horizon is too long, the Sprint Goal may become invalid, complexity may rise and risk may increase. Shorter Sprints give more learning cycles.

Is empiricism only for software teams?

No. Any team doing complex work with uncertain outcomes, such as marketing, research or product design, can use empirical process control. The 2020 Scrum Guide deliberately removed most software-specific language so it applies to any complex work.
