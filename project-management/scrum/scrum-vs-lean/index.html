Scrum vs Lean: Key Differences and How to Combine Them

Scrum vs Lean is not a straight contest: Lean is a broad management philosophy for maximising customer value and removing waste across a whole value stream, while Scrum is a lightweight framework for teams doing complex product work in fixed-length Sprints. The two overlap heavily, and the 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on empiricism and Lean thinking. This guide compares them side by side, explains where each fits and shows, step by step and with a worked example, how to use Lean thinking to get more from Scrum.

Key takeaways

  • Lean is a philosophy and management system from the Toyota Production System; Scrum is a team-level framework first presented in 1995.
  • The Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on empiricism and Lean thinking, which "reduces waste and focuses on the essentials".
  • Scrum prescribes accountabilities, events, artifacts and a Sprint cadence; Lean prescribes no team structure or iterations.
  • Both value customer focus, small batches, visible work, continuous improvement and respect for people.
  • Use Scrum inside product teams and Lean tools such as value stream mapping and WIP limits to improve the wider system around them.
  • Many teams find their biggest delays sit outside the Sprint, which only a Lean view of the whole value stream reveals.

Scrum vs Lean at a glance

The main difference between Scrum and Lean is scope. Lean is a way of thinking about an entire organisation's value stream, focused on flow and eliminating waste. Scrum is a specific framework that tells one small, cross-functional team how to organise its work into Sprints with defined accountabilities, events and artifacts. Lean answers "how should the whole system work?"; Scrum answers "how should this team deliver and learn?".

Because Scrum already uses Lean thinking, most organisations do not choose one or the other. They use Scrum inside product teams and Lean principles to improve how work reaches and leaves those teams.

What is Lean?

Lean is a management approach that aims to deliver the most value to customers with the least waste, by making work flow smoothly and improving continuously. It grew out of the Toyota Production System, developed from the late 1940s by Taiichi Ohno, Eiji Toyoda and colleagues, when Toyota needed to build cars efficiently in small volumes. The word "lean" itself was coined by researcher John Krafcik in 1988 and popularised by *The Machine That Changed the World* (1990) by Womack, Jones and Roos.

The five Lean principles

In *Lean Thinking* (1996), James Womack and Daniel Jones summarised Lean as five steps, which the Lean Enterprise Institute still teaches:

  1. Specify value from the end customer's point of view.
  2. Identify the value stream: every step needed to deliver that value, and remove the steps that add none.
  3. Make value flow through the remaining steps without interruptions, queues or rework.
  4. Let the customer pull work, so nothing is produced before it is needed.
  5. Pursue perfection by repeating the cycle, steadily removing more waste.

The seven wastes

Ohno identified seven forms of waste (in Japanese, *muda*): transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing and defects. Many practitioners add an eighth: unused talent. Toyota later summarised its philosophy, in *The Toyota Way 2001*, as two pillars: continuous improvement and respect for people.

Lean also brought tools that are now common in project work: kanban systems, Just-in-Time production, value stream mapping, 5S, root cause analysis with the Five Whys, and kaizen events. For the manufacturing roots, see our guide to Lean manufacturing; for applying Lean to projects, read Lean project management.

Lean in software and knowledge work

In 2003, Mary and Tom Poppendieck's *Lean Software Development: An Agile Toolkit* translated Lean for software. They described seven principles:

  • Eliminate waste.
  • Amplify learning.
  • Decide as late as possible.
  • Deliver as fast as possible.
  • Empower the team.
  • Build integrity in.
  • Optimise the whole.

Physical waste is easy to see on a factory floor. In knowledge work it hides in backlogs, queues and inboxes. The table maps Ohno's manufacturing wastes to their common knowledge-work equivalents, following the approach the Poppendiecks popularised.

Manufacturing wasteKnowledge-work equivalentExample

OverproductionExtra featuresBuilding options nobody asked for or uses
Over-processingExtra processesDuplicate sign-offs, reports nobody reads
TransportHandoffsWork passed from analysts to developers to testers to operations
MotionTask switchingPeople split across several projects at once
WaitingWaiting / delaysWaiting for decisions, environments or another team
DefectsDefectsBugs found late, rework after release

Lean ideas also shaped the Kanban Method for knowledge work, the theory of constraints mindset many teams use and the pull system that underpins both Kanban and Scrum's Sprint Planning.

What is Scrum?

Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organisations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. A small Scrum Team, made up of a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and Developers, works in Sprints of one month or less. Each Sprint contains Sprint Planning, Daily Scrums, a Sprint Review and a Sprint Retrospective, and produces at least one usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done.

Scrum is defined in the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. The current version dates from November 2020. Scrum was first presented publicly in 1995 and took its name from Takeuchi and Nonaka's 1986 Harvard Business Review article "The New New Product Development Game", which compared high-performing product teams, many of them at Japanese manufacturers, to a rugby scrum.

For the full framework, see what is Scrum and Scrum team roles. For how it compares with Agile more broadly, read Agile vs Scrum.

How the Scrum Guide uses Lean thinking

The 2020 Scrum Guide opens its theory section with: "Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking. Empiricism asserts that knowledge comes from experience and making decisions based on what is observed. Lean thinking reduces waste and focuses on the essentials." The Guide does not go further, but many Scrum rules are easy to read as Lean ideas in practice.

Scrum elementLean idea it reflects
Short Sprints with a usable Increment each timeSmall batches and fast feedback; less partially done work

Ordered Product Backlog and a Product GoalSpecify value; focus on the essentials

Sprint RetrospectiveContinuous improvement (kaizen)
Time-boxed eventsReduce waiting and over-processing in meetings
Transparent artifactsVisual management; make problems visible

The other foundation, empiricism, has three pillars: transparency, inspection and adaptation. Our guides to empiricism in Scrum and Scrum principles and values explain them in detail.

Key differences between Scrum and Lean

Scope: system versus team

Lean looks at the whole value stream: how a customer request travels through sales, design, development, approval, release and support. Scrum focuses on a single team and its product. A Scrum Team can be very efficient within the Sprint while most of the delay happens before work reaches it or after it leaves. Lean is the lens that finds those delays.

Structure and cadence

Scrum is prescriptive about a few things: three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts and their commitments, and a fixed Sprint length. Lean prescribes no roles, meetings or iterations. Lean-based systems such as Kanban usually work in continuous flow, releasing whenever an item is ready, with planning and review happening on their own cadences.

Roles

Scrum defines who is accountable for what: the Product Owner for maximising value and managing the Product Backlog, the Scrum Master for the team's effectiveness, and the Developers for creating a usable Increment each Sprint. Lean organisations often have value stream owners or improvement leaders, but Lean itself does not require any team roles.

Planning and change

Scrum plans in Sprint-sized chunks around a Sprint Goal. The plan can change during the Sprint, but nothing should endanger the Sprint Goal, so most new requests wait for the next Sprint Planning. Lean favours deciding as late as responsibly possible and pulling the next most valuable item whenever capacity frees up.

Measures

Scrum teams commonly track Sprint Goal success, velocity and burndowns, although the Scrum Guide mandates none of them. Lean emphasises end-to-end flow: lead time, cycle time, throughput and flow efficiency.

Improvement

Scrum guarantees an improvement conversation at least once per Sprint through the Retrospective. Lean expects improvement to happen continuously and anywhere, from small daily changes to structured kaizen events, often using root cause analysis.

What Scrum and Lean have in common

  • Customer value as the goal. Lean starts by specifying value; Scrum orders the Product Backlog by value and inspects outcomes at the Sprint Review.
  • Small batches and fast feedback. Lean reduces batch size to improve flow; Scrum delivers Increments every Sprint.
  • Built-in quality. Lean stops to fix problems at the source; Scrum will not release work that fails the Definition of Done.
  • Continuous improvement. Kaizen in Lean; the Sprint Retrospective in Scrum.
  • Respect for people. Lean treats it as a pillar; Scrum's values include respect, and teams are self-managing.
  • Visible work. Lean uses visual management; Scrum requires transparent artifacts, often on a board.
  • Pull. Lean lets demand pull work; Scrum Developers pull items into the Sprint rather than having work assigned.

These shared roots explain why Scrum and Lean tools mix so easily, and why the Agile Manifesto drew on both Lean manufacturing and 1990s methods such as Scrum.

When to use Scrum, Lean or both

The question is rarely "Scrum or Lean?" but "where does a fixed Sprint cadence help, and where would continuous flow serve better?". This is our guidance, not a rule from either body of knowledge.

SituationBetter fitWhy
New product with uncertain requirementsScrumSprints, Sprint Goals and Reviews create regular learning loops
Team needs structure, clear accountability and a shared rhythmScrumDefined events and accountabilities give a simple starting point
Steady stream of small, unpredictable requests (support, operations)Lean / KanbanContinuous flow avoids waiting for the next Sprint
Delays come mostly from handoffs between departmentsLeanValue stream mapping covers the whole system, not one team
Repeatable process with known stepsLeanStandard work and waste removal improve predictable processes
Several Scrum teams with long end-to-end lead timesBothScrum in the teams, Lean across the value stream
Scrum team with good velocity but slow delivery to usersBothLean exposes the waiting before and after the Sprint

Five questions to choose your approach

If you are setting up a new team or rethinking how an existing one works, answer these questions in order. They are our own checklist, drawn from the differences above.

  1. How uncertain is the work? If the team is discovering what to build and expects requirements to change as it learns, Scrum's Sprint Goals and Sprint Reviews give structured feedback. If the work is well understood and repeatable, Lean flow and standard work are usually enough.
  2. How do requests arrive? Work that can be planned in batches every week or two suits Sprints. Work that arrives unpredictably and must start within hours, such as incidents or service requests, suits continuous flow with WIP limits.
  3. Where does the delay happen? Measure lead time from request to delivery and compare it with your Sprint length. If most of the time is spent outside the team, start with a Lean value stream view before changing anything inside the team.
  4. Does the team need structure or freedom? A new or struggling team often benefits from Scrum's clear accountabilities and events. A mature team with good flow may prefer the lighter structure of a Lean or Kanban system.
  5. Who needs to be involved? Scrum changes how one team works. Lean improvements often need people from several departments, so they need sponsorship from someone who can change the wider system.

Two short examples show how the answers play out:

  • A product team building a new customer portal faces high uncertainty, plans work every two weeks and needs a regular rhythm with stakeholders. Scrum is the natural core, with WIP limits and cycle time added once the basics are stable.
  • An IT operations team handling access requests and incidents receives unpredictable, small items that need fast turnaround. A Lean, Kanban-style flow system fits better, with a regular improvement meeting borrowed from the Scrum Retrospective.

Many organisations end up with both patterns side by side, sharing the same Lean improvement practices across teams.

How to combine Lean and Scrum step by step

This process adds Lean thinking to an existing Scrum Team without changing the Scrum framework itself.

  1. Define value with the Product Owner. Make sure the Product Goal and the top of the Product Backlog describe customer outcomes, not just features. Remove backlog items nobody can explain the value of.
  2. Map the end-to-end value stream. With the team and people from upstream and downstream, walk one typical item from request to use. Record each step, who does it, active work time and waiting time. Our guide to value stream mapping shows how.
  3. Measure lead time and flow efficiency. Lead time is request to delivery; flow efficiency is active time ÷ lead time × 100. Compare lead time with Sprint length to see how much of the delay sits outside the Sprint.
  4. Pick the biggest queue, not the easiest fix. Use the map to find the longest wait or the most rework. That is your first improvement target.
  5. Limit work in progress inside the Sprint. Agree a WIP limit on the Sprint board so Developers finish items before starting new ones. This cuts partially done work and shortens cycle time.
  6. Bring work into the team to reduce handoffs. Where a separate team tests, reviews or releases, aim to include those skills in the Scrum Team and in the Definition of Done.
  7. Use the Retrospective for waste hunting. Every few Sprints, review the seven wastes as a checklist and pick one to reduce. Our Sprint Retrospective template has space for this.
  8. Find root causes. For recurring problems, use the Five Whys or a fishbone diagram before agreeing an action. The root cause analysis template helps structure it.
  9. Track flow metrics alongside Scrum metrics. Add cycle time and lead time next to Sprint Goal success and velocity, and review them at the Sprint Review and Retrospective.
  10. Repeat. Re-map the value stream every few months, as Lean's fifth principle suggests.
Tip

Keep the Scrum framework intact while you add Lean practices. WIP limits, flow metrics and value stream maps are complementary practices; they do not replace Sprints, the Sprint Goal or any Scrum event.

Worked example: finding waste around a Scrum team

The figures below are an illustrative example, not data from a real organisation. A Scrum Team in a mid-sized company works in two-week Sprints (10 working days) and has a stable velocity. Stakeholders still complain that requested changes take "months". The team maps one typical feature from approval to use.

StepActive work (days)Waiting (days)
Approved request waits in the Product Backlog030
Refinement with the Product Owner0.50
Waits for the next Sprint Planning05
Built during the Sprint46
Separate test team checks it14
Waits for the monthly release window0.59
Total654

Lead time is 6 + 54 = 60 working days. Flow efficiency is 6 ÷ 60 × 100 = 10%. The Sprint itself accounts for only 10 of the 60 days, so improving velocity alone could never fix the stakeholders' complaint.

The team agreed four changes over the next few Sprints:

  • Shorter backlog queue. The Product Owner removed low-value items and refined only the next few Sprints' worth of work in detail, cutting the backlog wait.
  • Testing inside the team. A tester joined the Scrum Team and automated regression tests were added to the Definition of Done, removing the separate test handoff.
  • WIP limit of three items on the Sprint board, reducing waiting inside the Sprint.
  • Release when done. The team worked with operations to automate deployment, so items release when they meet the Definition of Done rather than waiting for a monthly window.

Lean wastes as Scrum anti-patterns

The seven wastes make a practical checklist for a Retrospective. Each has a typical symptom in Scrum teams and a countermeasure from Scrum, Lean or both.

WasteHow it shows up in ScrumCountermeasure
Partially done workMany items in progress at Sprint end; work carried overWIP limits; smaller items; swarming
Extra featuresFeatures built without evidence of needProduct Goal focus; validate at Sprint Review
Extra processesStatus reports duplicating the Sprint Review; extra approvalsUse Scrum events instead of parallel meetings
HandoffsSeparate design, test or release teamsCross-functional team; stronger Definition of Done
Task switchingDevelopers split across several teams or projectsDedicated team members; fewer parallel goals
WaitingBlocked items, slow decisions, external dependenciesScrum Master removes impediments; map the value stream
DefectsEscaped defects and rework next SprintAutomated testing; Definition of Done; root cause analysis

Bring this table to your next Sprint Retrospective and ask which waste cost the team most in the last Sprint.

Scrum, Lean, Kanban and Lean Six Sigma: where each fits

Searchers often meet these terms together. They work at different levels.

ScrumTeam frameworkEmpiricism and Increments in SprintsComplex product development

Scaling frameworks such as the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) describe themselves in Lean-Agile terms and combine Scrum-style teams with Lean portfolio and flow practices. For the quality side, read Lean vs Six Sigma.

Common mistakes when comparing or combining Scrum and Lean

  • Treating them as rivals. Scrum already rests on Lean thinking; forcing a choice usually means losing useful tools from one side.
  • Calling Scrum "Lean" while ignoring waste outside the team. A fast Sprint inside a slow value stream still gives slow delivery.
  • Copying tools without the thinking. WIP limits or value stream maps without respect for people and genuine improvement become another layer of process.
  • Abandoning Scrum events in the name of flow. Dropping the Retrospective or Sprint Review removes the inspection points empiricism depends on.
  • Using Lean to cut people rather than waste. Lean's respect-for-people pillar is about improving the system, not headcount.
  • Measuring only velocity. Without lead time and flow efficiency, teams cannot see most of the delay customers experience.
  • Over-processing the combination. Adding every Lean tool at once creates the very waste Lean warns against. Start with one queue.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between Scrum and Lean?

Lean is a broad philosophy for maximising customer value and eliminating waste across a whole value stream. Scrum is a specific team framework with Sprints, accountabilities, events and artifacts for complex product work.

Is Scrum Lean or Agile?

Scrum is usually classed as an Agile framework, and both of its creators signed the Agile Manifesto. The Scrum Guide also says Scrum is founded on empiricism and Lean thinking, so it is Agile with Lean foundations rather than one of the classic Lean methods.

Does the Scrum Guide mention Lean?

Yes. The 2020 Scrum Guide says Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking, and that lean thinking reduces waste and focuses on the essentials. It does not prescribe specific Lean tools.

Can you use Lean and Scrum together?

Yes, and most organisations do. A common pattern is Scrum inside product teams, with Lean practices such as value stream mapping, WIP limits and flow metrics used to improve how work reaches and leaves those teams.

Which came first, Lean or Scrum?

Lean. It comes from the Toyota Production System, developed from the late 1940s, and the term "lean" dates from 1988. Scrum was first presented in 1995.

Is Kanban Lean or Scrum?

Kanban comes from Lean. It is a flow-based method rather than part of Scrum, though many Scrum teams borrow Kanban practices such as WIP limits and flow metrics.

Which is better, Lean or Scrum?

Neither is better in general. Scrum suits complex product work that benefits from a regular cadence of delivery and learning. Lean suits improving flow across a whole system or repeatable processes. Many organisations use both.

What are the five Lean principles?

As set out by Womack and Jones in Lean Thinking: specify value, identify the value stream, make value flow, let the customer pull, and pursue perfection.

What are the seven wastes in software development?

Following Mary and Tom Poppendieck: partially done work, extra processes, extra features, task switching, waiting, handoffs (motion) and defects.

Do Lean and Scrum define the same roles?

No. Scrum defines three accountabilities: Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. Lean does not prescribe team roles, although Lean organisations often have value stream owners or improvement leaders.

What is Lean Agile?

Lean Agile is a general term for combining Lean principles, such as flow and waste reduction, with Agile values and frameworks such as Scrum. Scaling frameworks such as SAFe describe themselves in Lean-Agile terms.

Is Lean Six Sigma the same as Scrum?

No. Lean Six Sigma is a process improvement method that combines waste reduction with statistical quality control. Scrum is a framework for delivering complex products in Sprints. They can coexist but solve different problems.

Which is faster, Lean or Scrum?

Neither is inherently faster. Lean shortens lead time across the whole system, while Scrum gives a regular cadence of delivery. Combining them usually gives the biggest gain, because many delays sit outside the Sprint.
