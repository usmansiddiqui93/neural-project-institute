Sprint Planning in Scrum: Agenda, Steps and Examples

Sprint Planning is the Scrum event that starts every Sprint: the whole Scrum Team agrees why the Sprint is valuable, what can be Done in it and how the work will get done. It is time-boxed to a maximum of eight hours for a one-month Sprint and produces the Sprint Backlog. This guide explains what the Scrum Guide 2020 requires, how to prepare, a step-by-step agenda, how to write a Sprint Goal and decide how much to take on, with a fully worked two-week example.

Key takeaways

  • Sprint Planning starts the Sprint and answers three questions: why is this Sprint valuable, what can be Done, and how will the chosen work get done.
  • The whole Scrum Team attends. The Product Owner brings the most important items; the Developers decide how much they can take on and how to do it.
  • The time-box is up to eight hours for a one-month Sprint; shorter Sprints usually need proportionally less.
  • The output is the Sprint Backlog: the Sprint Goal, the selected Product Backlog items and the Developers' plan. The Sprint Goal is the commitment.
  • Use past performance, upcoming capacity and the Definition of Done to forecast how much fits, and leave some slack for the unexpected.
  • Good refinement before the event is the single biggest factor in short, calm Sprint Planning.

What is Sprint Planning?

Sprint Planning is the first event of every Sprint in Scrum. The whole Scrum Team collaborates to agree a Sprint Goal that explains why the Sprint is valuable, the Product Backlog items the Developers forecast they can complete, and a plan for delivering them. Together these form the Sprint Backlog. It is time-boxed to eight hours for a one-month Sprint.

Sprint Planning is one of the five Scrum events. It happens at the very start of the Sprint, immediately after the previous Sprint ends, and it is followed by the Daily Scrum every working day, then the Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective at the end.

It is where the long-term view in the Product Backlog becomes a concrete plan for the next few weeks.

What the Scrum Guide 2020 says about Sprint Planning

The 2020 Scrum Guide is still the current official version (checked 26 October 2026). Its Sprint Planning section is short, so most of what teams do in the event is practice built around a few rules. The table summarises those rules and what they mean in practice.

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means for your team
Sprint Planning initiates the Sprint by laying out the work to be performedIt happens first; no Sprint work starts before it
The plan is created by the collaborative work of the entire Scrum TeamProduct Owner, Scrum Master and Developers all take part
It addresses three topics: why, what and howCover all three; skipping the "why" leaves you with a list of items, not a goal
The Sprint Goal must be finalised before the end of Sprint PlanningDo not leave the event without one
The more the Developers know about their past performance, upcoming capacity and Definition of Done, the more confident their Sprint forecastsBring data on recent Sprints and availability
How the work is done is at the sole discretion of the DevelopersNobody outside the Developers tells them how to build the Increment
Time-boxed to a maximum of eight hours for a one-month Sprint; usually shorter for shorter SprintsA maximum, not a target

The three topics of Sprint Planning

Topic one: why is this Sprint valuable?

The Product Owner proposes how the product could increase its value and utility in the current Sprint. The whole Scrum Team then collaborates to define a Sprint Goal that communicates why the Sprint is valuable to stakeholders. Start here: the goal tells the Developers which items matter most and gives them room to adjust the detail later.

Topic two: what can be Done this Sprint?

Through discussion with the Product Owner, the Developers select items from the Product Backlog to include in the Sprint. The Scrum Guide notes the Scrum Team may refine these items during this process, which increases understanding and confidence. Selecting how much to take on is the hardest part of the event; we cover it in detail below.

Topic three: how will the chosen work get done?

For each selected item, the Developers plan the work needed to create an Increment that meets the Definition of Done. They often break items into tasks of a day or less. How they do this is entirely their decision.

TopicLed byOutput
WhyProduct Owner proposes; whole Scrum Team agreesThe Sprint Goal
WhatDevelopers select, in discussion with the Product OwnerThe selected Product Backlog items (a forecast)
HowDevelopersA plan, often tasks of a day or less

The topics are not strictly sequential; teams often go back and forth. The firm rule is that all three are covered and the Sprint Goal is final before the event ends.

Who attends Sprint Planning and what each role does

Sprint Planning is for the whole Scrum Team. Each accountability brings something different.

WhoRole in Sprint PlanningDecides
Product OwnerBrings the ordered, refined Product Backlog; explains the Product Goal and the value of top items; proposes the Sprint's objective; answers questionsThe order of the Product Backlog
DevelopersAsk questions, size and refine items, select how much they can take on, plan how to do itHow much to take on and how to do the work
Scrum MasterMakes sure the event happens, stays within the time-box and is positive and productive; coaches the team on Sprint Goals and self-managementNothing about scope; the Scrum Master facilitates, not directs
Invited expertsGive advice on specific items, such as security, legal or dataNothing; they advise
Managers and stakeholdersNot participants by default; their input belongs in the Sprint Review and refinementNothing in this event

Who decides how much goes into the Sprint? The Developers. The Product Owner can negotiate on value but cannot make them take on more than they believe they can complete.

How long should Sprint Planning take?

The Scrum Guide sets a maximum of eight hours for a one-month Sprint and says the event is usually shorter for shorter Sprints. It does not give figures for other Sprint lengths. Many teams scale the maximum in proportion, as in the table, and aim well below it.

Sprint lengthProportional maximum (common practice)Typical well-run team
1 week2 hours45 to 90 minutes
2 weeks4 hours1.5 to 3 hours
3 weeks6 hours2 to 4 hours
4 weeks / 1 month8 hours (Scrum Guide maximum)3 to 6 hours

Rules of thumb vary. Atlassian suggests up to two hours per week of Sprint, while Mike Cohn of Mountain Goat Software suggests aiming for about 45 minutes per week of Sprint length and calls the eight-hour maximum far longer than most teams need.

Signs the event is running too long: the team is discovering what items mean for the first time, estimating everything from scratch, or trying to list every task for the whole Sprint. The fix for the first two is regular backlog refinement; the fix for the third is planning the first few days in detail and the rest in outline.

Outputs

  • A Sprint Goal: the single objective for the Sprint and the Developers' commitment.
  • The selected Product Backlog items: the Developers' forecast of what they will complete.
  • A plan for delivering them: often tasks for the first days, with the rest outlined.
  • Together these are the Sprint Backlog.

How to prepare for Sprint Planning

Most problems in Sprint Planning start before the event. These checklists are our own suggestions for the few days beforehand.

RoleBefore Sprint Planning
Product OwnerOrder the top of the Product Backlog; make sure roughly one to two Sprints' worth of items are refined and sized; draft a candidate Sprint objective; gather feedback from the Sprint Review and stakeholders
DevelopersTake part in refinement; flag items that still look too big or unclear; check their own availability; note technical work or debt that needs space
Scrum MasterBook the room or call and the time-box; collect availability; bring last Sprints' completed work and carry-over; remind the team of agreed retrospective actions
Whole Scrum TeamReview the Definition of Done and any changes to it; check known dependencies on other teams
Tip

A useful test from our backlog refinement guide: divide the points already refined at the top of the backlog by the team's average velocity. Below about one Sprint's worth, the next Sprint Planning is likely to stall.

How to run Sprint Planning, step by step

The Scrum Guide does not prescribe an agenda. The steps below are our recommended starting point for most teams; inspect and adapt them at your Retrospective.

  1. Open and set the frame. Restate the time-box, the Product Goal and what happened at the last Sprint Review.
  2. Confirm capacity and past performance. Share who is available, for how many days, and what the team completed in recent Sprints.
  3. Product Owner proposes the objective. Explain which outcome would add most value now and which items support it.
  4. Draft a Sprint Goal together. Write a short, outcome-focused sentence. Treat it as a draft until the end of the event.
  5. Select items. Developers pull items from the top of the backlog that serve the goal, asking questions and refining as needed, until they reach a realistic forecast.
  6. Check for dependencies and risks. Anything needing another team, a supplier or a specialist should be visible now.
  7. Plan the how. Break the first items into tasks of a day or less; outline the rest. Consider the order of work so testing starts early.
  8. Check the plan against capacity. If the tasks clearly exceed available time, or one skill is overloaded, remove or swap an item.
  9. Finalise the Sprint Goal. Reword it if selection has changed what is realistic.
  10. Confidence check and close. Ask each Developer how confident they are; agree what to do if it is low; update the Scrum board.

Sample agenda for a two-week Sprint (about 2 hours 45 minutes)

TimeStepLead
0:00 to 0:10Open: purpose, time-box, Product Goal, Sprint Review highlightsScrum Master and Product Owner
0:10 to 0:20Capacity and recent performanceDevelopers
0:20 to 0:35Product Owner proposes the Sprint's objectiveProduct Owner
0:35 to 0:50Draft the Sprint GoalWhole Scrum Team
0:50 to 1:35Select and clarify itemsDevelopers with the Product Owner
1:35 to 1:45Break
1:45 to 2:30Plan the how: tasks, order of work, risksDevelopers
2:30 to 2:45Finalise the Sprint Goal, confidence check, closeWhole Scrum Team

For a one-week Sprint, halve most blocks; for a four-week Sprint, consider two sessions rather than one long meeting.

How to write a good Sprint Goal

The Scrum Guide calls the Sprint Goal the single objective for the Sprint. It is a commitment by the Developers but gives them flexibility over the exact work needed to achieve it, and it encourages the Scrum Team to work together rather than on separate initiatives.

What makes a good Sprint Goal

  • Outcome-focused: describes a change for users or the business, not a list of tasks.
  • Single and coherent: one objective the selected items all support.
  • Achievable in one Sprint: the team believes it can meet it with some room to spare.
  • Negotiable in detail: several sets of items could achieve it, so scope can flex.
  • Understandable to stakeholders: someone outside the team can tell whether it was met.
  • Connected to the Product Goal: a step towards the larger objective.
Weak Sprint GoalWhy it is weakStronger version
Complete stories PB-112 to PB-119A list of items; no reason or flexibilityNew customers can sign up and verify their email without contacting support
Work on the reporting moduleActivity, not outcome; no way to tell when it is metFinance managers can export the monthly sales report to Excel
Fix bugs, update the API and start the redesignThree unrelated goalsPick the most valuable one, and treat the others as other items in the Sprint Backlog

A simple template some teams use: "By the end of this Sprint, [who] can [do what], so that [why]." Use it as a prompt, not a rule.

When the Sprint Goal is at risk

If the work turns out to be different from what was expected, the Developers collaborate with the Product Owner to renegotiate the scope of the Sprint Backlog without affecting the Sprint Goal. If the goal itself becomes obsolete, for example because a market change makes it pointless, only the Product Owner can cancel the Sprint. The Daily Scrum is where most teams first spot the risk.

Deciding how much work to take on

The Scrum Guide does not prescribe a method. It says the Developers' forecasts improve the more they know about their past performance, upcoming capacity and Definition of Done. In practice teams use one of three approaches, often in combination.

ApproachHow it worksStrengthsWatch out for
Velocity-basedTake items from the top of the backlog until their story points reach the recent average velocity, adjusted for availabilityQuick; uses real past resultsA single Sprint's velocity varies; ignores who is available for which skills
Capacity-based (commitment-driven)Break each item into tasks, estimate them in hours and stop when the team's available hours are used upReflects this Sprint's real availability and skillsSlower; hour estimates can become a target
Throughput-basedCount how many items of roughly similar size the team usually finishes per SprintSimple; no points neededWorks only if items are consistently small

Our recommended approach for most teams combines the first two: use velocity to set a rough range, then let the task plan and the Developers' judgement confirm it. Our guide to Agile capacity planning gives the formulas and a detailed example.

  1. Take the average velocity of the last three to five Sprints.
  2. Scale it by this Sprint's availability: available person-days divided by normal person-days.
  3. Use the lowest and highest recent Sprints to give a range rather than a single number.
  4. Select items up to the lower half of that range, so there is slack for the unexpected.
  5. Check the task plan and skills; adjust if one person or specialism is overloaded.
  6. Keep the next refined item ready at the top of the Product Backlog in case the team finishes early.
Tip

Plan to the recent average, never to your best Sprint ever. Planning at 100% of capacity leaves no room for support work, illness or learning, and repeated misses push teams to inflate estimates.

Worked example: planning a two-week Sprint

A Scrum Team is building a mobile app for a chain of fitness studios. It has a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and five Developers, and runs two-week Sprints of 10 working days. The Product Goal is: "Members can manage all their class bookings in the app without calling the front desk."

Step 1: inputs

  • Past performance: the last three Sprints delivered 28, 33 and 30 points, an average of about 30.
  • Capacity: normally 5 Developers × 10 days = 50 person-days. This Sprint one Developer is on leave for 4 days, so 46 person-days are available, a ratio of 0.92.
  • Forecast range: 30 × 0.92 ≈ 28 points as a central figure; 28 × 0.92 ≈ 26 to 33 × 0.92 ≈ 30 as a range.
  • Sprint Review feedback: members are frustrated that full classes simply show "Full" with no way to get a place.
  • Retrospective action: add automated tests around the booking service, after two booking bugs reached production.

Step 2: the Sprint Goal

The Product Owner proposes tackling full classes. After discussion, the Scrum Team agrees the goal: "Members can join a waiting list for a full class and are offered a place automatically when one opens."

Step 3: selecting items

OrderProduct Backlog itemPointsSupports the goal?Decision
1Join a waiting list for a full class8Yes, essentialSelected
2Offer the first person on the list a place, held for 30 minutes5Yes, essentialSelected
3Push notification when a place is offered5Yes, essentialSelected
4Leave a waiting list2YesSelected
5Automated tests for the booking service (retrospective action)3Indirectly; protects qualitySelected
6Show the member's position on the waiting list3Yes, nice to haveSelected; first to drop if needed
7Rate a class after attending5NoLeft at the top of the backlog

Items 1 to 6 total 26 points, at the bottom of the forecast range. The Developers deliberately stop there because nobody on the team has used the push notification service before, and they want slack for that uncertainty.

Step 4: planning the how

The Developers break the first two items into tasks of a day or less, such as the waiting-list API (1 day), the "Join waiting list" button (half a day) and the job that expires unaccepted offers (1 day). Later items are outlined for now. They start the push notification work on day 2, so problems with the new service appear early.

Step 5: final check

Planned tasks come to about 41 person-days against 46 available, and two Developers will write the automated tests so the tester is not overloaded. In a fist-of-five confidence check four Developers show four fingers and one shows three, citing the notification service. If notifications slip, item 6 is dropped and the Sprint Goal stays intact. The event took 2 hours 20 minutes.

Sprint Planning vs backlog refinement vs PI planning

These three are often confused because each involves choosing and preparing work. They operate at different levels.

Sprint PlanningBacklog refinementPI planning (SAFe)
TypeScrum eventOngoing activity in Scrum, not an eventEvent in the Scaled Agile Framework, not Scrum
HorizonOne SprintThe next one to two Sprints and beyondA Program Increment, typically 8 to 12 weeks
WhoOne Scrum TeamProduct Owner and Developers, plus experts as neededAll teams on an Agile Release Train and stakeholders
Main outputSprint Backlog with a Sprint GoalClear, sized, ordered Product Backlog itemsTeam and program objectives and a program board of dependencies
Time-boxUp to 8 hours for a one-month SprintNone in the Scrum GuideUsually two days

Refinement prepares options; Sprint Planning turns the best of them into a plan. If you work at scale, see our guide to PI planning, and for the bigger picture of when releases will land, Agile release planning.

Remote and hybrid Sprint Planning

  • Share one board. Plan directly in the tool the team will use during the Sprint, so the plan does not need to be copied afterwards.
  • Split long sessions. Two shorter calls (why and what, then how) work better online than one three-hour call. Keep both on the first day of the Sprint.
  • Prepare asynchronously. Share candidate items, the draft objective and availability a day ahead.
  • Mind time zones. Choose an overlap that works for everyone, and rotate inconvenient slots.

Common Sprint Planning mistakes and how to fix them

MistakeWhy it hurtsHow to fix it
Items arrive unrefinedThe event turns into hours of discovery and guessingRefine regularly; keep one to two Sprints of ready items at the top
No Sprint Goal, or a goal that is a list of itemsNo focus, no flexibility and nothing to tell stakeholdersStart with the why; use the good vs weak examples above
Planning to 100% of capacityNo room for support, illness or surprises; regular carry-overPlan to the recent average and leave slack
The Product Owner or a manager decides how muchDevelopers stop owning the forecast; quality suffersDevelopers select; the Product Owner negotiates on value
Skipping the howProblems and dependencies appear mid-SprintBreak down at least the first few days of work
Ignoring technical debt and retrospective actionsQuality erodes and the same problems repeatMake space for one improvement each Sprint; see technical debt in Scrum
Last-minute changes by the Product OwnerThe team plans work it has never seenBring new items to refinement first, unless urgent
Carry-over accepted as normalVelocity becomes unreliable; Sprint Goals are missedSplit items smaller and take on less until carry-over is rare

Frequently asked questions

What happens in Sprint Planning?

The Scrum Team agrees a Sprint Goal, the Developers select the items they forecast they can complete and plan how to do the work. The result is the Sprint Backlog.

How long is Sprint Planning for a two-week Sprint?

The Scrum Guide only sets a maximum of eight hours for a one-month Sprint. Scaling in proportion gives up to four hours for two weeks, but most well-prepared teams need 1.5 to 3 hours.

Who attends Sprint Planning?

The whole Scrum Team: the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the Developers. Other people may be invited to give advice.

Who decides how much work goes into the Sprint?

The Developers, through discussion with the Product Owner. The Product Owner can explain priorities but cannot make the Developers take on more than they believe they can complete.

Who creates the Sprint Goal?

The Product Owner proposes how the Sprint could increase value, and the whole Scrum Team collaborates to define the Sprint Goal. It must be finalised before Sprint Planning ends.

Can the Sprint Goal change during the Sprint?

No changes should be made that endanger the Sprint Goal. The scope of the Sprint Backlog can be renegotiated with the Product Owner. If the goal becomes obsolete, only the Product Owner can cancel the Sprint.

What are the inputs to Sprint Planning?

A refined, ordered Product Backlog, the Product Goal, the latest Increment, past performance, upcoming capacity and the Definition of Done.

What is the output of Sprint Planning?

The Sprint Backlog: the Sprint Goal, the selected Product Backlog items and the plan for delivering them.

Should we estimate tasks in hours?

Scrum does not require it. Some teams use hours to check capacity; many simply break items into tasks of a day or less.

Should tasks be assigned to people in Sprint Planning?

It is not required. Many teams leave most tasks unassigned so Developers can pull them during the Sprint, which encourages shared ownership of the Sprint Goal.

What happens to unfinished items from the last Sprint?

They return to the Product Backlog. The Product Owner reorders them, and they are considered again in Sprint Planning like any other item, re-estimated if the remaining work has changed.

What is the Scrum Master's role in Sprint Planning?

To make sure the event takes place, is productive and stays within the time-box, and to coach the team. The Scrum Master does not decide scope.

Can stakeholders attend Sprint Planning?

Only if the Scrum Team invites them to give advice. Stakeholder feedback normally comes through the Sprint Review and refinement.

What if the team cannot agree on a Sprint Goal?

Go back to the Product Goal and ask the Product Owner which outcome matters most now. Persistent difficulty usually means the top of the backlog holds unrelated items and needs reordering in refinement.
