The Scrum Process: Phases, Steps and the Sprint Cycle

The Scrum process is the repeating cycle a Scrum Team follows to turn an ordered Product Backlog into usable Increments: plan a Sprint, build and inspect the work every day, review the result with stakeholders, improve how the team works, then start the next Sprint straight away. The official Scrum Guide does not divide Scrum into phases, but several popular models do. This guide walks through each step in order, shows who does what with which inputs and outputs, compares the phase models with the Scrum Guide 2020, and follows a worked example across three Sprints.

Key takeaways

  • The Scrum process is a cycle of back-to-back Sprints, each one month or less, in which a Scrum Team turns Product Backlog items into a usable Increment.
  • Inside every Sprint the steps are the same: Sprint Planning, daily work with a Daily Scrum, ongoing backlog refinement, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide has no phases. Pregame, game and postgame (Schwaber, 1995) and the five SBOK phases are other models, useful for teaching but not official Scrum.
  • Before the first Sprint you need a Product Goal, a first ordered Product Backlog, a Scrum Team, a Definition of Done and a Sprint calendar. Scrum does not define a Sprint Zero.
  • The output of each Sprint is the input to the next: a Done Increment, an adapted Product Backlog and at least one improvement to how the team works.
  • Releases are not a separate phase. A team can release whenever an Increment is Done, including several times within a Sprint.

What is the Scrum process?

The Scrum process is the sequence of steps a Scrum Team repeats to deliver value: the Product Owner orders the work in a Product Backlog, the team turns a selection of it into a usable Increment during a fixed-length Sprint, the team and stakeholders inspect the result, and the plan is adjusted for the next Sprint. It is a cycle, not a line.

That four-step summary comes almost directly from the 2020 Scrum Guide, which describes Scrum 'in a nutshell' as: a Product Owner orders the work for a complex problem into a Product Backlog; the Scrum Team turns a selection of the work into an Increment of value during a Sprint; the Scrum Team and its stakeholders inspect the results and adjust for the next Sprint; repeat. A Scrum Master fosters the environment in which this happens.

Everything in the process happens inside Sprints. The Scrum Guide calls the Sprint 'the heartbeat of Scrum' and treats it as a container for the other four events: Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one ends, so there is no gap, no hand-over phase and no separate testing stage.

This guide follows the 2020 Scrum Guide, still the current official version (checked 26 October 2026). New to the framework? Start with what Scrum is.

Does Scrum have phases?

Not officially. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention phases at all. It describes accountabilities, events, artifacts and commitments, and all product work happens within Sprints that repeat until the product is no longer developed. Each Sprint contains its own planning, building, testing, reviewing and improving, so the activities a Waterfall project would spread across separate phases happen together, in small batches, every few weeks.

Many people still search for the 'phases of Scrum', because training courses, textbooks and older papers use phase models. Two are common.

Pregame, game and postgame (Schwaber, 1995)

In his 1995 paper 'SCRUM Development Process', Ken Schwaber described three groups of phases: a pregame of planning and high-level architecture, a game of development Sprints (each with develop, wrap, review and adjust steps) and a postgame of closure, covering final documentation, pre-release testing and release. The paper treated the Sprints as the empirical part and the planning and closure phases as defined processes.

That early model shows where Scrum came from, but the framework has moved on. Today's Scrum Guide has no pregame or postgame: preparation happens as part of normal backlog work and the first Sprint, and release can happen at any time.

The five SBOK phases

The SBOK Guide, published by SCRUMstudy, organises Scrum into five phases containing 19 processes (2013 edition): Initiate, Plan and Estimate, Implement, Review and Retrospect, and Release. Candidates for SCRUMstudy certifications learn this model, and it explains most search results for '5 phases of Scrum'. It is a separate body of knowledge, not part of the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland.

How the phase models map to the Scrum Guide

Scrum can still sit inside a phased governance model: many organisations wrap Scrum Teams in stage gates or funding milestones, as explained in the Waterfall comparison below.

Before the first Sprint: getting ready

The Scrum Guide says nothing about what happens before Sprint 1, so teams decide for themselves. In our experience the following steps make the first Sprint far smoother. Most take days, not weeks.

  1. Agree the product and the Product Goal. Name the product, describe who it serves and write the first Product Goal: a future state the team can plan against, such as 'Patients can book, move and cancel appointments online without phoning'.
  2. Form the Scrum Team. Confirm one Product Owner, a Scrum Master and the Developers, with all the skills needed to create a usable Increment. The Scrum Guide says a Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer people. See Scrum team roles.
  3. Build a first Product Backlog. Collect the ideas you have, write the most important ones as user stories or plain descriptions, and order them. Only the top items need detail; a story-mapping workshop is a quick way to start. Our product backlog guide covers this in depth.
  4. Agree a Definition of Done. Decide the quality standard every Increment must meet, such as tested, reviewed and deployable. If your organisation already has one, use it as the minimum. See Definition of Done.
  5. Choose a Sprint length and calendar. Pick a fixed length of one month or less and book the events at the same time and place for the next few Sprints. Our guide to the Sprint cycle compares lengths.
  6. Refine the top of the backlog. Hold one refinement session so the first few items are small and clear enough for Sprint Planning. See backlog refinement.
  7. Set up the board and tools. A physical or digital Scrum board is enough to start; access to code repositories, environments and test data matters more than fancy software.

What about Sprint Zero?

Sprint Zero is an informal name for a preparation period before 'real' Sprints start. It is not defined in the Scrum Guide, and Ken Schwaber has publicly criticised the term because it is often used to describe planning that is not a Sprint at all. Every Sprint, including the first, should aim to produce a usable Increment.

Our advice: keep preparation short and give Sprint 1 a modest Sprint Goal. If set-up work such as environments or pipelines is needed, pair it with a thin slice of real functionality that stakeholders can inspect.

The Scrum process step by step

Once the team is ready, the same steps repeat every Sprint. Each step below covers who is involved, what happens and what it produces, with a link to the detailed guide.

Step 1: Keep the Product Goal and Product Backlog current

The Product Backlog is the single source of work for the Scrum Team, and its commitment is the Product Goal. The Product Owner is accountable for ordering it, though they may delegate some of the work. The backlog changes constantly as the team learns; the Product Goal stays stable until it is fulfilled or abandoned, and the Scrum Guide says the team must fulfil or abandon one Product Goal before taking on the next.

Output: an ordered backlog whose top items are ready for Sprint Planning.

Step 2: Sprint Planning

Sprint Planning starts the Sprint. The whole Scrum Team attends, and the Scrum Guide organises it around three topics:

  • Why is this Sprint valuable? The Product Owner proposes how the product could increase its value; the team agrees a Sprint Goal before Planning ends.
  • What can be Done this Sprint? The Developers select items from the Product Backlog, using their past performance, upcoming capacity and the Definition of Done.
  • How will the chosen work get done? The Developers plan the work, often by breaking items into tasks of one day or less.

Output: the Sprint Backlog, which is the Sprint Goal (why), the selected items (what) and the plan for delivering them (how). Our Sprint Planning guide gives an agenda and examples.

Step 3: Daily work and the Daily Scrum

Most of the Sprint is spent building. Every working day, the Developers hold a 15-minute Daily Scrum at the same time and place to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog. Its output is an actionable plan for the next day. The Developers choose the format; the three questions were removed from the Scrum Guide in 2020.

Throughout the Sprint, no changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal and quality does not decrease.

Step 4: Backlog refinement (ongoing)

Refinement is not an event but an ongoing activity: the Product Owner and Developers break down upcoming Product Backlog items and add detail, such as a description, order and size. The Developers who will do the work are responsible for the sizing. Many teams hold one or two short sessions a Sprint so the next Sprint Planning has enough ready items. The 2020 Scrum Guide sets no time limit for refinement.

Output: the next one or two Sprints' worth of items, small and clear enough to plan.

Step 5: Build a Done Increment

An Increment is a concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal. Work only becomes part of an Increment when it meets the Definition of Done; anything that does not is returned to the Product Backlog for future consideration. Several Increments can be created in one Sprint, and an Increment may be delivered to stakeholders before the Sprint ends.

Output: one or more usable, Done Increments.

Step 6: Sprint Review

Near the end of the Sprint, the Scrum Team presents the results of its work to key stakeholders and discusses progress toward the Product Goal. The Scrum Guide describes it as a working session rather than a presentation: attendees look at what was achieved and what has changed in the environment, then collaborate on what to do next.

Output: a revised Product Backlog that reflects what everyone has learned. The Sprint Review guide explains how to run one.

Step 7: Sprint Retrospective

The Retrospective concludes the Sprint. The Scrum Team inspects how the last Sprint went with regard to individuals, interactions, processes, tools and its Definition of Done, identifies the most helpful changes and plans how to make them. The most impactful improvements are addressed as soon as possible and may even be added to the Sprint Backlog for the next Sprint.

Output: a small number of concrete improvements.

Step 8: Start the next Sprint

The next Sprint starts immediately, with Sprint Planning. Its inputs are the outputs of the Sprint just finished: the latest Increment, the revised Product Backlog and the team's improvements. This is how Scrum turns experience into better decisions, an idea explained in our guide to empiricism in Scrum.

Time-boxes for each step

Every Scrum event has a maximum duration. The Scrum Guide sets the maximums for a one-month Sprint and says that for shorter Sprints the events are usually shorter. Many teams scale them in proportion to Sprint length; that is common practice, not a rule.

Sprint Planning8 hoursUp to 4 hoursScrum Team; others by invitation for advice

A time-box is a maximum, not a target. A Sprint Review that achieves its purpose in 45 minutes has done its job. For more on choosing a Sprint length and the rules for changing or cancelling a Sprint, read our guide to the Scrum Sprint cycle.

Worked example: three Sprints of a booking app

This example is illustrative. A team of six (a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and four Developers) is building online booking for a group of physiotherapy clinics. Its Product Goal is: 'Patients can book, move and cancel appointments online without phoning the clinic.' The team uses two-week Sprints that start on a Wednesday.

Notice three things. First, every Sprint ended with something real that stakeholders could inspect, even Sprint 1. Second, the Sprint Review changed the order of the backlog each time: the plan was adapted to evidence, not defended. Third, the team released in Sprint 3 as soon as an Increment was ready; it did not wait for a release phase.

Releasing in the Scrum process

Scrum has no release phase. Under the 2020 Scrum Guide, an Increment can be delivered to stakeholders as soon as it meets the Definition of Done, and the Sprint Review should never be treated as a gate to releasing value. Some teams release once a Sprint; teams with automated pipelines may release several times a day.

Release decisions still need thought. The Product Owner usually decides when enough value has been built to release to users, with input from stakeholders and the Developers. Many teams also keep a lightweight release plan or product roadmap to forecast which Product Backlog items are likely to be in which release. That is a complementary practice: it should be updated from real progress every Sprint, not fixed at the start.

If your organisation needs a hardening or stabilisation Sprint before every release, treat it as a signal. It usually means the Definition of Done is too weak, because Done work should already be releasable.

Scrum process vs Waterfall phases

A Waterfall life cycle moves through sequential phases (requirements, design, build, test, deploy) and delivers at the end. The Scrum process repeats every activity in every Sprint, so planning, testing and user feedback happen every few weeks.

Waterfall (phased)Scrum process
PlanningMostly up front; changes go through change requestsContinuous: Product Backlog always, Sprint Backlog every Sprint
TestingA late phaseInside every Sprint, as part of the Definition of Done
User feedbackUsually at the endAt every Sprint Review and after each release
RiskBuilds up until integration and testingLimited to one Sprint at a time

Waterfall suits stable, well-understood work; the Scrum process suits complex work where requirements and solutions emerge.

Common mistakes in the Scrum process

MistakeWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Running a long Sprint Zero (or several)Weeks pass with nothing to inspect; plans are based on assumptionsKeep preparation short and make Sprint 1 deliver a thin, real slice
Treating SBOK or pregame/postgame phases as official ScrumTeams add a separate release or testing phase and lose fast feedbackUse phase models as teaching aids; release Done Increments whenever ready
Leaving gaps between SprintsMomentum drops and 'unofficial' work escapes planningStart the next Sprint Planning immediately after the Retrospective
Skipping refinementSprint Planning turns into a long analysis sessionRefine a little every week so the top of the backlog stays ready
Planning without a Sprint GoalThe Sprint becomes a list of unrelated tasks with no focus for the Daily ScrumAgree a single objective for the Sprint before Planning ends
Calling work Done when it is notHidden work piles up and the Sprint Review shows a false pictureApply the Definition of Done strictly; return unfinished items to the Product Backlog
Sprint Review as a demo onlyStakeholders watch but the backlog never changesEnd every Review by agreeing changes to the Product Backlog
Retrospectives with no follow-throughThe same problems repeat every SprintChoose one or two improvements and put them in the next Sprint Backlog
Changing Sprint length every SprintNo stable rhythm and no reliable data on what the team can doPick a length and keep it unless there is a good reason to change

Most of these come from treating the process as a schedule rather than a feedback loop. If someone plans a 'testing phase' after several Sprints, the team is drifting back to Waterfall.

Tools and templates to run the Scrum process

A whiteboard and sticky notes are enough for a co-located team; digital tools help remote teams and teams that share a backlog. A Scrum board with columns such as To do, In progress, Review and Done makes the Sprint Backlog visible, and our guide to Scrum software tools compares the main options. Frequently asked questions

What are the 5 phases of Scrum?

The five phases usually quoted are Initiate, Plan and Estimate, Implement, Review and Retrospect, and Release. They come from the SBOK Guide by SCRUMstudy. The official Scrum Guide does not divide Scrum into phases; all work happens within repeating Sprints.

What are the steps in the Scrum process?

Order the Product Backlog toward a Product Goal, then run Sprints: Sprint Planning, daily work with a Daily Scrum, ongoing backlog refinement, building a Done Increment, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective. The next Sprint starts immediately.

Is the Scrum lifecycle the same as the Scrum process?

Yes. Both terms describe the repeating cycle of Sprints through which a product evolves, Increment by Increment, toward its Product Goal.

What comes first in the Scrum process?

A Product Goal and an ordered Product Backlog. Without them there is nothing to plan at the first Sprint Planning. In practice the team also forms and agrees a Definition of Done and a Sprint length.

Is Sprint Zero part of the Scrum process?

No. Sprint Zero is an informal term for preparation before the first Sprint. The Scrum Guide does not define it, and every Sprint, including the first, should aim to produce a usable Increment. Keep preparation short.

What are the three phases of Scrum?

Pregame, game and postgame. Ken Schwaber described them in his 1995 paper on the SCRUM development process: planning and architecture, development Sprints, and closure. The current Scrum Guide no longer uses them.

How long is one cycle of the Scrum process?

One Sprint, which is a fixed length of one month or less. Two weeks is a popular choice. The overall process has no fixed end: Sprints continue for as long as the product is being developed.

Is there a gap between Sprints?

No. The Scrum Guide says a new Sprint starts immediately after the conclusion of the previous one. The Sprint Retrospective ends one Sprint and Sprint Planning begins the next.

Where does backlog refinement fit in the Scrum process?

It runs alongside the other steps throughout the Sprint. It is an ongoing activity rather than an event, so the 2020 Scrum Guide sets no time-box for it. See our backlog refinement guide.

When do you release in the Scrum process?

Whenever an Increment meets the Definition of Done and the Product Owner judges it valuable to release. That can be several times within a Sprint. The Sprint Review is not a release gate.

Can you skip a step in the Scrum process?

Not without losing something. Each event is a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt, and the Scrum Guide warns that failing to operate the events as prescribed means lost opportunities to inspect and adapt. Shorten events when the Sprint is short rather than dropping them.
