Sprint Review in Scrum: Agenda, Steps and Examples

A Sprint Review is the Scrum event where the Scrum Team and key stakeholders inspect the outcome of the Sprint and agree what to do next, usually by adjusting the Product Backlog. It is a working session rather than a presentation, it is the second to last event of the Sprint, and it lasts no more than four hours for a one-month Sprint. This guide covers what the Scrum Guide requires, a timed agenda, a preparation checklist, an illustrative worked example and the mistakes that turn reviews into slide shows.

Key takeaways

  • The Sprint Review inspects the outcome of the Sprint and determines future adaptations. Its main output is a revised Product Backlog, not a sign-off.
  • It is a working session with stakeholders. The 2020 Scrum Guide tells teams to avoid limiting it to a presentation.
  • Only work that meets the Definition of Done can be presented. Unfinished items go back to the Product Backlog.
  • The timebox is a maximum of four hours for a one-month Sprint. Many teams with two-week Sprints need 60 to 90 minutes.
  • The Sprint Review is never a gate to releasing value. A Done Increment can be released at any point in the Sprint.
  • Good reviews are prepared: the right stakeholders, a realistic environment, a clear agenda and specific questions you want answered.

What is a Sprint Review?

A Sprint Review is a Scrum event held near the end of each Sprint in which the Scrum Team shows key stakeholders what it achieved, discusses progress towards the Product Goal and what has changed in the wider environment, and agrees together what to do next. Its main result is an updated Product Backlog.

The Sprint Review comes after the Developers have finished the Sprint's work and before the Sprint Retrospective, which closes the Sprint. It is one of the five Scrum events, alongside the Sprint itself, Sprint Planning and the Daily Scrum.

It matters because it is the main scheduled feedback loop between the people building the product and the people who use, pay for or depend on it. Scrum is founded on empiricism: decisions based on what is observed rather than what was planned. The Sprint Review is where the team and stakeholders observe the real Increment together and use what they learn to steer the next Sprint.

What the Scrum Guide 2020 says about the Sprint Review

The Scrum Guide's Sprint Review section is short. In summary, it says:

  • Purpose: inspect the outcome of the Sprint and determine future adaptations.
  • Content: the Scrum Team presents the results of its work to key stakeholders, and progress towards the Product Goal is discussed.
  • Inspection: the Scrum Team and stakeholders review what was accomplished in the Sprint and what has changed in their environment.
  • Adaptation: based on that information, attendees collaborate on what to do next, and the Product Backlog may be adjusted to meet new opportunities.
  • Format: it is a working session, and the Scrum Team should avoid limiting it to a presentation.
  • Timing: it is the second to last event of the Sprint and is timeboxed to a maximum of four hours for a one-month Sprint. For shorter Sprints, it is usually shorter.

Two other rules in the guide shape the event. The section on the Definition of Done says that an item that does not meet it cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review; it returns to the Product Backlog instead. And the section on the Increment says an Increment may be delivered to stakeholders before the end of the Sprint, and that the Sprint Review should never be considered a gate to releasing value.

What changed from the 2017 Scrum Guide

Much of the advice online still follows the 2017 Scrum Guide, which listed specific elements: the Product Owner explaining what was and was not Done, the Development Team discussing what went well and demonstrating the work, the Product Owner projecting likely delivery dates, and a review of timeline, budget, capabilities and the marketplace. The 2020 version replaced that list with the shorter description above, added the Product Goal, and no longer states that the Product Owner invites the stakeholders. The old elements are still useful as an agenda, which is how this guide uses them, but they are no longer rules.

Tip

Scrum does not say who must chair the Sprint Review or who must demonstrate. Decide it as a team, and keep the focus on stakeholders using and discussing the product rather than on who holds the clicker.

Inputs and outputs of the Sprint Review

Knowing what goes in and what should come out keeps the event focused. If nothing in the right-hand column changes, the review was probably a presentation.

InputsOutputs
The Increment: all work that meets the Definition of DoneA revised Product Backlog: new, removed, re-ordered or reworded items
The Sprint Goal and whether it was metA shared understanding of progress towards the Product Goal
The current Product Backlog and Product GoalAn updated release or delivery forecast, where the team uses one
Progress data such as burn-up charts or velocityDecisions and agreed next steps, including candidate items for the next Sprint Goal
Changes in the environment: market, users, budget, regulation, timeline, dependenciesStakeholder feedback captured as backlog items rather than lost in meeting notes

The Sprint Review does not produce an approval, a sign-off or a list of accepted stories. The Product Owner can review work with Developers at any point during the Sprint, and the Definition of Done is what makes work complete.

Who attends the Sprint Review and what each person does

The Scrum Guide names the Scrum Team and key stakeholders. In practice the Product Owner usually decides who the key stakeholders are and sends the invitations, because the Product Owner is accountable for maximising the value of the product and for managing the Product Backlog.

AttendeeTypical role in the Sprint Review
Product OwnerHosts the session, restates the Sprint Goal, leads the discussion of the Product Backlog and forecast, and decides how feedback changes the order of work
DevelopersShow and explain the Done work, answer questions, describe problems they met and how they solved them, and hear feedback first-hand
Scrum MasterMakes sure the event takes place, is positive and productive, and stays within its timebox; facilitates if the team wants help
Customers and usersTry the Increment, describe how they would use it and say what is missing or confusing
Sponsors and business ownersShare changes in budget, strategy or priorities, and discuss trade-offs in the forecast
Other teams and specialistsSpot dependencies, integration issues and compliance, security or support needs

Keep the list to people who will give useful feedback or make decisions. Our guide to stakeholder management can help you map who matters most for each product area.

How long should a Sprint Review be?

The only rule is the maximum: four hours for a one-month Sprint, and usually shorter for shorter Sprints. A common rule of thumb, not found in the Scrum Guide, is about one hour per week of Sprint. Treat it as a starting point and inspect it in your Retrospectives.

Sprint lengthScrum Guide maximumTypical in practice
One weekShorter than four hours30 to 60 minutes
Two weeksShorter than four hours60 to 90 minutes
Three weeksShorter than four hours90 minutes to 2.5 hours
One monthFour hours2 to 4 hours

The timebox is a maximum, not a target. A tightly prepared 60-minute review with real discussion beats a three-hour tour of every ticket. For more on Sprint lengths and timeboxes, see our guide to what a Sprint is.

Sprint Review agenda

This agenda suits a two-week Sprint with a 75-minute review. It keeps the most time for hands-on inspection and backlog discussion. Scale each slot up or down for other Sprint lengths.

TimeItemLed byWhat happens
0:00–0:05Welcome and purposeProduct OwnerIntroductions if needed; remind everyone that this is a working session and feedback is the goal
0:05–0:10Sprint Goal and outcomeProduct OwnerRestate the Sprint Goal, say whether it was met and list what is Done and what is not
0:10–0:35Inspect the IncrementDevelopersLive demonstration, ideally with stakeholders trying it themselves; questions as you go
0:35–0:45What has changedProduct Owner and stakeholdersMarket, users, budget, timeline, regulation, dependencies, new risks or opportunities
0:45–1:05Product Backlog and forecastProduct OwnerWalk through the top of the backlog, discuss re-ordering, review the release forecast or progress towards the Product Goal
1:05–1:12Agree next stepsWhole groupConfirm backlog changes, likely focus of the next Sprint and any follow-up owners
1:12–1:15CloseProduct OwnerThank attendees, recognise contributions, confirm the date of the next review

Share the agenda and a one-paragraph summary of the Sprint Goal a day or two before. Our free meeting agenda template has timed items, owners and outcomes ready to adapt.

How to prepare for a Sprint Review

Most weak Sprint Reviews fail before they start. Preparation is light but it has to happen at the right times.

Preparation timeline

WhenWhat to doWho
Sprint PlanningBook the review for the whole year or quarter at a fixed time; note which stakeholders care about this Sprint GoalProduct Owner, Scrum Master
Mid-SprintConfirm attendance of key stakeholders; ask what they most want to see or decideProduct Owner
Two to three days beforeAgree which items will meet the Definition of Done; choose who shows what; prepare a stable demo environment and realistic dataDevelopers
Day beforeSend the agenda, the Sprint Goal and links to try the Increment; update the backlog and forecast chartProduct Owner
On the dayTest the demo, the video link and access for remote attendees 15 minutes beforeDevelopers, Scrum Master

Preparation checklist

  1. Key stakeholders invited, with at least one real user or customer representative where possible.
  2. Only items that meet the Definition of Done selected for inspection.
  3. A realistic environment and test data that let people try the product, not just watch it.
  4. A short list of what is not Done, and why, ready to share openly.
  5. The top of the Product Backlog ordered and visible, with any forecast chart up to date.
  6. Two to four specific questions you want stakeholders to answer.
  7. A way to capture feedback straight into the backlog, such as a shared board or a shared notes page.
  8. A facilitator and timekeeper agreed, so the Product Owner can focus on the conversation.

Avoid rehearsing a polished show. Fifteen minutes of preparation by the presenters is usually enough; anything longer is time taken from the Sprint Goal.

How to run a Sprint Review, step by step

Use these steps with the agenda above. They work in person, remotely or in a hybrid room.

  1. Open with purpose. The Product Owner explains that the aim is to inspect the product and decide what to do next, and invites honest feedback.
  2. Restate the Sprint Goal. Say plainly whether it was met. Transparency here sets the tone for the rest of the session.
  3. Separate Done from not Done. List the items that meet the Definition of Done and briefly mention the ones that do not. Do not demonstrate unfinished work.
  4. Let stakeholders use the Increment. Hand over the keyboard, share a test link or walk a real scenario. Ask them to think aloud.
  5. Capture feedback as it comes. A scribe writes each point on the shared board as a candidate backlog item, not as a note to remember later.
  6. Discuss what has changed. Ask stakeholders about market, budget, timeline and priorities. A change here can matter more than any feature shown.
  7. Review the Product Backlog together. The Product Owner shows the top items, explains the current order and discusses changes the group suggests.
  8. Update the forecast. If you use a release plan or burn-up chart, show how today's progress and feedback change it.
  9. Agree and record next steps. Summarise the backlog changes and decisions, name owners for follow-ups and close on time.
Tip

Let the Developers hear feedback directly. When it reaches them second-hand through the Product Owner, much of the context is lost.

Worked example: a Sprint Review for a two-week Sprint

The following example is illustrative. The organisation, people and numbers are invented to show how the parts fit together.

Context. A housing association's digital team is building an online repairs service for tenants. It works in two-week Sprints. The Sprint Goal was: Tenants can report a leaking tap online and receive a booking confirmation. The 75-minute review is attended by the Scrum Team, the head of repairs, two call-centre advisers, a tenant panel member and the IT security lead.

What was shown

  • Done: the repair report form for kitchen and bathroom taps, photo upload, and an automatic confirmation email with a reference number.
  • Not Done: the slot-booking step. Integration with the contractor's scheduling system was blocked by an access issue and did not meet the Definition of Done, so it was not shown. The Sprint Goal was therefore only partly met.
  • The tenant panel member reported a repair on a phone using test data while the group watched on screen.

What the group learned

  • The tenant could not tell whether the leak counted as an emergency. The call-centre advisers said about a third of their tap calls are emergencies that need a same-day response.
  • The head of repairs shared a change in the environment: a new contractor framework starts in two months, with a different scheduling system.
  • The security lead asked how long uploaded photos are kept, which no one could answer.

Resulting Product Backlog changes

ChangeBacklog itemDecision
NewAsk an emergency triage question before the form and route emergencies to the phone lineOrdered first; candidate for the next Sprint Goal
NewAgree and apply a retention period for uploaded photosOrdered second; security lead to supply the policy
Re-orderedSlot booking with the current contractor's systemMoved down; building it for a system being replaced in two months is poor value
NewSlot booking with the new contractor's scheduling systemAdded; needs refinement once the API documentation arrives
RemovedPrintable PDF of the repair reportRemoved; no stakeholder needed it

Forecast. The Product Owner updated the release burn-up to show the first public release moving two Sprints later than the earlier forecast, because emergency triage and the new booking integration were added. The head of repairs accepted the change because it avoids rework. The review ended with clear next steps and a next Sprint Goal that was already half-formed: safe triage of emergency repairs.

Notice what made this a working session: stakeholders used the product, the team was open about the missed part of the goal, a change in the environment re-ordered the backlog, and every piece of feedback became a backlog decision. You can record the same structure in our meeting notes template and keep the backlog itself in a product backlog template.

Questions to ask stakeholders in a Sprint Review

Polite applause is not feedback. Prepare a few open questions that invite people to share what they actually think. Pick two to four per review rather than asking them all.

About the Increment

  • What would you do next if you were using this for real?
  • What surprised or confused you?
  • What is missing before you would use or release this?

About value and priorities

  • Which of the next five backlog items matters most to you, and why?
  • If we could only do one more thing before release, what should it be?
  • Is there anything on the backlog you no longer need?

About the environment

  • Has anything changed in your area since the last review: customers, budget, deadlines, rules?
  • Are there risks or dependencies we have not seen?
  • Is the current release forecast still acceptable? What would change if it moved?

What if the Sprint Goal was not met?

Hold the Sprint Review anyway. A missed goal is exactly the kind of information stakeholders need, and hiding it only delays decisions.

  • Say early and plainly that the goal was not met, what was achieved and what blocked the rest.
  • Show only the items that meet the Definition of Done. Unfinished items return to the Product Backlog; the Product Owner decides where they go.
  • Discuss the impact on the forecast and the Product Goal rather than dwelling on blame.
  • Leave the question of how the team works for the Retrospective. The review is about the product, the Retrospective is about the process.

If the same problem appears in review after review, the Sprint Goal or the forecast may be too ambitious. Our guide to Scrum velocity explains how to use past performance to set more realistic expectations.

Sprint Review vs demo vs Sprint Retrospective

These three are often confused. A demo is part of many Sprint Reviews, but a Sprint Review is more than a demo, and it is a different event from the Retrospective.

Remote, hybrid and multi-team Sprint Reviews

Remote and hybrid reviews

  • Send a test link in advance so stakeholders can try the Increment before or during the session.
  • Use a shared online board for feedback, with one column per backlog decision: add, change, remove, park.
  • Give everyone the same experience in hybrid sessions: if anyone is remote, everyone joins from their own device.
  • Keep the session shorter and add a five-minute break if it runs over an hour.
  • Record the demonstration for people who cannot attend, but do not let recordings replace the live conversation.

Several teams on one product

When several Scrum Teams work on one product, hold a single joint Sprint Review of the integrated Increment rather than a series of separate team demos. Stakeholders see the whole product, and integration problems surface early. The Nexus framework, for example, replaces individual team reviews with one Nexus Sprint Review. A popular format is a review bazaar or market: each team runs a station, stakeholders move between them, and everyone regroups for the backlog discussion. See our guide to the Scrum of Scrums for coordinating teams between reviews.

Sprint Reviews for non-software teams

The Sprint Review works wherever a team delivers something usable each Sprint. What changes is what the Increment looks like and who the stakeholders are.

TeamWhat stakeholders inspectTypical backlog adaptation
MarketingLive campaign assets, landing pages, early resultsShift budget to the best-performing channel, add a new audience segment
HR or internal changeA new onboarding pack tried by two recent startersRemove steps that confused new starters, add a manager checklist
Construction designUpdated models or drawings walked through with the clientRe-order design packages after a planning condition changes
Research or policyDraft findings and a short briefing noteDrop a line of enquiry, add interviews with an overlooked group

The principle is the same: inspect real output with the people who will use it, then adapt what comes next.

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
A slide show instead of the productStakeholders react to descriptions, not to the real thingShow the working Increment and let people use it
Demonstrating unfinished workCreates false expectations and breaks the Definition of DoneShow only Done items; mention the rest briefly
No real stakeholdersThe team talks to itself and learns nothing newInvite users and decision-makers; ask them what they want to see
Feedback recorded but never acted onStakeholders stop attendingTurn feedback into backlog items during the session and show the changes next time
Treating it as an approval gateDelays value and contradicts the Scrum GuideRelease Done work when it is ready; use the review to inspect and adapt
A status report of tickets and hoursFocuses on activity rather than outcomesTalk about the Sprint Goal, value and what was learned
Skipping the backlog and forecast discussionLoses the adaptation half of the eventProtect at least a quarter of the time for the backlog and forecast
Hiding a missed Sprint GoalErodes trust and delays decisionsState it early and discuss the impact openly
Turning it into a process post-mortemMixes up review and retrospectiveKeep team process issues for the Retrospective

Frequently asked questions

What is the main purpose of a Sprint Review?

To inspect the outcome of the Sprint and decide what to do next. The Scrum Team and stakeholders look at the Increment, discuss progress towards the Product Goal and changes in the environment, and adapt the Product Backlog.

Who runs the Sprint Review?

The Scrum Guide does not name a chair. In most teams the Product Owner hosts and leads the backlog discussion, the Developers show the work, and the Scrum Master makes sure the event happens, stays productive and keeps to its timebox.

How long is a Sprint Review for a two-week Sprint?

The guide sets a maximum of four hours for a one-month Sprint and says shorter Sprints usually have shorter reviews. Many two-week teams use 60 to 90 minutes, based on the common rule of thumb of about an hour per Sprint week.

Is a Sprint Review the same as a demo?

No. A demo shows features. A Sprint Review also discusses feedback, changes in the environment, the Product Backlog and the forecast, and ends with decisions about what to do next. A demo is often one part of it.

Can you show unfinished work in a Sprint Review?

No. The Scrum Guide says an item that does not meet the Definition of Done cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review. You can mention it when explaining what was and was not completed; it returns to the Product Backlog.

Is the Sprint Review mandatory?

Yes, if you are using Scrum. It is one of the five Scrum events, and the Scrum Guide says that each element serves a specific purpose. Skipping it removes the main feedback loop with stakeholders.

Do stakeholders have to attend the Sprint Review?

They are not obliged to, but without them the event loses most of its value. If stakeholders stop coming, ask why, adjust the timing or format, and show them how their earlier feedback changed the backlog.

What is the output of a Sprint Review?

A revised Product Backlog that reflects what was learned, plus a shared view of progress towards the Product Goal and often an updated release forecast. It does not produce a formal sign-off.

Can the team release before the Sprint Review?

Yes. The 2020 Scrum Guide says an Increment may be delivered to stakeholders before the end of the Sprint and that the Sprint Review should never be considered a gate to releasing value.

Does the Product Owner accept or reject work in the Sprint Review?

Not as a formal step. Work is complete when it meets the Definition of Done. The Product Owner can review items with the Developers at any time during the Sprint, so nothing should be a surprise at the review.

What comes first, the Sprint Review or the Retrospective?

The Sprint Review. It is the second to last event of the Sprint, and the Sprint Retrospective concludes it. Holding the review first means the team can reflect on stakeholder feedback in the Retrospective.

Can a Sprint Review be held remotely?

Yes. Share a test link so stakeholders can try the Increment themselves, use a shared online board to capture feedback as backlog items, and keep the session slightly shorter than you would in person.

What should you do if no stakeholders turn up?

Still inspect the Increment and the backlog as a team, then find out why stakeholders stayed away. Common fixes are a better time slot, a shorter agenda, inviting different people and showing how their feedback has changed priorities.
