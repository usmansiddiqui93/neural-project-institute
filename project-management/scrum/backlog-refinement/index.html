Backlog Refinement in Scrum: How to Run It, With Examples

Backlog refinement is the ongoing activity of breaking down Product Backlog items and adding detail, such as a description, order and size, so the top of the backlog is ready for Sprint Planning. In Scrum it is not a formal event, but most teams still give it regular time. This guide explains what the Scrum Guide 2020 says, who takes part, how much time to spend, and how to run refinement step by step, with a timed agenda, splitting techniques, a worked example and the mistakes to avoid.

Key takeaways

  • Backlog refinement is an ongoing activity, not one of the five Scrum events, and the 2020 Scrum Guide sets no time limit for it.
  • It breaks large items into smaller, more precise ones and adds a description, order and size, so items can be Done within one Sprint.
  • The Product Owner and Developers refine together; the Developers who will do the work size the items.
  • Aim to keep roughly one to two Sprints' worth of items ready at the top of the backlog, and leave the rest coarse.
  • Good refinement has work before and after the session: prepare items, refine in short focused rounds, then follow up open questions.
  • Split items vertically, so every slice is usable on its own, and treat a Definition of Ready as an optional guide, not a gate.

What is backlog refinement?

Backlog refinement (or product backlog refinement) is the ongoing work of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller, more precise items. The Product Owner and Developers clarify what each item means and why it matters, split anything too large, add acceptance criteria and size the work, so the next Sprints can be planned with confidence.

Refinement turns rough ideas near the top of the product backlog into work the team understands. Its outputs are usually:

  • Shared understanding of the problem, the user and the outcome wanted.
  • Smaller items that can each be Done within one Sprint.
  • Sizes from the Developers, for example in story points.
  • Acceptance criteria and notes on dependencies, risks and open questions.
  • An updated order, because what the team learns often changes what should come first.
Backlog refinement isBacklog refinement is not
Clarifying what an item means and why it mattersCommitting to work for the next Sprint
Splitting large items into smaller ones that still deliver valueAssigning tasks to individuals
Sizing items so they can be ordered and forecastDetailed technical design of every item
Adding acceptance criteria and surfacing risksA status meeting or progress report
Ongoing and collaborativeSomething the Product Owner does alone

What the Scrum Guide 2020 says about refinement

The Scrum Guide gives refinement only a few sentences. The 2020 edition is still the current official version (checked 25 October 2026). The table summarises what it says and what each point means for your team.

The Scrum Guide saysWhat it means in practice
Refinement is the act of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller, more precise itemsRefinement is about precision and size, not about writing long specifications
It is an ongoing activity to add details, such as a description, order and sizeIt is not one of the five Scrum events, so it has no official time-box or agenda
Attributes often vary with the domain of workUse the attributes that help your team; user stories and story points are optional
The Developers who will be doing the work are responsible for the sizingThe Product Owner explains and makes trade-offs, but does not set estimates
Items that can be Done within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint PlanningThis is the only readiness test in Scrum; anything bigger must be split first
During the Sprint, the Product Backlog is refined as neededRefinement happens alongside delivery, inside the Sprint
The Scrum Team may refine items during Sprint PlanningLast-minute clarification is allowed, but planning goes faster if most refinement is done earlier
The Scrum Master helps find techniques for effective Product Backlog managementThe Scrum Master coaches and facilitates; they do not own the backlog

What changed from earlier versions

  • The 10% guideline was removed. The 2017 Scrum Guide said refinement usually consumed no more than 10% of the Development Team's capacity. The 2020 Guide sets no figure, so the team decides how much time it needs.
  • "Grooming" became "refinement" in the 2013 Scrum Guide. Backlog grooming and backlog refinement mean the same activity, and refinement is now the standard term.
Tip

The Scrum Guide does not mention a Definition of Ready, refinement meetings, story points or a fixed cadence. These are complementary practices that you can adopt, change or drop.

Who takes part in backlog refinement?

Refinement works best as a conversation between the people who understand the need and the people who will build the solution. The table shows who usually does what.

WhoRole in refinementAttendance
Product OwnerBrings the items, explains the value and context, answers or finds answers to questions, makes trade-offs and updates the orderNormally every session; accountable for the backlog
DevelopersAsk questions, explore edge cases and risks, suggest simpler options and splits, write or improve acceptance criteria and size the itemsThose likely to do the work; many teams invite all Developers
Scrum MasterCoaches the team on techniques, facilitates when needed, keeps the session focused and helps remove obstacles to good refinementOptional once the team is confident
Stakeholders and expertsUsers, designers, architects, compliance or support staff who hold knowledge an item needsInvited for specific items, not every session

Who facilitates? Anyone can. The Product Owner often leads because they bring the items, the Scrum Master often facilitates while the team is learning, and mature teams rotate the role. The facilitator keeps time and makes sure everyone is heard.

Whole team or a few people? Involving all Developers builds shared understanding. Larger teams sometimes let two or three Developers refine an item in depth and then walk the others through it. Either works if the whole team agrees with the result before Sprint Planning.

How long and how often should refinement take?

The 2020 Scrum Guide sets no time limit for refinement. The old 2017 guideline of no more than 10% of capacity is still a useful ceiling for many teams, but treat it as a reference point and inspect and adapt the amount at your Sprint Retrospective.

Worked example: how much time is 10%?

Take five Developers in a two-week Sprint of ten working days, each with about six focused hours a day. Their capacity is 5 x 10 x 6 = 300 hours, so 10% is 30 hours. Two one-hour refinement sessions with all five Developers use 10 hours. That leaves around 20 hours for informal refinement work such as spikes, prototypes and conversations with users. In our experience, struggling teams more often spend too little time on refinement than too much.

Suggested cadence by Sprint length

This is our own starting suggestion, not a Scrum rule. Adjust it once you see how ready your backlog is at each Sprint Planning.

Sprint lengthTypical refinement patternTotal per Sprint
1 weekOne session of 45 to 60 minutes mid-Sprint, plus ad hoc conversationsAbout 1 hour
2 weeksTwo sessions of about 60 minutes, for example on days 4 and 8About 2 hours
3 weeksTwo or three sessions of 60 to 75 minutes2 to 3 hours
4 weeksWeekly sessions of about 60 minutes3 to 4 hours

Short, regular sessions beat one long session the day before Sprint Planning: attention drops after an hour, and late questions have no time to be answered.

How far ahead to refine

Aim to keep roughly the next one to two Sprints' worth of items refined and sized. Refining much further ahead wastes effort because priorities and understanding change. Refining less makes Sprint Planning slow and uncertain. Items further down the backlog stay as coarse epics until they approach the top.

How to run backlog refinement, step by step

The session is the visible part of refinement; the preparation before and the follow-up after are what make it work.

Before the session

  1. Check the top of the backlog. The Product Owner reviews the next items against the Product Goal and removes or moves anything no longer relevant.
  2. Pick a short list. Choose three to six items for a one-hour session, in backlog order, plus one or two spare items in case the team moves quickly.
  3. Add what you already know. For each item, write the problem, who it is for and why it matters now. Attach designs, data or customer feedback if you have them.
  4. Share the list a day or two ahead so Developers can arrive with questions, and invite any expert an item needs for that part of the session only.

During the session: a 60-minute agenda

TimeActivityLed byOutput
0 to 5 minContext: current Product Goal, what changed since the last session, list of itemsProduct OwnerShared focus
5 to 45 minWork through the items one at a time (about 8 to 10 minutes each): explain the why, ask questions, agree acceptance criteria, split if needed, sizeProduct Owner and DevelopersRefined, sized items or a list of open questions
45 to 52 minReview the order in the light of what was learnedProduct OwnerUpdated backlog order
52 to 57 minCheck readiness: which items could be Done within one Sprint? How many Sprints are ready?FacilitatorReady backlog depth
57 to 60 minConfirm follow-ups: who will answer each open question, and by whenFacilitatorAction list

For each item, a useful rhythm is: the Product Owner explains the need in two minutes; the Developers ask questions; the group writes or adjusts the acceptance criteria; the Developers judge whether it fits in one Sprint and split it if not; then they size it. If an item is still unclear after ten minutes, stop, record the questions and move on.

After the session

  1. Update the backlog straight away: new or split items, acceptance criteria, sizes and the new order.
  2. Chase open questions. Each question has an owner and a date. Answers go into the item so everyone can see them.
  3. Run spikes where needed. If uncertainty is too high to size an item, add a short, time-boxed spike to the backlog or the current Sprint.
  4. Inspect the practice. At the Retrospective, ask whether refinement is giving the team what it needs for Sprint Planning.

Techniques for refining items

The Scrum Guide leaves technique to the team. These are widely used complementary practices.

Splitting large items

Split vertically, so each slice delivers something a user can see or the team can learn from. Splitting horizontally into layers such as database, interface and testing creates items that have no value on their own and cannot be Done in a meaningful sense. Mike Cohn's SPIDR approach (Spikes, Paths, Interfaces, Data, Rules) is a popular checklist, and the patterns below cover most situations.

PatternHow it splitsExample
SpikeInvestigate first, then size the real workTime-boxed spike: can the courier API handle our volume?
Paths or workflow stepsOne route or step through the workflow at a time"Checkout" becomes basket review, delivery details and payment
InterfacesOne device, browser or channel firstMobile web first, the app later
DataOne type or subset of data firstCard payments first, then digital wallets
RulesSimplest business rule first, exceptions laterStandard delivery first, express and international delivery later
User or roleOne group of users firstCustomers first, then customer service staff
Simple, then betterA basic version first, improvements laterBasic search, then filters, then saved searches

Collaboration techniques

  • Three Amigos. Business, development and testing perspectives discuss an item together before it is sized. Quick, and useful between full sessions.
  • Example mapping. Usually credited to Matt Wynne. Write the story, its rules, concrete examples under each rule and open questions on different coloured cards. Many rules or questions suggest the item should be split.
  • User story mapping. Popularised by Jeff Patton. Arrange user activities left to right and detail top to bottom, then slice across the map to refine a large epic into releasable pieces.

Estimating during refinement

Refinement is where most sizing happens. Common techniques include Planning Poker (each Developer reveals an estimate at the same time, then the highest and lowest explain their reasoning), T-shirt sizes for coarse items and affinity estimation for sorting many items quickly. A wide spread of estimates is useful: it usually means people understand the item differently, so talk before re-voting. Our guides to Agile estimation and Scrum velocity explain how sizes feed forecasts.

Writing acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria describe the conditions a specific item must meet. Many teams use the Given / When / Then format, for example: "Given an order has shipped, when the customer opens their account page, then they see the status Shipped and the date it was sent." Acceptance criteria are different from the Definition of Done, which is the quality standard that applies to every item in the Increment. Our guide to user stories has more examples.

When is an item ready? Ready vs Definition of Ready

According to the Scrum Guide, items that can be Done by the Scrum Team within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint Planning, and they usually reach that level of transparency through refinement. That is the only readiness rule in Scrum.

Some teams add a Definition of Ready: a short checklist an item should meet before it enters Sprint Planning. It is not part of the Scrum Guide. Many teams also use Bill Wake's INVEST criteria (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable) as a quick check of item quality. A light checklist might be:

  • The problem and the user are clear, and the Product Owner can explain why it matters now.
  • Acceptance criteria are drafted and understood by the Developers.
  • The Developers have sized it and believe it can be Done within one Sprint.
  • Known dependencies are visible, and none blocks the start of the work.
  • Any design, data or access the team needs is available or planned.

Use it as a guide, not a gate. A long, strict Definition of Ready turns refinement into a hand-off process and delays learning. If an item is nearly ready and important, the Scrum Team can still discuss it in Sprint Planning.

Worked example: refining "Customers can track their order"

An online shop's Scrum Team usually completes about 25 points a Sprint. Near the top of the backlog is an item from customer service: "Customers can track their order." Calls asking "Where is my order?" are the most common reason customers contact the shop.

Preparation. Two days before the session the Product Owner adds the context: call volumes from the support team, a screenshot of the current account page and a note that the courier offers a tracking API.

Discussion. The Developers ask what customers most need to know, whether all orders use the same courier, how often tracking can be refreshed and what happens when a delivery fails. Their first estimate is about 20 points: too big to fit comfortably in one Sprint alongside other work, and uncertain because nobody knows the API's limits.

Splitting. Using the paths and spike patterns, they split the item into four smaller ones:

OrderNew itemAcceptance criteria (summary)Size
1Show order status on the account pageStatus shows Placed, Packed, Shipped or Delivered, with the date of each change5
2Email the customer when an order shipsEmail sent within 10 minutes of dispatch, including the tracking link3
3Spike: courier API limitsAnswer by the end of the spike: can we request updates every 15 minutes for all open orders?2
4Show live courier tracking on the account pageLatest courier event shown; message if tracking is unavailable8

Re-ordering. The total is 18 points, and each item is usable on its own. The Product Owner orders the status page and the shipping email first because together they answer most "Where is my order?" calls. The spike comes next so that live tracking is lower risk by the time it reaches the top. A fifth idea, SMS notifications, is added near the bottom of the backlog as a coarse item.

Readiness check. After the session, the ready items at the top of the backlog total 46 points. With an average velocity of about 25, that is just under two Sprints of ready work, which is where the team likes to be. The Product Owner records the open question about failed deliveries against item 4 and asks customer service for the current process before the next session.

Backlog refinement vs Sprint Planning

Refinement prepares items over time; Sprint Planning turns the best-prepared items into a plan for one Sprint. The better the refinement, the shorter and calmer Sprint Planning becomes.

Backlog refinementSprint Planning
TypeOngoing activityScrum event
Time-boxNone in the Scrum GuideUp to 8 hours for a one-month Sprint, usually shorter for shorter Sprints
WhenThroughout the SprintAt the start of each Sprint
FocusItems for the next one to two Sprints and beyondThe current Sprint: why, what and how
Main outputsClear, smaller, sized and ordered itemsSprint Goal, selected items and the plan: the Sprint Backlog
CommitmentNone; it prepares optionsThe Sprint Goal

If Sprint Planning keeps running long because the team is discovering what items mean, move that conversation into refinement.

How to tell whether refinement is working

Refinement is working when Sprint Planning is short and focused and items rarely surprise the team mid-Sprint. These measures are our suggestions, not Scrum rules.

MeasureHow to calculate itHealthy range (guide only)
Ready backlog depthPoints (or items) refined and sized at the top, divided by average velocity (or throughput)About 1 to 2 Sprints
Sprint Planning timeMinutes spent on the "what" part of Sprint PlanningFalling or stable
Mid-Sprint surprisesItems whose scope changed significantly after selectionRare
Carry-overItems selected but not Done at the end of the SprintOccasional, not routine
Open questionsQuestions recorded in refinement and still unanswered at Sprint PlanningClose to zero

Worked example. A team with an average velocity of 30 points has refined items worth 24 points. Its ready depth is 24 / 30 = 0.8 Sprints, so the next Sprint Planning risks stalling. The team adds a second refinement session this Sprint and asks the Product Owner to bring larger items early so they can be split in time. Use these numbers for the team's own improvement, never to compare teams or individuals.

Remote, asynchronous and multi-team refinement

Remote refinement

  • Share the item on screen and edit it live, so everyone sees the same acceptance criteria.
  • Keep sessions to 45 to 60 minutes and use a digital whiteboard for mapping techniques.
  • Use simultaneous estimation, so quieter voices are not anchored by the first number spoken.

Asynchronous pre-refinement

Teams spread across time zones often comment on items in the tool before the session: questions, risks and a first-pass estimate. The live session then focuses on items where people disagree. Comments are a good supplement, but they rarely replace the conversation entirely.

Refinement with several teams

When several Scrum Teams work on one product, they share one Product Backlog and one Product Owner. Large-scale frameworks add structure: Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS), for example, describes overall refinement to look across the product and multi-team refinement in which members of several teams refine items together. A simpler approach is to send one or two representatives from each team to a short cross-team session, then refine in detail within each team.

Frequently asked questions

What is backlog refinement in Scrum?

It is the ongoing activity of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller, more precise items, adding details such as a description, order and size, so they are ready for Sprint Planning.

Is backlog refinement a Scrum event?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes it as an ongoing activity, not one of the five events. Teams can still schedule regular refinement sessions.

Is backlog refinement the same as backlog grooming?

Yes. Grooming is the older term; the Scrum Guide switched to refinement in 2013, and refinement is now the standard name.

Who attends backlog refinement?

The Product Owner and the Developers, with the Scrum Master helping where useful. Stakeholders or experts join for items that need their knowledge.

Who facilitates backlog refinement?

Anyone the team chooses. The Product Owner often leads because they bring the items, and the Scrum Master often facilitates for new teams. Many mature teams rotate facilitation.

How long should backlog refinement take?

The 2020 Scrum Guide sets no limit. The 2017 Guide said it usually took no more than 10% of capacity, which is still a common ceiling. Many two-week teams spend about two hours a Sprint in sessions, plus informal work.

How often should we refine the backlog?

Continuously. Most teams hold one or two short sessions per Sprint and handle smaller questions as they arise, rather than one long session before Sprint Planning.

Who estimates during backlog refinement?

The Developers who will do the work. The Product Owner explains the items and trade-offs but does not set the estimates.

What is the difference between backlog refinement and Sprint Planning?

Refinement prepares items over time and has no time-box. Sprint Planning is a time-boxed event at the start of the Sprint where the Scrum Team sets the Sprint Goal, selects items and plans the work.

What does ready mean in backlog refinement?

The Scrum Guide says items that can be Done within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint Planning. Some teams add an optional Definition of Ready checklist.

How far ahead should we refine the backlog?

Usually enough for the next one or two Sprints. Refining far ahead risks waste because priorities and understanding change.

Can refinement happen during the Sprint or in Sprint Planning?

Yes to both. The Scrum Guide says the backlog is refined as needed during the Sprint, and that the Scrum Team may refine items during Sprint Planning.

What if refinement sessions keep overrunning?

Prepare items in advance, focus on the top of the backlog, time-box each item to about ten minutes and move deep technical questions to a smaller follow-up or a spike.
