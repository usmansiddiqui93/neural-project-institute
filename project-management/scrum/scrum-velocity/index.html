Scrum Velocity: How to Calculate and Use It, With Examples

Scrum velocity is the amount of work a Scrum Team completes to its Definition of Done in one Sprint, usually measured in story points. The Scrum Guide does not mention it, but it is the most common way teams record the past performance that Sprint Planning relies on. This guide shows how to calculate velocity step by step, how to use it in each Scrum event and for release forecasts, what changes it, and how to avoid the misuse that makes it worthless.

Key takeaways

  • Velocity is the total estimate of the Product Backlog items that met the Definition of Done in a Sprint. Partly finished items count as zero.
  • The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention velocity. It is a complementary practice from Extreme Programming that records the past performance the Guide refers to.
  • Plan from the average of the last three to five Sprints, scaled for this Sprint's availability, and keep the Sprint Goal more important than the number.
  • Forecast releases as a range from slower and faster recent Sprints, and update the forecast after every Sprint Review.
  • Velocity belongs to the Developers. It is specific to one team, so never compare teams, add it up across teams without care, or turn it into a target.
  • You improve velocity by removing what slows the team down, not by pushing for bigger numbers.

What is velocity in Scrum?

Velocity in Scrum is the sum of the estimates of all Product Backlog items that a Scrum Team completes to its Definition of Done during a Sprint. It is usually measured in story points. Averaged over several Sprints, it tells the team roughly how much it can take on next and helps forecast when larger pieces of work may be finished.

A simple illustration: if a team finishes items estimated at 8, 5, 5 and 3 points in a Sprint, its velocity for that Sprint is 21. An item that was 90% complete at the end of the Sprint adds nothing.

Velocity measures output in the team's own units, not value, quality or effort. Two teams can both have a velocity of 30 and be doing very different amounts of work, because each team sizes its points differently. That is why velocity is a planning aid for the team that produces it, and almost useless outside it.

Is velocity part of Scrum?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide, still the current version (checked 26 October 2026), does not use the word velocity. What it does say, in the Sprint Planning section, is that the more the Developers know about their past performance, their upcoming capacity and their Definition of Done, the more confident they will be in their Sprint forecasts. Velocity is simply the most widely used way to record that past performance.

The Guide also notes that various practices exist to forecast progress, such as burn-downs, burn-ups and cumulative flows, and that while proven useful, these do not replace the importance of empiricism. Velocity sits in the same category: helpful, optional and subordinate to what the team actually learns each Sprint.

The term came from Extreme Programming (XP), and Scrum teams adopted it widely. Many teams use velocity alongside other measures, and some use none at all, forecasting instead from item counts or flow metrics. All of these are compatible with Scrum.

How to calculate Scrum velocity, step by step

  1. Agree the unit and the rules. Usually story points; decide once how you treat bugs, spikes and items added mid-Sprint, and write it down.
  2. Record the forecast at Sprint Planning. Note the total estimate of the items selected. This is the committed figure, useful for comparison later.
  3. At the end of the Sprint, list the items that meet the Definition of Done. Do this before the Sprint Review, using the Definition of Done strictly.
  4. Add up their original estimates. Use the estimate agreed before work began, not one revised afterwards.
  5. Give unfinished items zero. Return them to the Product Backlog; they count in full in the Sprint in which they are Done.
  6. Record the result and repeat. After three to five Sprints you have a meaningful average and range.

The formulas

  • Sprint velocity = sum of the estimates of items Done in the Sprint.
  • Average velocity = total points Done over the last *n* Sprints ÷ *n* (most teams use three to five).
  • Velocity range = the lowest and highest velocities over the same Sprints.
  • Say/do ratio = points Done ÷ points forecast at Sprint Planning × 100%.

Worked example: six Sprints of velocity

A Scrum Team of five Developers is building a lettings website and runs two-week Sprints. Here is how it calculated velocity for its fourth Sprint.

Product Backlog itemEstimateDone at Sprint end?Counts
Search properties by postcode8Yes8
Filter results by price and bedrooms5Yes5
Save favourite properties5Yes5
Fix map pins on mobile3Yes3
Accessibility labels on search form3Yes3
Email alerts for new listings5No: about 80% complete, not yet tested0
Total29 forecast24 velocity

The email alerts item goes back to the Product Backlog. The Product Owner places it at the top again, and its 5 points will count in Sprint 5 if it is Done then. Over six Sprints the team's record looks like this:

SprintForecastVelocity (Done)Say/do ratioNote
1261869%First Sprint as a new team
2252496%
3242292%One Developer ill for 3 days
4292483%Email alerts not finished
5252392%
6262596%
  • Average of the last three Sprints: (24 + 23 + 25) ÷ 3 = 24 points.
  • Average of the last five Sprints: (24 + 22 + 24 + 23 + 25) ÷ 5 = 23.6 points.
  • Range over the last five Sprints: 22 to 25 points.
  • Overall say/do ratio: 136 Done ÷ 155 forecast ≈ 88%, and 92% or more in four of the last five Sprints.

Sprint 1 is left out of the averages because a new team's first Sprint is rarely representative. The team now has a stable velocity of about 24, with a narrow range, which makes both Sprint Planning and release forecasts much more reliable.

How velocity is used across the Scrum events

Velocity is not discussed in every event, but it informs several of them. The table shows where it typically appears in practice.

Event or activityHow velocity helpsWhat to avoid
Backlog refinementShows how much refined work the team needs at the top of the backlog: about one to two Sprints' worthRefining far more than velocity suggests the team can use
Sprint PlanningA starting point for how much the Developers forecast they can completeTreating the average as a quota
Daily ScrumNot normally used; the focus is progress toward the Sprint Goal, often with a Sprint burndownTracking points per day or per person
Sprint ReviewUpdates release forecasts for stakeholders: what is likely to be ready, and whenPresenting velocity as a performance score
Sprint RetrospectiveThe trend and the gap between forecast and Done prompt questions about flow, quality and interruptionsBlaming individuals for a low Sprint

In each case velocity starts a conversation; it does not settle one. The Sprint Goal and what the team learned in the Sprint matter more than the number.

Using velocity in Sprint Planning

Continuing the example, the team prepares for Sprint 7. A public holiday falls in the Sprint, so each of the five Developers has 9 working days instead of 10.

  1. Start with the recent average: 24 points (last three Sprints).
  2. Work out availability: 5 × 9 = 45 person-days against a normal 50, a ratio of 0.9.
  3. Scale the average and the range: 24 × 0.9 ≈ 22 points; the range 22 to 25 becomes about 20 to 23.
  4. Agree the Sprint Goal first, then select items from the top of the Product Backlog that support it, stopping at about 20 to 23 points.
  5. Plan the how and sense-check. If the task plan shows one skill overloaded, swap or drop an item, whatever the points say.
  6. Keep the next refined item ready in case the team finishes early.
Tip

Do not subtract an extra allowance for interruptions from velocity. Past velocity already reflects the support work and surprises of past Sprints, so a further buffer would count them twice. Only adjust for what is genuinely different this Sprint, such as holidays.

Velocity is one input among several. The Scrum Guide puts past performance alongside upcoming capacity and the Definition of Done, and many teams cross-check the points with a task plan in hours or days. Forecasting with velocity

Beyond the next Sprint, velocity answers two questions stakeholders ask most: when will it be ready, and how much will we get by a date. Always answer with a range, because a single figure suggests more certainty than any team has.

Fixed scope: how many Sprints?

The lettings team has 110 points of estimated work left for a tenant portal release. Using the last five Sprints:

ScenarioVelocityCalculationSprints needed
Faster25110 ÷ 25 = 4.45
Typical23.6110 ÷ 23.6 = 4.75
Slower22110 ÷ 22 = 5.05
Slower, with 3 points of new scope per Sprint22 − 3 = 19110 ÷ 19 = 5.86

The honest message is: "Five Sprints, about ten weeks, if scope stays steady; six if requests keep arriving at the current rate."

Fixed date: how much scope?

A trade show is four Sprints away. At 22 to 25 points per Sprint, the team can expect to finish 88 to 100 points of work. In the ordered Product Backlog, the top 88 points are likely, the next 12 are possible, and anything beyond 100 points is unlikely. This gives the Product Owner a clear basis for deciding what matters most before the date.

Update both forecasts after every Sprint Review as velocity and the backlog change. For larger or higher-stakes forecasts, techniques such as Monte Carlo simulation use past Sprint results to give probabilities rather than ranges.

Velocity vs capacity vs throughput vs Sprint Goal success

Velocity is often confused with other measures. Each answers a different question, and healthy teams look at more than one.

MeasureQuestion it answersUnitLooks
VelocityHow much estimated work did we finish per Sprint?Story points per SprintBackwards
CapacityHow much time do we have available next Sprint?Person-days or hoursForwards
ThroughputHow many items did we finish per Sprint or week?Items per periodBackwards
Sprint Goal successDid we achieve what the Sprint was for?Goals met ÷ SprintsBackwards
Say/do ratioHow realistic was our forecast?PercentageBackwards

A team can have a steady velocity and still miss its Sprint Goal every time, which usually means it is finishing items that do not add up to the goal, or that the goal was never clear. Equally, a team can meet every Sprint Goal with a modest, steady velocity, which is a far better result.

What affects velocity

Velocity moves for many reasons, and most have nothing to do with how hard people work. The table lists the common ones and what to do about each.

FactorEffect on velocityWhat to do
Holidays, illness, trainingTemporary dropScale the forecast for availability; do not treat as a trend
Team membership changesDrop for several Sprints while people learn and the team re-formsKeep teams stable where possible; expect a dip
Large or unclear itemsSaw-tooth pattern as items finish in alternate SprintsSplit user stories smaller in refinement
Unplanned work and interruptionsLower and less predictableMake it visible; route requests through the Product Owner
Technical debtGradual decline as every change takes longerPay it down steadily as part of each Sprint
Dependencies on other teamsItems stall and carry overSurface dependencies in refinement and Sprint Planning
A stronger Definition of DoneDrop, because each item now includes more workExpected and healthy; start a new baseline
Estimate drift or inflationRise without more value deliveredRe-anchor estimates to reference items; remove targets
Better tools, skills and automationGradual genuine riseNote what changed so the trend makes sense

Velocity when things change

A new team with no history

A new team has no past performance to draw on. Our advice: for the first Sprint, plan by capacity instead, breaking items into tasks and stopping when the available time is used, and treat the result as a learning exercise. Use the actual velocity from Sprints 2 to 4 as your first baseline. SAFe describes a starting method for new teams of about 8 points per full-time developer or tester per two-week iteration, minus one point for each day of leave or public holiday, but even SAFe intends this only until measured velocity is available.

Someone joins or leaves

Expect a dip for a few Sprints. A new member needs time to learn the product, and existing members spend time helping them. Avoid simply scaling velocity by headcount; watch the next two or three Sprints and re-baseline.

Changing Sprint length

Velocity is per Sprint, so a change from two to three weeks breaks the series. Scaling by working days gives a rough first estimate, but the shorter or longer cycle changes how work flows, so treat the next few Sprints as a new baseline.

Changing the Definition of Done

Adding automated testing or security review to the Definition of Done usually lowers velocity because each item now includes more work. That is a sign of higher quality, not lower performance. Mark the change on your chart and compare only with Sprints after it.

Changing the estimation scale

If the team re-calibrates its story points, or switches to counting items, start a new series rather than mixing old and new numbers.

Velocity in Scrum vs flow metrics in Kanban

Velocity depends on fixed-length Sprints, so it fits Scrum naturally. Kanban teams work in a continuous flow and usually measure flow instead. Many Scrum Teams now use both: velocity for Sprint-level planning, and flow metrics to see where work gets stuck inside the Sprint.

Velocity (common in Scrum)Flow metrics (common in Kanban)
MeasuresEstimated work Done per SprintThroughput, cycle time, work item age and work in progress
Needs estimates?Yes, usually story pointsNo; items are counted and timed
Time framePer SprintContinuous, often per week
Best atDeciding how much to take into the next SprintSpotting bottlenecks and forecasting single items
Forecasting methodRemaining points ÷ velocity rangeThroughput history, often with Monte Carlo simulation

If your items are small and similar in size, counting them may give forecasts as good as points with less estimation effort.

Velocity with multiple Scrum Teams

When several Scrum Teams work on one product, it is tempting to add their velocities together or rank them. Both cause problems, because each team's points mean something different.

  • Do not compare teams. A team with a velocity of 40 is not twice as productive as one with 20; it may simply use bigger numbers.
  • Forecast per team, then combine dates, not points. Ask each team when its part is likely to be Done, then look at the combined picture and the dependencies.
  • Normalise only if you must. The Scaled Agile Framework describes optionally aligning teams' story points to a shared reference so that estimates can be added at Agile Release Train level. It helps aggregate forecasting, but it does not make velocities a fair performance comparison.
  • Count items as a cross-check. If all teams split work to similar small sizes, item counts can be compared more safely than points.

Whatever the scale, the Scrum Guide's position holds: forecasts come from each team's own past performance and capacity.

Who owns velocity?

WhoRelationship with velocity
DevelopersOwn their estimates and their velocity; use it to decide how much to take on in Sprint Planning
Product OwnerUses it to forecast releases and order the Product Backlog; does not set it or push for a higher number
Scrum MasterCoaches the team and the organisation to use velocity for planning, not judgement; helps remove the impediments that lower it
Managers and stakeholdersReceive forecasts as ranges; should not use velocity to compare teams or rate individuals

Velocity is always a team measure. Tracking points per person discourages pairing and helping colleagues, which are exactly the behaviours that help a team meet its Sprint Goal.

How to improve velocity the right way

You cannot improve velocity directly without distorting it: ask for more points and estimates grow. What you can do is improve the conditions that let the team finish work, and a steadier, sometimes higher velocity follows.

  • Split items smaller so most finish well within the Sprint.
  • Refine regularly so items arrive at Sprint Planning clear and sized.
  • Limit work in progress inside the Sprint: finish before starting.
  • Protect the Sprint from unplanned requests, and make the ones you accept visible.
  • Invest in automation and technical debt reduction, which speeds every future item.
  • Keep the team stable and give it time to learn together.
  • Use each Retrospective to pick one improvement and check its effect over the next few Sprints.

Velocity anti-patterns and how to avoid them

Goodhart's law, usually paraphrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", explains most velocity problems. These are the patterns we see most often.

Anti-patternWhat happensBetter approach
Velocity targets ("get to 40 points")Estimates inflate; velocity rises while value does notTrack Sprint Goal success and outcomes instead
Comparing teamsTeams game their scales; trust dropsCompare each team only with its own past
Individual velocityPeople stop helping each otherMeasure the team only
Partial credit for unfinished itemsVelocity looks better than reality; hidden unfinished work builds upCount only items that meet the Definition of Done
Re-estimating after the work is knownVelocity becomes self-fulfilling and useless for forecastingKeep the estimate agreed before work began
Weakening the Definition of Done to finish moreQuality falls and defects return laterTreat the Definition of Done as fixed within a Sprint
Planning at the best-ever velocityRepeated misses and pressure to inflatePlan to the recent average
Single-date forecastsStakeholders hear a promiseGive a range and update it every Sprint

Frequently asked questions

What is velocity in Scrum?

The total estimate, usually in story points, of the Product Backlog items a Scrum Team completes to its Definition of Done in a Sprint. Teams use the average over several Sprints for planning and forecasting.

How do you calculate velocity in Scrum?

Add up the original estimates of every item that met the Definition of Done in the Sprint, giving unfinished items zero. For planning, average the last three to five Sprints.

Is velocity part of the Scrum Guide?

No. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention velocity. It refers to the Developers' past performance, upcoming capacity and Definition of Done; velocity is a common way to record past performance.

What is a good velocity?

There is no good number, because each team sizes points differently. A good velocity is a stable one, because stability makes forecasts reliable.

Should velocity increase over time?

Not necessarily. It may rise a little as a team matures, but a steady rise often signals estimate inflation. Aim for stability and better conditions for finishing work.

Do incomplete stories count toward velocity?

No. They count as zero and return to the Product Backlog. Their full estimate counts in the Sprint in which they meet the Definition of Done.

What is the difference between velocity and capacity?

Velocity looks back at how much the team finished; capacity looks forward at how much time it has next Sprint. Teams scale velocity by capacity when planning.

How do you estimate velocity for a new team?

Plan the first Sprint by capacity, breaking items into tasks until the available time is used, then use actual velocity from the next few Sprints as the baseline.

How many Sprints before velocity is reliable?

Usually three to five Sprints with a stable team, a consistent Definition of Done and consistent counting rules.

Can you compare velocity between teams?

No. Story points are relative to each team, so the same number means different amounts of work. Comparisons encourage teams to inflate estimates.

Why does velocity drop?

Common causes are leave, team changes, unplanned work, technical debt, large or unclear items and a stronger Definition of Done. Look for a simple cause first, then discuss sustained drops at the Retrospective.

Is velocity a measure of productivity?

No. It measures output in team-specific units, not value or outcomes. Using it to judge productivity leads to inflated estimates rather than better results.

Who is responsible for velocity?

The Developers own their estimates and velocity. The Product Owner uses it to forecast, and the Scrum Master coaches everyone to use it for planning rather than judgement.

Can you use velocity without story points?

Yes. Some teams count finished items per Sprint instead, which works well when items are consistently small. Kanban teams usually track throughput and cycle time instead.
