Scrum Software Tools: What to Look For and How to Choose

Scrum software tools are applications that help a Scrum Team manage its Product Backlog, plan and track Sprints, and make progress visible to everyone. Scrum does not require any software, but most teams, especially remote and multi-team ones, use a tool, and the right one makes Scrum easier while the wrong one adds bureaucracy. This guide explains which features matter, compares the main options using our review data, and gives you a step-by-step way to choose and set up a tool.

Key takeaways

  • A Scrum tool needs an ordered Product Backlog, Sprint planning, a Sprint board, estimation fields, burndown or velocity reports and a visible Sprint Goal. Everything else is optional.
  • Dedicated Agile trackers such as Jira give the deepest Scrum features; all-in-one platforms such as Wrike, monday.com, Asana and ClickUp suit organisations where Scrum teams work alongside other teams.
  • Wrike, our top-rated tool overall (9.4/10), is a strong choice when Scrum teams need cross-team visibility, portfolio views and resource planning; Jira scores highest for pure software Scrum in our reviews.
  • Choose by trialling two or three tools on a real Sprint, scoring them against weighted criteria and asking the team which helped most.
  • Start with default settings, one Product Backlog per product and as few workflow statuses as possible. Add configuration only when a real problem needs it.
  • Tools support transparency but never replace the conversations in Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Retrospective.

What are Scrum software tools?

Scrum software tools are applications that hold a Scrum Team's Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and progress in one shared place. They let teams order work, plan Sprints, track items on a board, estimate effort and see reports such as burndown charts. Good tools make Scrum's artifacts transparent without changing how the team works.

The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention software at all. A wall, sticky notes and a marker still make a perfectly good Scrum board for a co-located team. Teams adopt software because it solves practical problems: remote and hybrid team members need to see the same board, stakeholders want access without attending every event, reports are tedious to draw by hand, and work items need to link to code, documents and chat.

The risk runs the other way too. A tool configured with dozens of statuses, mandatory fields and approval steps can quietly replace self-management with process. The best Scrum tool is the simplest one that makes your artifacts transparent.

Tip

Before you compare products, write down the three problems you want a tool to solve. If you cannot name them, a physical board or a free plan may be all you need for now.

Useful extras

  • Automation, for example moving an item to Done when its pull request merges, or rolling unfinished items back to the backlog.
  • Cross-team views, such as shared backlogs, dependency links and portfolio dashboards, once you have several teams.
  • Retrospective boards built into the tool, or an integration with a whiteboard app.
  • Workload and capacity views that show who is overloaded across teams.
  • AI assistance for drafting stories, summarising threads or answering questions about progress. Useful, but check which plan includes it.

The main Scrum tools in brief

Each tool below can support Scrum. The notes focus on how well each one fits Scrum specifically; follow the links for full reviews.

Wrike

Wrike is our top-rated project management tool overall (9.4/10). For Scrum, it offers board views on every plan, including Free, plus ready-made Agile Teamwork, Sprint Planning and Sprint Retrospective templates, custom workflows, workload views and dashboards. Its main strength for Scrum is what sits around the team: cross-project dashboards, portfolio views and resource planning that help when several Scrum teams, marketing and operations share one platform, or when you are scaling Scrum. Our review rates its Sprint tooling as partial and its Agile score as 8/10: it is less specialised in Scrum reports than Jira. Wrike Sync, an add-on included in Apex, offers two-way sync with Jira and GitHub so developers and business teams can each stay in their preferred tool.

Jira

Jira is the most complete Agile tool for software teams that we review, with a 10/10 Agile score. Backlogs, epics, story points, Sprints, Scrum boards, burndown, velocity and cumulative flow reports, and deep links to code are all mature, and the Free plan covers up to 10 users. The trade-offs are a steep learning curve and real administration effort, and non-technical teams often find it heavy. Compare the two leaders in Wrike vs Jira.

ClickUp

ClickUp is one of the stronger general-purpose tools for Scrum (9/10 for Agile). Sprints are dated Lists, unfinished work can roll over automatically, and the Business plan adds sprint points plus burndown, burnup, velocity and cumulative flow reporting. Docs for specifications sit next to the work. Expect to invest time in setup; reviewers mention a dense interface and occasional lag.

monday.com

monday.com is one of the easiest capable platforms to roll out. Work management boards handle simple Sprints, and monday.com sells a separate product, monday dev, for Sprints, roadmaps and bug tracking. It suits organisations where many non-technical teams also need workflows and dashboards.

Asana

Asana is approachable and strong at coordinating work across teams and linking it to goals. It supports boards and Sprint-style projects; teams that rely on backlogs, story points and Agile reports will find dedicated tools deeper. Product teams often pair Asana with Jira or GitHub through integrations.

Microsoft Planner

Planner's premium plans (Plan 1 and above) add a prioritised backlog and time-boxed Sprints inside Microsoft 365, which suits light Agile work. Microsoft does not document story points, velocity or burndown, so Scrum teams that rely on these should test carefully.

Notion

Notion can add Sprints to a task database, with Current sprint, Sprint planning and Backlog views, a choice of what happens to unfinished tasks and optional automated Sprints that start and close on schedule. It is excellent for specs and decision logs next to the work, but lacks packaged velocity and burndown reports.

Trello

Trello is the quickest way to get a Sprint board in front of a small team. Custom fields (Standard plan and above) or Power-Ups can hold story points, but there is no native backlog management or Sprint reporting. Jira, from the same company, is the natural next step.

Smartsheet and Basecamp

Smartsheet offers a board view on a grid-first platform and suits teams that plan in spreadsheets; its Sprint tooling is limited compared with Jira. Basecamp is designed for calm, communication-centred projects and has no backlogs, Sprints or velocity reports, so Scrum teams usually pair it with, or choose, a dedicated tool.

Other options

Outside our reviews, Azure DevOps (Azure Boards) is common in Microsoft-centred engineering teams: its Basic plan is free for the first five users, then $6 per user per month, and includes backlogs, Sprint planning and Kanban boards (checked 4 November 2026). Linear is popular with product and engineering teams that like a fast, keyboard-driven tracker; it calls Sprints cycles. Check both vendors' current plans directly.

How to choose a Scrum tool, step by step

A structured selection takes two to four weeks and avoids choosing on demos alone.

  1. List your problems and must-haves. Start from the feature table above. Mark each feature as must-have, nice-to-have or not needed.
  2. Identify who will use it. Developers, Product Owners, Scrum Masters, stakeholders and other teams have different needs. Include at least one person from each group in the selection.
  3. Set weighted criteria. Typical criteria are Scrum features, cross-team visibility, ease of adoption, integrations, scalability and cost. Weights should add up to 100%.
  4. Shortlist two or three tools. Use the situation table and our tool reviews to narrow the field.
  5. Trial on a real Sprint. Set up your actual backlog, run one full Sprint, including all events, in each tool or in parallel teams. Sample data hides friction.
  6. Score each tool. Ask trial users to score each criterion from 1 to 5, then calculate weighted scores.
  7. Check the total cost. Include the plan you will really need (for example, story points or dashboards may need a higher tier), add-ons, AI usage and admin time.
  8. Decide and plan the rollout. Choose, agree conventions, migrate the backlog and review the decision after three or four Sprints.

How to set up a Scrum tool

Whatever you choose, these steps keep the tool working for Scrum rather than against it.

  1. Create one Product Backlog per product. Several teams on one product should draw from the same backlog, filtered by team if needed.
  2. Keep the workflow short. Start with To do, In progress and Done, and add a column such as In review only if it shows a real wait state.
  3. Add only the fields you need. Item type, estimate, Sprint, priority order and acceptance criteria are usually enough.
  4. Set the Sprint length and naming. Use consistent dates and names so reports line up.
  5. Make the Sprint Goal visible on the board or Sprint, not buried in a document.
  6. Link the Definition of Done. Add it as a checklist template or a pinned page so every item shows the quality bar. See our guide to the Definition of Done.
  7. Switch on the core reports: burndown or burnup, velocity and cumulative flow if available.
  8. Give stakeholders read access or a shared dashboard so they can follow progress between Sprint Reviews.
  9. Connect integrations for code, chat and documents so the board stays current without double entry.
  10. Agree conventions for writing items, using labels and moving cards, and write them down in one short page.

Free and low-cost Scrum tools

Several tools offer free plans that can run real Sprints. The limits below come from our reviews (checked 7 October 2026).

  • Jira Free: up to 10 users, with Scrum and Kanban boards, backlogs, the timeline, reports and basic automation. One of the most capable free options for software teams.
  • ClickUp Free Forever: unlimited members, Board view and Sprints, but 60MB of storage and trial-only Dashboards; sprint points need Business.
  • Wrike Free: board and table views with basic task and project management; Gantt charts and shareable dashboards start on Team.
  • Trello Free: up to 10 collaborators per Workspace with simple boards; no native Sprint reports.
  • Notion Free: task databases with Sprint views, good for small teams that also want docs.
  • Azure DevOps Basic: free for the first five users.

Free plans are a good way to test whether a tool suits your way of working. Pay when a real problem, such as reporting, more users or integrations, needs a paid feature.

Can you run Scrum with a spreadsheet or physical board?

Yes, within limits. A physical board is excellent for a co-located team: it is always visible, easy to change and encourages conversation at the Daily Scrum. A spreadsheet can hold an ordered backlog, a Sprint plan and a simple burndown.

The limits appear as soon as people work remotely, stakeholders want live visibility or you run more than one team. Spreadsheets have no live board, weak collaboration on individual items and manual reports. Treat them as a starting point or a stopgap.

If you want to start that way, our free product backlog template and Sprint planning template give you a ready-made structure, and the columns map directly onto most Scrum tools when you are ready to move.

Tips for adopting a Scrum tool

  • Start with default settings and add fields, statuses and automations only when the team agrees a real need.
  • Keep one Product Backlog per product, ordered by the Product Owner.
  • Agree conventions for writing items, labels and estimates, and keep them on one page.
  • Train by doing. Run Sprint Planning in the tool from day one rather than holding a separate training session.
  • Use the board at the Daily Scrum so it stays current.
  • Review the tool in a Retrospective after three or four Sprints. Ask what helps, what gets in the way and what to switch off.
  • Name a tool owner to handle administration, but keep decisions about the workflow with the team.

Common mistakes when choosing and using Scrum tools

  • Choosing on a demo. Demos use perfect sample data. Always trial on a real Sprint.
  • Letting the tool define the process. If a default workflow does not match how the team works, change the workflow, not the team.
  • Over-configuring. Ten statuses and fifteen mandatory fields slow everyone down and hide the real state of work.
  • Several backlogs for one product. Teams pull against each other and the Product Owner loses control of order.
  • Using reports to judge people. Velocity and burndown are planning aids, not performance targets. See our guide to Scrum metrics and KPIs.
  • Forcing one tool on very different teams without checking their needs. Shared reporting matters, but so does adoption.
  • Ignoring total cost. Key Scrum features such as story points, dashboards or AI may sit on higher plans or add-ons.
  • Replacing conversations with comments. A comment thread is not a Sprint Review.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best Scrum tool?

There is no single best tool. In our reviews Jira scores highest for Agile and software teams, while Wrike is our top-rated tool overall and suits Scrum teams that need cross-team visibility and resource planning. ClickUp, monday.com and Asana also support Scrum, with different strengths.

Do we need a paid Scrum tool?

No. Many teams start with a physical board or a free plan such as Jira Free or ClickUp Free Forever. Pay for features that solve real problems, such as reporting, more users or integrations.

Is Jira a Scrum tool?

Jira is a general work management and issue-tracking tool with very strong Scrum support: backlogs, Sprints, story points, Scrum boards and burndown and velocity reports, all available on its Free plan for up to 10 users.

Can Wrike be used for Scrum?

Yes. Wrike offers board views, Agile Teamwork, Sprint Planning and Sprint Retrospective templates, custom workflows, workload views and dashboards. Its strength is visibility across teams and projects; for the deepest Scrum-specific reporting, software teams often use Jira, which Wrike can sync with through its Wrike Sync add-on.

Can Trello work as a Scrum tool?

Yes, for simple setups. Lists for each stage and custom fields or Power-Ups for story points can work, but backlog management and Sprint reporting are limited.

What features should a Scrum tool have?

An ordered Product Backlog, Sprint planning with capacity, a Sprint board, estimation fields, burndown or velocity reports, a visible Sprint Goal, stakeholder access and integrations with code and chat tools.

Can Excel be used as a Scrum tool?

A spreadsheet can hold a backlog, a Sprint plan and a simple burndown, but it lacks a live board and item-level collaboration. It is a sensible stopgap rather than a long-term solution for remote or multi-team work.

Are there free Scrum tools?

Yes. Jira Free (up to 10 users), ClickUp Free Forever, Wrike Free, Trello Free, Notion Free and Azure DevOps Basic (first five users) can all support small teams. Check each vendor's current limits.

What is the difference between Scrum and Kanban tools?

Scrum tools add time-boxed Sprints, Sprint Goals, estimation and Sprint reports such as burndown and velocity. Kanban tools focus on continuous flow, WIP limits and flow metrics. Most modern tools support both.

Should all teams use the same Scrum tool?

It helps for coordination and reporting across teams, but forcing one tool on very different teams can backfire. A common compromise is one platform for planning and reporting, with integrations for teams that need specialised tools.

Who should own the Scrum tool?

Anyone can administer it, often a Scrum Master or a tool administrator. What matters is that the team decides how the workflow works and the tool supports that, rather than dictating it.

How do you choose between Scrum tools?

List must-have features, set weighted criteria, trial two or three tools on a real Sprint, score them with the people who used them and check the total cost of the plan you really need.

Do Scrum tools replace Scrum events?

No. Tools support transparency, but the conversations at Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Retrospective cannot be replaced by software.

Which Scrum tool is best for non-software teams?

Teams in marketing, HR or operations usually adopt easier, more visual platforms such as Wrike, monday.com or Asana faster than developer-focused trackers. Choose based on your trial and how much Sprint reporting you need.
