The Scrum Guide: Learn Scrum Step by Step

This Scrum guide explains how Scrum works in plain English: a small team delivers value in short, fixed-length Sprints, inspecting the result and adapting its plan as it goes. Start with the summary below, follow the reading path for your role, then go deeper with 35 practical guides to the Scrum Team, events, artifacts, estimation and scaling.

What is Scrum?

Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organisations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems, as the official Scrum Guide puts it. A small, cross-functional Scrum Team works in Sprints of one month or less, produces a usable Increment each Sprint and uses regular events to inspect progress and adapt the plan.

Scrum was created by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, who first presented it publicly in 1995. They published the first Scrum Guide in 2010 and have revised it several times since; the current version is the 2020 Scrum Guide. That revision made Scrum less software-specific and less prescriptive: it replaced "roles" with accountabilities, merged the Development Team into a single Scrum Team with Developers, and added the Product Goal and the idea of a commitment for each artifact.

In June 2025, Ralph Jocham, John Coleman and Jeff Sutherland published the Scrum Guide Expansion Pack, which adds context, theory and examples around the framework. It is a supplement, not a replacement: the 2020 Scrum Guide remains the definition of Scrum.

This hub is our own, independent guide. Where we describe a rule of Scrum, we follow the 2020 Scrum Guide; where we recommend a practice or format, we say it is our advice or common practice.

Scrum at a glance: the 3-5-3 structure

People often summarise Scrum as 3-5-3: three accountabilities, five events and three artifacts. The table pulls the essentials from the Scrum Guide into one place. Time-boxes are the maximum for a one-month Sprint and are usually shorter for shorter Sprints.

ElementWhat it isKey rule
Product OwnerAccountable for maximising the value of the product and for the Product BacklogOne person, not a committee
Scrum MasterAccountable for the Scrum Team's effectiveness and for establishing ScrumServes the team, the Product Owner and the organisation
DevelopersThe people who create a usable Increment each SprintOwn the Sprint Backlog and the plan for the Sprint
The SprintThe container for all other eventsFixed length of one month or less
Sprint PlanningSets why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done and howUp to 8 hours
Daily ScrumDevelopers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt15 minutes, every working day
Sprint ReviewInspects the outcome with stakeholders and adapts the Product BacklogUp to 4 hours
Sprint RetrospectivePlans ways to improve quality and effectivenessUp to 3 hours
Product BacklogOrdered list of what is needed to improve the productCommitment: Product Goal
Sprint BacklogSprint Goal, selected items and the plan to deliver themCommitment: Sprint Goal
IncrementA usable step toward the Product GoalCommitment: Definition of Done

The Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer people, with no sub-teams or hierarchies. Underneath the structure sit the three pillars of empiricism (transparency, inspection and adaptation) and five values (commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage).

How Scrum works: one Sprint step by step

Every Sprint follows the same rhythm. Here is what happens from start to finish.

  1. The Product Owner keeps the Product Backlog ordered toward a clear Product Goal, and the team refines upcoming items so they are small and clear enough to select.
  2. Sprint Planning starts the Sprint. The team agrees a Sprint Goal, selects Product Backlog items and plans how to deliver them, creating the Sprint Backlog. See Sprint Planning.
  3. The Developers work toward the Sprint Goal, meeting for the Daily Scrum to inspect progress and adjust the plan.
  4. Work that meets the Definition of Done becomes part of the Increment. The team can release it to users whenever it is ready, even before the Sprint ends.
  5. The Sprint Review shows stakeholders what was achieved and gathers feedback, which updates the Product Backlog.
  6. The Sprint Retrospective closes the Sprint. The team decides on the most helpful improvements to how it works.
  7. The next Sprint starts immediately, with the improved backlog and ways of working.

Official Scrum vs common Scrum practices

The Scrum Guide is deliberately short. It defines what Scrum requires and leaves teams to choose their own practices. Many things people associate with Scrum come from those choices, which is why different Scrum teams can look quite different.

Defined in the Scrum GuideCommon complementary practices
Product Owner, Scrum Master and DevelopersJob titles such as "Scrum Master" as a full-time role, Agile coach
The Sprint and its four eventsThe term "Scrum ceremonies", Sprint 0, hardening Sprints
Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and IncrementUser stories, epics, story mapping
Product Goal, Sprint Goal and Definition of DoneDefinition of Ready, acceptance criteria templates
Refinement as an ongoing activityA regular backlog refinement meeting
Monitoring progress toward goalsStory points, velocity, burndown and burnup charts, planning poker
Transparency of the artifactsPhysical or digital Scrum boards and software tools

Complementary practices are not wrong; many are very useful. The point is to treat them as choices you can change when they stop helping, rather than as rules. Our guide to Scrum techniques covers the most useful ones.

Scrum compared with Agile, Kanban and Waterfall

ComparisonThe short answerRead more
Scrum vs AgileAgile is a set of values and principles; Scrum is one framework for putting them into practiceAgile vs Scrum
Scrum vs KanbanScrum works in fixed Sprints with defined accountabilities; Kanban manages continuous flow with WIP limitsKanban vs Scrum
Scrum vs WaterfallScrum plans and delivers in short cycles; Waterfall plans the whole project up front and delivers in sequenceScrum vs Waterfall
Scrum vs LeanLean focuses on flow and removing waste; Scrum adds a team structure and cadenceScrum vs Lean

Many teams combine approaches, for example Scrum with Kanban-style WIP limits, or Scrum teams delivering inside a wider predictive programme. The Scrum Guide allows this as long as the Scrum framework itself is kept intact.

Scrum templates and tools

You can run Scrum with a whiteboard and sticky notes, but templates and software help once the team is distributed or the backlog grows.

  • Sprint planning template to record the Sprint Goal, selected items and capacity.
  • Product backlog template to order and refine backlog items.
  • User story template for writing items with acceptance criteria.
  • Sprint retrospective template to capture improvements and follow them up.

For software, Jira is the specialist choice for software teams running Scrum, with backlogs, Sprints and Agile reports. Teams mixing Scrum with Gantt-based planning or work from other departments often prefer an all-in-one platform such as Wrike, which ranks first in our project management tools ranking.

Common mistakes when adopting Scrum

  • Renaming old roles and meetings without changing how decisions are made, for example a project manager assigning tasks under the title of Scrum Master.
  • No clear Sprint Goal, so the Sprint becomes a list of unrelated tasks and the Daily Scrum turns into a status report.
  • A weak or missing Definition of Done, which leaves "almost finished" work piling up and makes the Increment unusable.
  • A Product Owner without authority to order the backlog, or a committee acting as Product Owner.
  • Treating velocity as a target or comparing it between teams, which encourages inflated estimates.
  • Skipping the Retrospective when the team is busy, which removes the event that makes every other improvement possible.
Tip

If you change only one thing, make the Sprint Goal real: one clear objective, agreed in Sprint Planning, that the whole team uses to make trade-offs during the Sprint.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Scrum Guide?

The Scrum Guide is the official, freely available definition of Scrum, written by Scrum's creators Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. It sets out Scrum theory, values, the Scrum Team, events, artifacts and commitments. This hub is our independent, practical companion to it.

What is the latest version of the Scrum Guide?

The current official version is the November 2020 Scrum Guide. The Scrum Guide Expansion Pack, published in June 2025 by Ralph Jocham, John Coleman and Jeff Sutherland, adds context and examples but does not replace it.

What is the 3-5-3 rule in Scrum?

It is shorthand for Scrum's structure: three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), five events (the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective) and three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Increment).

What are the 3 roles in Scrum?

The 2020 Scrum Guide calls them accountabilities: the Product Owner, the Scrum Master and the Developers. Together they form one Scrum Team with no sub-teams or hierarchies. See Scrum team roles.

What are the 5 Scrum events?

The Sprint, Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. The Sprint contains the other four. See Scrum ceremonies.

What are the 3 Scrum artifacts?

The Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment. Each has a commitment: the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done. See Scrum artifacts.

What are the pillars and values of Scrum?

The three pillars are transparency, inspection and adaptation. The five values are commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage.

How long is a Sprint in Scrum?

One month or less, and the same length each time. Many teams choose one or two weeks. Shorter Sprints mean more frequent feedback and less risk per Sprint.

How big should a Scrum Team be?

The Scrum Guide says a Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer people: small enough to stay nimble, large enough to complete significant work within a Sprint.

Is Scrum the same as Agile?

No. Agile is a mindset described by the Agile Manifesto's values and principles. Scrum is one specific framework for working in an Agile way. See Agile vs Scrum.

Can Scrum be used outside software development?

Yes. The 2020 Scrum Guide was written to be less software-specific, and teams use Scrum for marketing, product, research, operations and other complex work where requirements change.

How do I learn Scrum and get certified?

Read the Scrum Guide and our beginner reading path above, practise on a real team, then consider a certification such as PSM or CSM for Scrum Masters, or PSPO or CSPO for Product Owners. See Scrum certification and training.
