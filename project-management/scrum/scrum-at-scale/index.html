Scrum@Scale Framework: Roles, Cycles and How to Start

Scrum@Scale is a framework created by Jeff Sutherland, co-creator of Scrum, for scaling Scrum from one team to a whole organisation by linking small teams into networks that work the same way at every level. It adds a Scrum Master cycle for how work gets done, a Product Owner cycle for what gets built, and two leadership forums, the Executive Action Team and the Executive MetaScrum. This guide explains every component, walks through an illustrative example and gives you a step-by-step way to start.

Key takeaways

  • Scrum@Scale extends Scrum rather than replacing it. Each team still runs Scrum as the 2020 Scrum Guide describes; Scrum@Scale only adds what is needed to coordinate many teams.
  • Its structure is scale-free: teams of about five people, grouped four or five at a time into a Scrum of Scrums, which can be grouped again into a Scrum of Scrum of Scrums.
  • Two cycles run in parallel. The Scrum Master cycle covers impediment removal, cross-team coordination and delivery; the Product Owner cycle covers vision, prioritisation, refinement and release planning.
  • The Executive Action Team acts as the Scrum Master for the whole organisation, and the Executive MetaScrum acts as its Product Owner, meeting at least once per Sprint.
  • Start small: get one team working well, build a reference model of a few teams, then scale out. Scaling poor Scrum only scales the problems.
  • Compared with SAFe, Scrum@Scale is lighter and less prescriptive; compared with LeSS and Nexus, it reaches further into executive leadership and organisational change.

What is Scrum@Scale?

Scrum@Scale is a framework for scaling Scrum across a team of teams, a department or a whole organisation. It links small Scrum Teams into networks, coordinates them through a Scrum Master cycle and a Product Owner cycle, and gives leadership two forums to remove impediments and set priorities. It aims to use the least bureaucracy needed to deliver value.

Jeff Sutherland developed Scrum@Scale with Scrum Inc. from fieldwork with organisations of many sizes. Its official definition is The Scrum@Scale Guide, currently version 2.1 (February 2022), which is published free under a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike licence at scrumatscale.com. Like the Scrum Guide, it is short and deliberately incomplete: it tells you which components you need and leaves the detailed practices to you.

The Scrum Guide describes a single Scrum Team of ten or fewer people. Once a product needs many teams, you need answers Scrum does not give on its own: how teams integrate their work every Sprint, how one set of priorities reaches every team, and who removes impediments above team level. Scrum@Scale answers them with Scrum's own building blocks.

Tip

Scrum@Scale assumes good team-level Scrum. If your teams cannot yet deliver a Done Increment each Sprint, fix that first. Scaling multiplies whatever you already have.

Scale-free architecture and minimum viable bureaucracy

Two ideas shape everything else in Scrum@Scale.

Scale-free architecture

In Scrum@Scale, the same pattern repeats at every level. A small team runs Scrum. A handful of teams form a Scrum of Scrums (SoS), which itself behaves like a Scrum Team, with its own backlog, events and accountabilities. A handful of Scrums of Scrums form a Scrum of Scrum of Scrums (SoSoS), and so on. Because each level works the same way, the organisation can grow without inventing a new type of structure each time, and people moving between levels already understand how things work.

Why teams of about five

The guide points to research by J. Richard Hackman suggesting that the average optimal team size is about 4.6 people, and applies the same logic to groups of teams. That is why it recommends four or five teams per Scrum of Scrums. These are guidelines, not rules.

Minimum viable bureaucracy

The guide defines minimum viable bureaucracy as the least amount of governing bodies and processes needed to carry out the organisation's functions without slowing the delivery of customer value. In practice it means every meeting, approval and role you add should earn its place. If a steering committee duplicates what the Executive MetaScrum already decides, Scrum@Scale would have you remove the committee rather than keep both.

If you already run a Scrum of Scrums, you have the first building block. Scrum@Scale adds the Product Owner side, the organisation-level forums and the feedback loops that connect them.

The Scrum Master cycle: how the work gets done

The Scrum Master cycle is about delivery and continuous improvement. It is led by Scrum Masters at team level, the Scrum of Scrums Master at the next level and the Executive Action Team at the top. It has three components.

Continuous improvement and impediment removal

Teams surface impediments in their Daily Scrums and Retrospectives. Anything a team cannot fix itself goes up to the Scrum of Scrums, and anything the Scrum of Scrums cannot fix goes to the Executive Action Team. The aim is to remove impediments at the lowest level possible and to spread successful improvements across teams rather than leaving them in one team's notes.

Cross-team coordination

Teams working on one product must manage dependencies, share interfaces and avoid blocking each other. The Scrum of Scrums handles this through the Scaled Daily Scrum and through shared practices such as a common Definition of Done. The goal is fewer hand-offs, not more meetings.

Delivery

The guide expects the teams in a Scrum of Scrums to produce a fully integrated set of potentially shippable Increments at the end of every Sprint. Delivery is a joint responsibility: one team's Done work is not enough if the integrated product does not work.

The Product Owner cycle: what gets built and why

The Product Owner cycle sets direction. It is led by Product Owners at team level, a Chief Product Owner and Product Owner Team for each Scrum of Scrums, and the Executive MetaScrum at the top. It has four components.

Strategic vision

A clear, shared vision explains why the organisation and its products exist and where they are heading. The guide asks for it to be communicated both internally and externally, and revisited as conditions change. Without it, every team optimises for its own backlog.

Backlog prioritisation

Priorities must follow a single path from the executive level down to each team. The Chief Product Owner creates one ordered backlog for the Scrum of Scrums, and team Product Backlogs align with it. A product backlog template helps you keep the order visible.

Backlog decomposition and refinement

Large initiatives are broken into items that individual teams can finish in a Sprint. The Product Owner Team does this together so dependencies are spotted before work starts, using the same habits as team-level backlog refinement.

Release planning

Release planning looks further ahead than a single Sprint. It forecasts when valuable groups of features are likely to ship, communicates expectations to stakeholders and shows the likely financial impact. See our guide to agile release planning and the release plan template for a practical format.

Where the two cycles meet

The cycles are drawn as two loops that touch at three points. These are where the how and the what must stay in step.

  • Team process. The first point of contact: each team runs Scrum as the Scrum Guide describes, aiming to maximise the flow of Done work, improve performance over time, work at a sustainable pace and speed up customer feedback.
  • Product and release feedback. What customers and stakeholders say about released work feeds back into the vision, the backlog and the way teams work.
  • Metrics and transparency. Everyone can see the same data about progress, value and quality, so decisions are based on evidence rather than status opinions.

The guide suggests a minimum set of four metric categories. The examples in the right-hand column are illustrative; choose measures that fit your product and keep the set small. Our guide to Scrum metrics and KPIs covers how to calculate the common ones.

CategoryWhat it tells youExample measures
ProductivityIs the system delivering more working product?Change in working product delivered per Sprint; integrated Increments released
Value deliveryIs the work worth doing?Business value per unit of team effort; adoption of new features
QualityIs what we deliver reliable?Defect rate; service downtime; escaped defects per release
SustainabilityCan people keep working this way?Team happiness or engagement surveys; unplanned overtime

Scrum@Scale roles and accountabilities

Scrum@Scale keeps the three Scrum accountabilities at team level and adds a small number of scaled ones. Many organisations fill scaled roles with people who already hold a team-level role, which keeps the structure lean.

Role or forumAccountable forWho usually fills it
Scrum of Scrums Master (SoSM)Making sure scaled events happen, stay productive and stay within their timeboxes; coordinating delivery with release plans; removing cross-team impedimentsOne of the teams' Scrum Masters or a dedicated person
Chief Product Owner (CPO)Strategic vision for the group, a single ordered backlog, choice of metrics, assessing feedback and facilitating the MetaScrumAn experienced Product Owner or product leader
Product Owner TeamMaking sure teams' priorities follow one path; a coordinated roadmap and release planThe CPO plus each team's Product Owner
Executive Action Team (EAT)The Scrum Master accountabilities for the whole organisation: the agile operating model, funding the Product Owner organisation, measuring Scrum quality, removing organisational impedimentsSenior leaders with authority over structure, budget and policy
Executive MetaScrum (EMS)The Product Owner accountabilities for the whole organisation: vision, priorities, budgets and realigning teamsExecutives and key stakeholders, facilitated by a Chief Product Owner

Two points are easy to miss. First, the EAT is not a status committee: it has its own backlog of transformation initiatives and should work it in Sprints. Second, the Executive MetaScrum is where priorities and budgets change. The guide says decisions of that kind should be made in this forum and at no other time during the Sprint, which protects teams from mid-Sprint priority swings.

For the team-level accountabilities, see our guides to Scrum Team roles, the Scrum Master role and the Product Owner.

Hold the Scaled Daily Scrum shortly after the team Daily Scrums and keep it to cross-team questions: what affects other teams, and what is blocking us that we cannot fix alone. Our guide to the Daily Scrum covers the team-level version.

Worked example: scaling from three teams to twelve

The following example is illustrative. The company, numbers and timings are invented to show how the parts fit together.

Starting point. A payments company has three Scrum Teams building a merchant app on two-week Sprints. Each team has its own Product Owner and backlog. Releases slip because teams integrate only at the end of each quarter, and the sales director regularly asks individual developers for urgent changes.

Months 1–3: build the reference model

  • The three teams form a Scrum of Scrums. One Scrum Master becomes the Scrum of Scrums Master alongside her team role.
  • The most experienced Product Owner becomes Chief Product Owner. The three Product Owners merge their backlogs into one ordered backlog for the merchant app, with team backlogs drawn from it.
  • Teams agree a shared Definition of Done that includes integration testing, so each Sprint ends with one integrated Increment rather than three separate ones.
  • A 15-minute Scaled Daily Scrum starts at 9.45, after the team Daily Scrums at 9.15.
  • The chief technology officer, head of product, head of sales and HR director form an Executive Action Team with a short backlog: approve continuous integration tooling, move a shared database specialist into a team, and agree how urgent sales requests reach the Chief Product Owner instead of developers.

Months 4–6: add the executive forums

  • An Executive MetaScrum meets on the first Monday of each Sprint for 60 minutes. Sales requests are now negotiated there and ordered in the single backlog.
  • The team measures four things: integrated features released per Sprint, merchant adoption of new features, escaped defects and a monthly team happiness score.
  • Integration now happens every Sprint. The first quarterly release under the new model ships on the planned date.

Months 7–12: scale out

  • Demand grows, and the company adds a consumer app. It forms nine more teams over six months, two at a time, rather than all at once.
  • The 12 teams become three Scrums of Scrums of four teams each: merchant, consumer and platform. Each has its own SoSM and Chief Product Owner.
  • A Scrum of Scrum of Scrums coordinates the three groups. Its Scaled Daily Scrum has three representatives, one from each SoS.
  • The Executive MetaScrum now includes the three Chief Product Owners, who bring a single ordered list of cross-product priorities for leaders to agree.

Notice what did not change: each team still runs all five Scrum events. Scrum@Scale only added the coordination each new level needed.

How to implement Scrum@Scale step by step

The guide recommends incremental, prioritised change rather than a big-bang launch. A practical sequence looks like this.

  1. Check team-level Scrum. Each team should be able to deliver a Done Increment every Sprint and run all five events well. Use the Sprint Retrospective to fix gaps before adding scale.
  2. Form the Executive Action Team. Choose leaders who can change structure, budget and policy. Give the EAT a transformation backlog, a Product Owner and a Scrum Master of its own, and ask it to work in Sprints.
  3. Pick a reference model. Choose one product area with four or five teams that need to work together. This becomes your prototype for the rest of the organisation.
  4. Set up the Product Owner side. Appoint a Chief Product Owner, form a Product Owner Team and create one ordered backlog for the group. Agree a shared vision statement.
  5. Set up the Scrum Master side. Appoint a Scrum of Scrums Master, start the Scaled Daily Scrum and Scaled Retrospective, and agree a shared Definition of Done that includes integration.
  6. Start the Executive MetaScrum. Hold it at least once per Sprint. Make it the only place where priorities, budgets and team alignment change mid-stream.
  7. Agree a small set of metrics. Pick one or two measures for productivity, value delivery, quality and sustainability, and make them visible to everyone.
  8. Inspect the reference model for several Sprints. Use the EAT and Scaled Retrospective to fix what does not work before you copy it.
  9. Scale out in small steps. Add new Scrums of Scrums one at a time, using the reference model as the pattern. When you have four or five SoS, add a Scrum of Scrum of Scrums.
  10. Keep removing bureaucracy. Review existing committees, reports and approvals against the new forums and retire anything that duplicates them.

For the wider organisational change, see our guide to agile transformation.

Scrum@Scale vs SAFe, LeSS and Nexus

Scrum@Scale is one of several scaling frameworks. None is best for every organisation; the table summarises the main differences so you can choose based on your situation.

In short, choose SAFe if you need a detailed blueprint and portfolio governance, LeSS or Nexus if you have one product and want to stay as close to plain Scrum as possible, and Scrum@Scale if you want a light, modular approach that extends to leadership. Common Scrum@Scale mistakes

  • Scaling before teams are ready. If teams cannot reliably meet their Sprint Goals, adding scaled events increases overhead without improving delivery.
  • Treating the EAT as a steering committee. An EAT that only receives reports and does not work a backlog of impediments will not change much.
  • Several backlogs with no single order. If each Product Owner prioritises independently, teams pull against each other. The Chief Product Owner must own one order.
  • Changing priorities outside the MetaScrum. Side-channel requests to individual teams undermine the whole Product Owner cycle.
  • Scaled Daily Scrums that become status meetings. Keep them to cross-team impediments and dependencies, and keep them to 15 minutes.
  • Separate Definitions of Done. Teams that do not share integration standards cannot produce one integrated Increment.
  • Adding bureaucracy instead of removing it. Keeping old governance alongside the new forums doubles the overhead.
  • Measuring activity rather than outcomes. Story points completed across teams say little; track value delivered, quality and sustainability.

Tools for running Scrum@Scale

Scrum@Scale does not require any particular software, but once you have several teams you need shared visibility of one backlog, cross-team dependencies and the metrics in your transparency layer.

  • Wrike (our top-rated tool, 9.4) works well when Scrum@Scale reaches beyond software teams. Its cross-project dashboards, custom workflows and portfolio views help an Executive MetaScrum and EAT see priorities, dependencies and progress in one place.
  • Jira is widely used by software teams for Scrum boards, backlogs and Sprint reports, and Atlassian offers further tooling for scaled planning.
  • monday.com, Asana and ClickUp all support Scrum-style boards, shared backlogs and dashboards that suit teams of teams, with different strengths in ease of use and breadth of features.

Whatever you choose, make every team draw from the same ordered backlog. Our roundup of Scrum software tools compares the options in more detail, and our guide to agile portfolio management explains how to connect team work to strategic priorities.

Scrum@Scale training and certification

Scrum Inc. and its registered trainers run a Scrum@Scale Practitioner course. The credential is usually listed as Registered Scrum@Scale Practitioner (RS@SP), though some providers use slightly different names. Scrum Inc. also runs a programme for people who want to become Scrum@Scale trainers. Course formats, prices and exam rules change, so check the official Scrum@Scale or Scrum Inc. website before you book.

If you are new to Scrum, a foundation Scrum Master or Product Owner certificate is usually the better first step. See our comparison of Scrum certification and training.

Frequently asked questions

What is Scrum@Scale?

Scrum@Scale is a framework created by Jeff Sutherland for scaling Scrum across an organisation. It links small Scrum Teams into networks, coordinates them through a Scrum Master cycle and a Product Owner cycle, and adds two leadership forums, the Executive Action Team and the Executive MetaScrum.

Who created Scrum@Scale?

Jeff Sutherland, co-creator of Scrum, developed Scrum@Scale with Scrum Inc. The official Scrum@Scale Guide is free to read at scrumatscale.com; the current version is 2.1, published in February 2022.

Is Scrum@Scale the same as Scrum of Scrums?

No. A Scrum of Scrums is one building block of Scrum@Scale: a group of four or five teams that coordinate their work. Scrum@Scale adds the Product Owner cycle, the Executive Action Team, the Executive MetaScrum and shared metrics around it.

What are the two cycles in Scrum@Scale?

The Scrum Master cycle covers how work is delivered: continuous improvement and impediment removal, cross-team coordination and delivery. The Product Owner cycle covers what is built: strategic vision, backlog prioritisation, backlog decomposition and refinement, and release planning.

What is the Executive Action Team in Scrum@Scale?

The Executive Action Team (EAT) fulfils the Scrum Master accountabilities for the whole organisation. Its members are leaders who can change structure, budget and policy. It removes impediments teams cannot solve and works a backlog of initiatives for transforming the organisation.

What is a MetaScrum?

A MetaScrum is a forum where Product Owners, leaders and stakeholders align vision and priorities across teams. At the top level it is called the Executive MetaScrum. It meets at least once per Sprint and is where priorities, budgets and team alignment should change.

What does the Chief Product Owner do in Scrum@Scale?

The Chief Product Owner sets the strategic vision for a group of teams, maintains one ordered backlog, decides which metrics to monitor, assesses customer feedback and facilitates the MetaScrum. They lead a Product Owner Team made up of each team's Product Owner.

How many teams can be in a Scrum of Scrums?

The guide recommends four or five teams per Scrum of Scrums, based on research suggesting an optimal team size of about five. When you have more teams, you form additional Scrums of Scrums and coordinate them through a Scrum of Scrum of Scrums.

How is Scrum@Scale different from SAFe?

Scrum@Scale is a minimal, modular extension of Scrum that leaves most practices to you. SAFe is a more detailed enterprise framework with many defined roles, events and portfolio structures. Organisations wanting a documented blueprint often prefer SAFe; those wanting a lighter approach often prefer Scrum@Scale.

How is Scrum@Scale different from LeSS and Nexus?

LeSS and Nexus both focus on several teams working on one product with a single Product Owner and Product Backlog. Scrum@Scale also uses a single ordered backlog, but adds Chief Product Owners, a Product Owner Team and explicit executive forums, so it reaches further into organisational leadership.

How long does it take to implement Scrum@Scale?

There is no fixed timescale. A reference model of four or five teams can usually be set up within a few Sprints, but extending Scrum@Scale across a large organisation is a gradual change that typically continues for many months or longer, depending on size and starting point.

Is there a Scrum@Scale certification?

Yes. Scrum Inc. and its registered trainers offer a Scrum@Scale Practitioner course, usually leading to the Registered Scrum@Scale Practitioner credential. Check the official Scrum@Scale or Scrum Inc. website for current formats and requirements.
