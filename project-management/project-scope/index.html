Project Scope: Definition, Statement, Examples and Steps

Project scope is the total work a project must do to deliver its agreed product, service or result, and nothing beyond that work. It sets the boundary everyone plans against: what will be delivered, what will not, and how you will know each deliverable is finished. This guide explains how to define scope, write a scope statement with a full worked example, and keep scope under control once work starts.

Key takeaways

  • Project scope is all the work needed to deliver the agreed result, and only that work.
  • Product scope describes what you build; project scope describes the work to build it.
  • A scope statement records objectives, deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions, constraints and assumptions.
  • Write exclusions as specific as the inclusions: they prevent most scope disputes.
  • The approved scope statement, WBS and WBS dictionary form the scope baseline; change it only through change control.
  • Agile projects usually fix time and cost and let detailed scope flex through a prioritised backlog.

What is project scope?

Project scope is the work that must be performed to deliver a product, service or result with the specified features and functions. It defines the boundary of the project: everything inside it is planned, estimated, resourced and tracked, and everything outside it is not the project's job unless a formal change brings it in.

That definition follows the one PMI uses in the PMBOK Guide, and the important words are "only that work". A good scope is complete (nothing required is missing) and closed (nothing unrequired is included). Missing work turns into late surprises; extra work turns into overruns and scope creep.

Scope is not the same as a task list. It is written in terms of outcomes and deliverables, such as "a booking website that lets patients book, change and cancel appointments", and only later broken down into the activities needed to produce them. That breakdown is the job of the work breakdown structure (WBS).

Scope is usually defined during planning, after the project charter authorises the project and sets its high-level boundaries, and before detailed scheduling and budgeting. On iterative projects it is defined at a high level up front and refined continuously as the team learns.

Product scope vs project scope

Two kinds of scope are easy to confuse, and both need to be clear.

Product scopeProject scope
What it describesThe features, functions and characteristics of the resultThe work required to deliver that result
Question it answersWhat are we making?What do we have to do to make it?
Typical contentRequirements, specifications, user storiesDeliverables, work packages, activities, management work
How completion is checkedAgainst the requirements and acceptance criteriaAgainst the project management plan and scope baseline
Website exampleSearch, online checkout, account pages, mobile-friendly layoutDesign, build, test, migrate content, train staff, launch, hand over

Project scope always includes work that never appears in the product: planning, procurement, testing, training, documentation, data migration, handover and project management itself. Forgetting this work is one of the most common reasons estimates come in low.

Product scope comes from your project requirements. Project scope comes from asking what it takes to deliver those requirements, which is why the two are defined together but checked differently.

Why project scope matters

A clear scope is the foundation for almost every other part of the plan.

  • Shared understanding. Sponsor, customer and team agree what "done" means before money is spent.
  • Reliable estimates. You cannot estimate time or cost for work you have not identified. Scope feeds cost estimation and scheduling.
  • Fewer disputes. Written inclusions, exclusions and acceptance criteria settle arguments about whether something was promised.
  • Better risk management. Assumptions and constraints recorded in the scope reveal risks early, which feeds risk management.
  • Controlled change. Change is only visible against a baseline. Without an agreed scope, every request looks equally valid.
  • Clear acceptance. Deliverables can be formally accepted when they meet criteria everyone signed up to.

In our experience the projects that struggle most are not the ones with a lot of change, but the ones where nobody wrote down what the starting point was.

Project scope and the triple constraint

Scope is one corner of the classic triple constraint, with time and cost; quality sits in the middle. If one changes, at least one of the others usually has to move. Our guide to project constraints covers the wider set, including risk, resources and benefits.

If scope…Time is likely to…Cost is likely to…Quality risk
Increases, with deadline fixedStay fixed but the team is stretchedRise (overtime, extra people)Higher: less time per feature
Increases, with budget fixedExtendStay fixedHigher if testing is squeezed
DecreasesShorten or create bufferFall or be reallocatedLower
Stays fixed but the deadline moves earlierCompressRiseHigher

This is why a scope change is never "just one more feature". The scope statement records which constraint is the least flexible, so that when change arrives you already know whether the answer is more time, more money or less of something else.

Tip

Ask the sponsor early: "If we cannot have everything, which matters most: the date, the budget or the full feature list?" Record the answer in the constraints section.

What a project scope statement includes

The project scope statement is the document that describes the project's scope, major deliverables, assumptions and constraints. It is the main output of defining scope and the reference point for later decisions. These are the sections we recommend; they match our free project scope template.

SectionWhat it containsWhat good looks like
Project objectivesWhat the project must achieve and whySpecific and measurable, e.g. "60% of bookings made online within six months of launch"
Product descriptionThe product, service or result being createdA short paragraph a newcomer could understand
DeliverablesEvery output handed over, including documents and trainingNouns, not activities; each one has an owner
In scopeWork and features includedSpecific items, grouped by deliverable
Out of scopeExplicit exclusionsThings a reasonable stakeholder might otherwise expect
Acceptance criteriaHow each deliverable will be judged completeTestable conditions and who signs off
ConstraintsFixed limits: budget, dates, regulations, resources, technologyStated as numbers or named rules
AssumptionsWhat you believe to be true but have not provenEach one testable, with an owner to confirm it
MilestonesKey dates and decision pointsLinked to deliverables, not to effort
ApprovalWho approved the scope, and whenNames, roles and dates

Some organisations add a risks section, a list of stakeholders or a summary budget. Keep it as short as the project allows: a two-week internal project may need one page, a regulated infrastructure project far more. The test is whether anyone reading it can decide if a given piece of work is in or out.

For more on writing objectives and deliverables, see project objectives and project deliverables.

In scope vs out of scope

The out-of-scope list is the most valuable part of the statement and the most often skipped. A useful exclusion names something a stakeholder could reasonably assume was included. "Out of scope: building a rocket" adds nothing; "Out of scope: migrating appointment history older than 24 months" prevents a real argument.

  • Go through each deliverable and ask: "What might someone assume comes with this?"
  • Look at adjacent systems, teams, locations and phases: integrations, other sites, ongoing support, later releases.
  • Write exclusions as specifically as inclusions, and say where the excluded work belongs if you know ("phase 2", "IT operations", "client responsibility").
  • Read the exclusions aloud to the sponsor. Surprise at this stage is cheap; surprise at acceptance is expensive.

Scope statement vs SOW, charter and scope management plan

Several documents talk about scope. They do different jobs.

DocumentPurposeLevel of detailTypical owner
Project charterAuthorises the project and names the project managerHigh level: purpose, objectives, summary scope, budget, milestonesSponsor
Project scope statementDefines exactly what the project will and will not deliverDetailed deliverables, exclusions, acceptance criteriaProject manager, approved by sponsor
Statement of work (SOW)Describes work a supplier will do under a contractContractual; includes terms, schedule and paymentBuyer and supplier
Scope management planExplains how scope will be defined, validated and controlledProcess, roles and approval routes, not the scope itselfProject manager

If you work with suppliers, read our guide to the statement of work. The scope management plan normally sits inside the project management plan.

How to define project scope: step by step

This is the process we recommend for predictive and hybrid projects. On a small project you can run steps 2 to 7 in a single workshop; on a large one each step may take weeks.

  1. Start from the charter and business case. Pull out the purpose, high-level objectives, budget, deadline and anything already declared out of bounds. Scope must serve these, not compete with them. Our project charter template shows what you should have.
  2. Identify the stakeholders who shape scope. List who will use, pay for, approve, maintain or be affected by the result. Missing a stakeholder now means a surprise requirement later. See project stakeholder management.
  3. Elicit and analyse requirements. Use interviews, workshops, observation, document review and prototypes. Capture each requirement once, give it an ID and a source, and prioritise it (for example must, should, could, won't). Resolve conflicts before they reach the scope statement.
  4. Write measurable objectives. Turn the charter's aims into objectives with a number and a date. These become the test of whether the scope is worth delivering.
  5. List the deliverables and their acceptance criteria. For each deliverable, write what it is, who accepts it and the conditions it must meet. Include project deliverables such as training, documentation and handover, not just the product.
  6. State what is out of scope. Use the questions in the previous section. Aim for at least as many exclusions as you have major deliverables.
  7. Record constraints and assumptions. Constraints are limits you must work within; assumptions are beliefs you have not yet proven. Give every assumption an owner and a date by which it will be confirmed.
  8. Review, refine and get sign-off. Walk the sponsor and key stakeholders through the draft section by section. Record their approval with names and dates. This approved version becomes part of the scope baseline.
  9. Decompose the scope into a WBS. Break each deliverable down into smaller deliverables and then work packages that can be estimated and assigned. Check the 100% rule: the WBS must contain all the scope and nothing outside it.
  10. Agree how changes will be handled. Before work starts, confirm who can request a change, who assesses it and who approves it. A simple change control process and a change request template are enough for most projects.
Tip

Write the first draft yourself, quickly, then review it with stakeholders. Workshops that start from a blank page tend to produce wish lists; workshops that start from a draft produce decisions.

Project scope example: a worked scope statement

The example below is illustrative. It describes a fictional group of four physiotherapy clinics that wants patients to book appointments online instead of by phone.

SectionContent
ProjectOnline booking system for a four-clinic physiotherapy group
Objectives60% of appointments booked online within six months of launch; reduce phone booking time at reception by 40% within the same period; launch before 31 March 2027
Product descriptionA web-based booking service, linked to the existing practice management system, that lets patients see availability and book, change or cancel appointments at any of the four clinics
Deliverables1. Booking web application. 2. Integration with the practice management system. 3. Email confirmations and reminders. 4. Staff user guide. 5. Training for 24 reception and clinical staff. 6. Go-live and four weeks of post-launch support
In scopeBooking, rescheduling and cancellation for existing appointment types; clinic and practitioner selection; email notifications; accessibility to WCAG 2.2 AA; testing on current versions of major browsers
Out of scopeNative mobile apps; online payment and deposits; SMS reminders; migration of appointment history older than 24 months; changes to the practice management system itself; marketing the new service
Acceptance criteriaAll in-scope user journeys pass user acceptance testing with no open critical or high defects; 50 test bookings sync correctly to the practice management system; accessibility audit passed; all 24 staff trained; operations manager signs acceptance
ConstraintsBudget £85,000 including contingency; go-live before 31 March 2027; must use the practice management supplier's published booking API; patient data handled under UK GDPR
AssumptionsThe supplier API supports all four clinics' appointment types (IT lead to confirm by 15 November 2026); clinics will provide practitioner availability rules by 30 November 2026; reception staff can attend two-hour training sessions in March
MilestonesScope sign-off 6 November 2026; design approved 11 December 2026; integration tested 12 February 2027; user acceptance complete 12 March 2027; go-live 29 March 2027
ApprovalSponsor: managing director. Accepting stakeholder: operations manager. Project manager: practice IT lead

Notice how each exclusion targets something a stakeholder could reasonably expect from a booking system: payment, text reminders, an app. Each assumption has an owner and a deadline, so it can be turned into a fact or a risk before it causes trouble.

From scope statement to WBS

The deliverables become level 2 of the work breakdown structure. Each is then decomposed until the pieces can be estimated and assigned. The first levels might look like this:

WBS codeElementExample work packages
1Online booking system project-
1.1Project managementPlanning, status reporting, risk and change log, closure
1.2Booking web applicationRequirements sign-off, UX design, front-end build, accessibility fixes
1.3Practice system integrationAPI configuration, availability rules, sync testing
1.4NotificationsEmail templates, reminder scheduling, delivery testing
1.5Training and documentationUser guide, training plan, four training sessions
1.6Go-live and supportCut-over plan, launch, four-week support rota, handover to operations

Each work package then gets a WBS dictionary entry describing its scope, owner, effort, cost and acceptance criteria. A work breakdown structure template gives you the codes, levels and roll-ups.

A change request against the scope

In January, the operations manager asks for SMS reminders because email reminders are often missed. SMS is explicitly out of scope, so the request goes through change control rather than straight to the developers. The project manager assesses it:

Impact areaAssessment
ScopeNew deliverable 1.4.4 SMS reminders; adds supplier SMS gateway setup and consent wording
ScheduleAbout two weeks of extra build and testing; go-live moves from 29 March to 12 April 2027 unless something else is deferred
CostEstimated £6,000 build plus a per-message running cost for operations
RiskConsent and data-protection review needed; new dependency on an SMS supplier
BenefitLikely fewer missed appointments; supports the online-booking objective

The sponsor is given three options: approve with the later date and extra budget, approve and defer the staff user guide's advanced sections to after launch, or log SMS for phase 2. Whichever is chosen, the scope statement, WBS, schedule and budget are updated together and a new baseline version is recorded. That is the difference between a controlled scope change and scope creep.

Scope management processes

Defining scope is one part of a wider set of scope management activities. The PMBOK Guide 6th edition, still widely used for training and exam preparation, describes six processes:

ProcessProcess groupMain output
Plan Scope ManagementPlanningScope management plan and requirements management plan
Collect RequirementsPlanningRequirements documentation and traceability matrix
Define ScopePlanningProject scope statement
Create WBSPlanningScope baseline (scope statement, WBS, WBS dictionary)
Validate ScopeMonitoring and controllingAccepted deliverables
Control ScopeMonitoring and controllingWork performance information and change requests

The PMBOK Guide 8th edition, published in November 2025, groups project work into seven performance domains, one of which is Scope. Commentaries on the 8th edition list its scope processes as Plan Scope Management, Elicit and Analyze Requirements, Define Scope, Develop Scope Structure, Validate Scope, and Monitor and Control Scope. The names have changed in places, but the underlying work is the same.

The scope baseline

The scope baseline is the approved version of the scope statement, the WBS and the WBS dictionary. It is one of the three baselines in the project management plan, alongside the schedule and cost baselines, and it can only be changed through formal change control. Performance is measured against it, and every approved change creates a new baseline version. Our guide to the project baseline covers how baselines are set and re-set.

Validate Scope vs Control Scope

Validate ScopeControl Scope
QuestionHas the customer formally accepted this deliverable?Is the scope still what we agreed, and are changes controlled?
WhenAs deliverables are completedThroughout the project
WhoCustomer or sponsor, with the project managerProject manager and team
OutputAccepted deliverablesChange requests and performance information
Related activityQuality control checks correctness first; validation confirms acceptanceChange control assesses and approves changes

Quality control usually happens before validation: the team checks a deliverable meets its specification, then the customer accepts it. Keep the two steps distinct so a deliverable is never sent for acceptance before it has been checked.

Project scope in Agile and hybrid projects

Agile approaches handle scope differently. Instead of fixing scope and estimating time and cost, they usually fix time (the iteration length and release date) and cost (the team) and allow detailed scope to vary within an agreed goal.

Predictive (waterfall)Agile
What is fixedScope, then time and cost are estimatedTime and cost; scope flexes
Where scope livesScope statement and WBSProduct goal and ordered product backlog
When detail is addedMostly up frontJust in time, through backlog refinement
Who decides prioritySponsor through change controlProduct owner, continuously
How completion is checkedAcceptance criteria and Validate ScopeAcceptance criteria per item and the Definition of Done
How change is handledChange requests against a baselineNew items added to the backlog and ordered

In Scrum, the scope of the product is held in the product backlog, ordered by the Product Owner toward a Product Goal. Each Sprint has a Sprint Goal that the team protects during the Sprint, and each increment must meet the Definition of Done. Individual requirements are often written as user stories with acceptance criteria.

That does not mean Agile projects have no scope. Most still need a high-level statement of the product vision, the main capabilities, the constraints and, importantly, what the product will not do. Contracts and budgets still need boundaries. Many organisations run hybrid projects: a fixed high-level scope and milestones for governance, with an Agile backlog underneath for delivery.

Common project scope mistakes

These are the mistakes we see most often, and the simplest fix for each.

MistakeWhat happensFix
Vague deliverables ("improve the website")Every stakeholder imagines something differentName the deliverable and its acceptance criteria
No out-of-scope listAssumed work is argued about at acceptanceList specific exclusions and read them to the sponsor
Only product work listedTraining, migration and handover are missed and estimates come in lowAdd project deliverables and a project management branch to the WBS
Unowned assumptionsAssumptions quietly turn out falseGive each an owner and a confirmation date; move failed ones to the risk register
Sign-off by the wrong personScope is reopened by someone with more authorityConfirm who can approve scope before you draft it
Informal changesScope creep: work grows, time and budget do notRoute every change through change control, however small
Gold platingThe team adds unrequested extras, using time and adding riskDeliver to the acceptance criteria; log ideas for later
Scope frozen too early on uncertain workThe project delivers what was asked, not what is neededUse iterations or a discovery phase for uncertain areas

Project scope checklist before sign-off

Use this list before you ask the sponsor to approve the scope statement.

  1. Every objective has a number and a date.
  2. Every deliverable is a noun, has an owner and has acceptance criteria.
  3. Project deliverables (training, documentation, migration, handover) are included, not just product features.
  4. There is a specific out-of-scope list covering adjacent systems, locations, phases and support.
  5. Constraints are stated as figures, dates or named rules.
  6. Each assumption has an owner and a confirmation date.
  7. Milestones are linked to deliverables.
  8. The people who will accept the deliverables have reviewed the acceptance criteria.
  9. The change control route is agreed.
  10. The approver's name, role and date are recorded.

Once approved, share it with the whole team and keep it where everyone can find it. A RACI matrix helps make clear who approves scope, who is consulted and who simply needs to be informed.

Templates and tools for managing project scope

You do not need special software to define scope. A well-structured document or spreadsheet is enough for most projects:

Frequently asked questions

What is project scope in simple words?

Project scope is everything the project has agreed to deliver and the work needed to deliver it, plus a clear statement of what it will not do. It is the boundary the plan, budget and schedule are built on.

What should a project scope include?

Objectives, a product description, deliverables, what is in scope, what is out of scope, acceptance criteria, constraints, assumptions, milestones and approval. Our project scope template covers all ten.

What is the difference between project scope and product scope?

Product scope is the features and functions of the result. Project scope is the work required to deliver that result, including management, testing, training and handover.

What is a project scope statement?

A document that describes the project's scope, deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions, constraints and assumptions. Once approved it becomes part of the scope baseline.

What are the steps of project scope management?

In the PMBOK Guide 6th edition: Plan Scope Management, Collect Requirements, Define Scope, Create WBS, Validate Scope and Control Scope. The 8th edition uses similar processes with some renamed, such as Elicit and Analyze Requirements and Develop Scope Structure.

Who is responsible for defining project scope?

The project manager leads the work of defining scope with the team and stakeholders. The sponsor or customer approves it.

What does out of scope mean?

Out of scope means work or features the project will not deliver. Listing them explicitly prevents stakeholders assuming they are included.

What is the scope baseline?

The approved scope statement, work breakdown structure and WBS dictionary. Project performance is measured against it and it changes only through formal change control.

What is the difference between Validate Scope and Control Scope?

Validate Scope is the formal acceptance of completed deliverables by the customer or sponsor. Control Scope is monitoring scope throughout the project and managing changes to the baseline.

What is the difference between a scope statement and a statement of work?

A scope statement defines the project's scope for the project team and sponsor. A statement of work describes work a supplier will perform, usually as part of a contract.

How is project scope managed in Agile?

Time and cost are usually fixed per iteration and scope flexes. The product backlog holds the scope, the Product Owner orders it, and each increment must meet the Definition of Done.

What is gold plating?

Gold plating is the team adding features or quality the customer did not ask for. It is a form of scope creep that uses time and budget and adds risk.

How detailed should a project scope be?

Detailed enough that anyone reading it can tell whether a given piece of work is in or out of scope. Small projects may need a page; complex or contracted projects need far more.

Can project scope change?

Yes. Scope changes are normal, but each one should be assessed for its impact on time, cost, quality and risk, approved by the right person and reflected in an updated baseline through change control.
