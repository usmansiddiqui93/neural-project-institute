Critical Success Factors in Project Management (+ Examples)

Critical success factors in project management are the small number of conditions that must be in place for a project to achieve its objectives, such as an engaged sponsor, clear goals or the right people on the team. Naming them early tells you where to focus your attention, your governance and your reporting. This guide explains what CSFs are, how they differ from KPIs and success criteria, gives examples for different project types and shows you how to identify and track them.

Key takeaways

  • A critical success factor (CSF) is a condition that must go right for the project to succeed; it is not a result or a task.
  • Objectives say what you want, success criteria say how you will judge it, CSFs say what must be true to get there and KPIs measure whether it is.
  • Keep the list short: three to six CSFs per project is typical.
  • Give every CSF an owner and at least one measurable indicator, and review them at each stage gate.
  • Research and practice consistently point to the same factors: clear goals, sponsor support, competent people, stakeholder involvement, sound planning and good communication.

What are critical success factors?

Critical success factors (CSFs) are the few essential conditions, capabilities or actions that must be in place for a project, programme or organisation to achieve its objectives. If a CSF is missing, the project is likely to fail however well everything else goes. CSFs answer the question: *what must go right for us to succeed?*

The idea is older than most project management standards. Management consultant D. Ronald Daniel described it in 1961, and MIT's John Rockart popularised it in a 1979 Harvard Business Review article about how executives decide what information they need. Rockart described CSFs as the limited number of areas in which results, if satisfactory, ensure successful performance. Project managers later applied the same thinking to individual projects.

Three features define a good CSF:

  • Few in number. If everything is critical, nothing is. Most projects have between three and six.
  • A condition, not an outcome. "Committed executive sponsorship" is a CSF; "deliver on budget" is a success criterion.
  • Measurable through indicators. You cannot measure "strong sponsorship" directly, but you can measure how often the sponsor attends steering meetings or how quickly decisions are made.

CSFs vs objectives, success criteria and KPIs

These four terms are often used interchangeably, which causes confusion in charters and status reports. They form a chain: the objective is the destination, the success criteria define arrival, the CSFs are the conditions you need on the way, and the KPIs tell you whether those conditions hold.

TermQuestion it answersExample (new customer portal)
ObjectiveWhat do we want to achieve?Launch a self-service customer portal by Q2
Success criteriaHow will we judge success at the end?Portal live by 30 June; 40% of support requests handled through it within six months
Critical success factorWhat must be true for us to get there?Customer service staff are involved in design and testing
Key performance indicatorHow do we know the CSF is holding?Number of service staff attending each sprint review; usability test pass rate

CSF vs KPI

The simplest way to remember the difference: a CSF is a condition, a KPI is a measurement. CSFs are usually qualitative and few; KPIs are quantitative and can be numerous. Each CSF should be linked to one or two KPIs so that you can see early when it starts to slip.

Success criteria and CSFs also differ in timing. Success criteria are judged mainly at the end of the project or after it; CSFs need attention from initiation onwards.

Why critical success factors matter

  • Focus. A short list of CSFs tells the team and the sponsor where attention matters most when time and money are limited.
  • Early warning. Because CSFs are conditions, you can see them weakening long before the schedule or budget shows a problem. A sponsor who stops attending meetings is an early signal.
  • Better governance. CSFs give steering committees something concrete to review beyond percentage complete. See our guide to project governance.
  • Shared understanding. Agreeing CSFs with stakeholders at the start surfaces assumptions and dependencies that would otherwise appear later as conflicts.
  • Lessons for the future. Comparing CSFs across projects shows which conditions your organisation repeatedly struggles to provide.

CSFs work closely with risk management. A risk is something that *might* happen; a CSF is something that *must* happen. If a CSF is uncertain, that uncertainty belongs in your risk register with an owner and a response.

Types of critical success factors

Rockart identified four sources of CSFs for organisations. They still help you think broadly before narrowing down:

TypeWhere it comes fromProject example
IndustryThe characteristics of your sectorRegulatory approval for a medical device project
EnvironmentalThe economy, technology, politics and societyAvailability of skilled contractors in a tight labour market
StrategicYour organisation's chosen strategy and positionThe project supports the organisation's top strategic priority
TemporalShort-term internal situationsRecruiting a replacement for a departing technical lead

For individual projects it is often more practical to group CSFs by where they sit:

  • People: project manager competence and authority, team skills and stability, stakeholder engagement.
  • Organisation: sponsorship, funding, alignment with strategy, decision-making speed.
  • Process: clear scope, realistic planning, change control, monitoring and communication.
  • External: suppliers, regulators, customers, market timing.

Common critical success factors for projects

The exact CSFs depend on your project, but the same themes appear again and again in practice and in research. Use this list as a starting point, not a checklist to copy.

Critical success factorWhat it looks like when it is in place
Clear, agreed objectivesEveryone can state what the project will deliver and why; objectives are specific and measurable
Committed sponsorshipAn executive who champions the project, makes decisions quickly and protects funding
Competent project manager with authorityThe project manager is formally appointed and empowered to make day-to-day decisions
Skilled, stable teamKey roles are filled with the right skills and people are not constantly pulled away
Stakeholder and user involvementUsers help shape requirements and test results; concerns are raised early
Well-defined scopeScope is documented and changes go through change control
Realistic plans and estimatesThe schedule and budget are based on evidence, with contingency for known risks
Effective communicationThe right people receive the right information at the right time
Proactive risk managementRisks are identified, owned and reviewed regularly
Adequate resources and fundingMoney, people and equipment are available when the plan needs them

What the research says

Project management research has studied success factors for decades, and the findings are remarkably consistent. Jeffrey Pinto and Dennis Slevin's widely cited work in the late 1980s identified ten factors critical to project implementation: project mission, top management support, project schedule and plans, client consultation, personnel, technical tasks, client acceptance, monitoring and feedback, communication, and trouble-shooting. Their 1988 paper in PMI's Project Management Journal also showed that the relative importance of these factors changes across the project life cycle.

A 2005 paper presented at a PMI Global Congress by Seweryn Spalek surveyed experienced project managers about 61 factors. The factors rated most influential were grouped as: a formally appointed, competent project manager with real authority; a clearly and measurably defined project goal; an experienced, competent team; and top management support.

The lesson for practitioners is that most CSFs are about people and organisation, not tools or techniques. Methods matter, but they rarely rescue a project with no clear goal and no committed sponsor.

Critical success factor examples by project type

Here are typical CSFs for different kinds of project. Each is written as a condition you can check.

Project typeExample critical success factors
Software product releaseProduct owner available to the team every day; automated testing in place; early user feedback each sprint
Construction projectPlanning permission granted on time; reliable subcontractors secured; site safety culture enforced
ERP or CRM implementationBusiness process owners committed to the redesign; clean data migrated; users trained before go-live
Marketing campaignClear target audience and message; creative approval within agreed turnaround; tracking set up before launch
EventVenue contracted early; sponsor commitments confirmed; registration system tested
Organisational change programmeVisible leadership support; change champions in each team; two-way communication with staff

Notice that none of these is a task such as "write the test plan". They are the conditions that make the tasks succeed. For guidance specific to your sector, see our industry project management guides.

How to identify critical success factors

Identify CSFs during initiation, ideally while you write the project charter, and refine them during planning.

  1. Start from the objectives. Review the business case, charter and objectives. You cannot find the conditions for success until you know what success means.
  2. Ask what must go right. With the sponsor and key stakeholders, ask for each objective: what must be true for us to achieve this? Capture every answer.
  3. Look at past projects. Review lessons learned from similar work. Which conditions were present on successful projects and missing on troubled ones?
  4. Check the four sources. Run through industry, environmental, strategic and temporal factors, plus people, organisation, process and external, so you do not miss a category.
  5. Filter ruthlessly. For each candidate ask: if this were missing, would the project very probably fail? Keep only the answers that are a clear yes. Aim for three to six.
  6. Assign an owner and indicators. Give every CSF a named owner and one or two KPIs with targets or warning thresholds.
  7. Agree and record them. Confirm the list with the sponsor and record it in the charter or project management plan.
Tip

Write each CSF as a short statement that can be true or false at any moment, such as "Finance team available two days a week for testing". Vague phrases like "good teamwork" are hard to track and easy to ignore.

How to measure and track CSFs

A CSF that nobody looks at after the kick-off has no value. Link each one to indicators and build them into your regular reporting.

Critical success factorKPIWarning thresholdOwner
Active executive sponsorshipSteering meeting attendance; days to resolve escalated decisionsTwo missed meetings in a row; decisions taking more than 5 working daysProject manager
Stable, skilled teamKey roles filled; unplanned departuresAny key role vacant for more than 2 weeksResource manager
User involvementUsers attending reviews and testsFewer than 3 users at a reviewBusiness lead
Controlled scopeApproved vs unapproved changesAny work started without an approved change requestProject manager
  • Report CSFs in your status report. Add a short CSF section with a red, amber or green rating and a comment. See our guide to the project status report.
  • Review at stage gates. Check every CSF before approving the next phase, and stop or replan if a critical condition cannot be met.
  • Use a dashboard. Project management software can display the related KPIs automatically. Tools such as Wrike offer custom dashboards and reports that work well for this; compare options in our guide to project management software.
  • Turn threats into risks. When a CSF looks shaky, log it as a risk with an owner and a response.

If your organisation already uses objectives and key results, an OKR template is a convenient place to keep CSF indicators alongside the goals they support. Agile teams can also connect CSFs to their delivery measures; see Agile metrics and KPIs.

CSFs across the project life cycle

Pinto and Slevin's research showed that different factors matter more at different stages. In practice, the emphasis shifts like this:

PhaseCSFs that usually need most attention
InitiationClear objectives, sponsor commitment, a sound business case and early stakeholder involvement
PlanningRealistic estimates, well-defined scope, the right team and secured funding
ExecutionCommunication, monitoring and feedback, user involvement, change control and trouble-shooting
ClosureClient acceptance, handover to operations and capturing lessons learned

Review your CSFs at the start of each project phase. A factor that was easy to satisfy in planning, such as team availability, can become critical in execution when other projects compete for the same people.

Worked example: CRM implementation

A mid-sized company plans to replace its spreadsheets with a CRM system for a 60-person sales team. The objective is for all sales activity to be managed in the CRM within six months, with forecast accuracy improving as a success criterion.

The project manager runs a workshop with the sponsor (the sales director), the IT lead and two sales managers. They list fourteen candidate factors and filter them to five CSFs:

CSFKPIOwner
Sales director visibly champions the CRMDirector uses CRM data in every weekly sales meetingSponsor
Sales managers commit time to design and testingHours attended vs planned in design workshopsSales managers
Customer data is clean before migrationDuplicate and incomplete records below 2%Data lead
Every user is trained before go-livePercentage of users completing trainingTraining lead
Scope is limited to core sales processes for release oneNumber of unapproved change requestsProject manager

The CSFs go into the charter and appear as a short red, amber and green table in each fortnightly status report. When the training KPI shows amber three weeks before go-live, the team adds extra sessions rather than discovering the gap after launch.

Common mistakes with critical success factors

  • Listing too many. Twenty "critical" factors dilute attention. Keep the list short and move the rest to the risk register or plan.
  • Confusing CSFs with tasks or deliverables. "Complete user training" is a task; "users are confident with the new system before go-live" is a condition.
  • Confusing CSFs with success criteria. "On time and on budget" describes the result, not what enables it.
  • No owner. A CSF without an owner becomes everyone's concern and nobody's job.
  • No measure. Without at least one indicator, you will only discover a missing CSF when it is too late.
  • Setting and forgetting. CSFs written at kick-off and never reviewed provide no early warning.
  • Ignoring organisational factors. It can be uncomfortable to name sponsorship or decision speed as a CSF, but these are often the most important ones.

Many of these issues also show up as scope creep and weak stakeholder management, so tackling CSFs properly tends to strengthen the whole project.

Frequently asked questions

What are critical success factors in project management?

They are the few essential conditions that must be in place for a project to achieve its objectives, such as clear goals, committed sponsorship and a capable team.

What is an example of a critical success factor?

"The sales director actively champions the new CRM" is a CSF for a CRM implementation. Other common examples are clear objectives, user involvement and a stable, skilled team.

What is the difference between a CSF and a KPI?

A CSF is a condition that must be true for success; a KPI is a metric that shows whether it is. For example, sponsorship is a CSF and sponsor attendance at steering meetings is a KPI.

What is the difference between success criteria and success factors?

Success criteria are the standards used to judge whether the project succeeded, such as delivering by a date. Success factors are the conditions that help you get there.

What are the four types of critical success factors?

John Rockart described four sources: industry, environmental, strategic and temporal factors.

How many critical success factors should a project have?

Usually three to six. If you have many more, some are probably risks, tasks or nice-to-haves rather than truly critical conditions.

Who identifies critical success factors?

The project manager leads the work with the sponsor and key stakeholders, often in a workshop during initiation.

When should critical success factors be defined?

During initiation, alongside the charter, and refined during planning. Review them at each stage gate.

Where are critical success factors documented?

In the project charter or project management plan, and summarised in regular status reports.

How do you measure critical success factors?

Link each CSF to one or two KPIs with warning thresholds and an owner, then report them regularly.

Can critical success factors change during a project?

Yes. Their relative importance changes through the life cycle, and a major change in scope or context can introduce new ones.

Why do projects fail when CSFs are missing?

Because CSFs are the conditions success depends on. Projects without clear goals, active sponsorship or user involvement struggle however good the plan is.

Are critical success factors the same as key success factors?

The terms are often used interchangeably. Key success factor is more common in strategy and industry analysis; critical success factor is the usual term in project management.
