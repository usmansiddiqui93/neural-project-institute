Project Governance: Framework, Structure and Roles

Project governance is the framework of roles, decision rights and controls that directs a project and holds it to account. It answers three questions: who can decide what, how those decisions are made, and how the organisation knows the project is still worth doing. This guide explains the structure, the key mechanisms and how to set up governance that is proportionate to your project.

Key takeaways

  • Project governance decides direction and holds the project to account; project management plans and delivers the work.
  • A typical structure has a sponsor, a steering committee or project board, a project manager, a PMO and some form of independent assurance.
  • Clear decision rights, tolerances and escalation routes let senior people manage by exception instead of approving everything.
  • Stage gates and a regularly re-checked business case stop projects drifting on after they stop being worthwhile.
  • Good governance is proportionate: a small internal project needs a one-page framework, a major programme needs much more.

What is project governance?

Project governance is the set of roles, responsibilities, decision rights, processes and controls that directs and oversees a project on behalf of the organisation that funds it. It defines who is accountable, who can approve what, how progress is reported and how the project is checked against its business case.

Governance sits above day-to-day delivery. Its job is to make sure the organisation is doing the right project, doing it in the right way, and stopping or changing course when the case for the project changes. Done well, it speeds decisions up, because everyone knows who decides and on what information.

Many practitioners describe governance with a simple three-pillar model. It is a useful way to check a framework is complete, though it is a teaching model rather than a formal standard:

  • Structure: the decision-making bodies and how they relate, such as the sponsor, steering committee, project board and PMO.
  • People: the named individuals in those roles, with the authority, time and skills to fill them.
  • Information: the reports, logs and reviews that give decision-makers an honest view of progress, risks and benefits.

Project governance vs project management

The two are closely linked but they are not the same. Governance directs and oversees; management plans and delivers. A project manager works within the authority that governance delegates.

Project governanceProject management
PurposeDirect, authorise and hold to accountPlan, organise and deliver
Key questionIs this still the right project, run the right way, and is it worth continuing?How do we deliver the agreed outcomes within the agreed limits?
WhoSponsor, steering committee or project board, PMO, assuranceProject manager, team leads and the project team
Typical decisionsApprove funding and stage progression, accept major risks, approve significant changes, stop or close the projectAssign work, manage day-to-day issues, approve changes within tolerance
Time horizonWhole life of the project and the benefits after itCurrent stage, sprint or work package

Project governance vs PMO

A project management office (PMO) supports governance but is not the governing body. It typically sets standards and templates, consolidates reporting and may carry out assurance reviews. Decisions such as approving funding or stopping a project normally sit with the sponsor, the board or a portfolio committee, not with the PMO itself.

Project governance vs corporate and portfolio governance

Corporate governance is how the whole organisation is directed and controlled by its board. Portfolio governance decides which projects and programmes to fund and how to balance them. Project governance applies those rules to a single project. The three should connect: a project's tolerances, reporting and stage gates should reflect the organisation's wider policies.

Project governance structure and roles

Titles vary between organisations and methods, but most governance structures contain the same roles. On a small project one person may hold several; on a large one each may be a group.

Project sponsor

The sponsor owns the business case and is ultimately accountable for the project delivering its benefits. They secure funding, chair or sit on the steering committee, make or escalate the big decisions, and champion the project with senior stakeholders. An absent sponsor is one of the most common causes of weak governance.

Steering committee or project board

A small group of senior people who give direction, approve each stage and resolve issues the project manager cannot. In PRINCE2 the project board has three roles: the executive (who represents the business and owns the business case), the senior user (who represents the people who will use the outputs) and the senior supplier (who represents those designing and building them). Keep it small; a board of 12 rarely decides anything quickly.

Project manager

Runs the project day to day within delegated authority: planning, coordinating the team, managing risks and issues, and reporting honestly upwards. When a forecast breaches tolerance, the project manager escalates with options rather than solving it alone.

PMO

Sets methods and templates, supports planning and reporting, maintains the portfolio view and often runs health checks. In some organisations a portfolio PMO also prepares information for investment committees.

Project assurance

Independent checks that the project is being run properly and that reports reflect reality. Assurance may come from the PMO, internal audit, an external reviewer or, in PRINCE2, from assurance roles appointed by the board. It should be independent of the project manager.

Change control board

On larger projects a change control board, or the steering committee itself, decides on changes above the project manager's authority.

Decision rights: who decides what

The most practical governance document is a short table of decision rights. It removes the question 'can I approve this?' and stops small decisions travelling to the board. The thresholds below are an illustration only; set your own to match your organisation's financial delegations and the project's risk.

DecisionProject managerSponsorSteering committee / board
Re-plan tasks within a stageDecidesInformed-
Change within tolerance (for example up to £10,000 or 1 week)Decides and logsInformed-
Change beyond PM tolerance but within stage toleranceRecommendsDecidesInformed

Record every significant decision in a decision log: what was decided, by whom, when, which options were considered and why. It protects the project manager, helps new team members understand history and feeds lessons learned at the end.

Project governance in PMBOK and PRINCE2

Both major standards treat governance as central, though they describe it differently.

  • PMBOK Guide, eighth edition: PMI published the eighth edition in November 2025. It organises the guide around seven performance domains, and governance is the first of them, alongside scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources and risk. One of its six principles is to lead accountably. See our PMBOK Guide overview.
  • PRINCE2 (7th edition): governance runs through its seven principles, several of which are governance rules: ensure continued business justification, define roles, responsibilities and relationships, manage by stages and manage by exception. The organising practice sets out the project board and assurance roles, and tolerances can be set for time, cost, scope, quality, benefits, risk and sustainability.
  • Agile frameworks: the Scrum Guide does not describe project governance, but it gives the product owner clear accountability for maximising value and makes progress visible every sprint, which governance bodies can use directly.

Our own view: take the vocabulary from whichever method your organisation uses, but judge your framework by whether decisions are fast, well informed and recorded.

How to set up project governance step by step

  1. Check what already exists. Ask the PMO or finance team for the organisation's delegation limits, stage-gate rules and reporting standards. Do not invent a parallel system.
  2. Confirm the sponsor. Agree one accountable sponsor with the authority and time to do the job.
  3. Size the governance. Decide how much control the project needs based on cost, risk, complexity and visibility (see the scaling table below).
  4. Form the decision-making body. Name the steering committee or project board members, write short terms of reference and set a meeting rhythm.
  5. Agree decision rights and tolerances. Fill in a delegation table and set tolerances for time, cost and scope at project and stage level.
  6. Define stage gates. Choose the decision points, usually at the end of each project phase, and what evidence each gate needs.
  7. Set up reporting and logs. Agree the status report format and frequency, the escalation path and where the RAID log, change log and decision log live.
  8. Plan assurance. Decide who will run independent health checks and when.
  9. Record and communicate it. Put the framework in the charter, share it with everyone involved and include it in the communication plan.
  10. Review it at each gate. Tighten or lighten governance as the risk profile changes.
Tip

Agree governance before the first major decision, not after the first crisis. It is much easier to set tolerances when nothing has gone wrong yet.

Tolerances, escalation and management by exception

Tolerances are the agreed amount a project or stage can deviate from plan before the next level of authority must be involved. Management by exception, a core PRINCE2 principle, means senior people do not review every decision; they set tolerances, receive regular reports and step in only when a forecast breaches those limits. This keeps the board focused on decisions only it can make.

When a forecast goes beyond tolerance, the project manager raises an exception report that explains the cause, the impact and the options, with a recommendation. The board then chooses an option, and the result is recorded in the decision log.

Worked example

A £2 million office relocation sets a cost tolerance of ±5% (£100,000). In month four the project manager forecasts an overrun of £140,000 because of extra electrical work. As this is outside tolerance, she sends an exception report to the board with three options: absorb the cost from contingency, reduce the scope of meeting-room technology, or delay the move by six weeks to retender the electrical work.

The board approves £90,000 from contingency and trims £50,000 of scope, which brings the forecast back within tolerance. The decision is logged, the plan is re-baselined and the tolerance for the remaining stages is confirmed. The whole exchange took a week, because everyone knew the threshold, the format and who decided.

Scaling governance to the size of the project

Too little governance lets projects drift; too much slows every decision. Match the weight of governance to the project's cost, risk and visibility.

Project typeTypical governance
Small internal projectNamed sponsor, one-page charter with decision rights, fortnightly status note, simple issue and risk log
Medium projectSponsor plus small steering group, stage gates at major phases, weekly status, RAID and change logs, PMO check at go-live
Large or high-risk projectFormal project board, full delegation table and tolerances, monthly board and exception reporting, independent assurance at each gate
Programme or portfolioProgramme board above project boards, benefits management, portfolio prioritisation and consolidated reporting through a PMO
Tip

A useful test: if a decision routinely takes longer to approve than the work it governs, your governance is too heavy for that level.

Governance in Agile projects

Agile teams still need governance, but it looks different. Instead of approving detailed plans up front, governance bodies review working product often and fund outcomes over time.

  • Fund products or value streams, not fixed scope. Agree a budget and a goal, then review value delivered at regular intervals.
  • Use the product owner's accountability. The product owner makes most scope decisions; governance sets the boundaries.
  • Make reviews the evidence. A sprint review or quarterly planning event shows real progress, which is better evidence than a written status report.
  • Keep gates light and frequent. Short, regular decision points replace long phase gates.
  • Set guardrails, not approvals. Agree security, architecture and spending rules the team can apply themselves.
  • Adapt the PMO. An agile PMO supports teams with metrics and coaching rather than document checks.

Hybrid projects often keep formal stage gates for funding and contracts while running delivery in sprints. That works well as long as the gate criteria use the same evidence the team already produces.

Signs of weak governance and common mistakes

Warning signWhat to do about it
Nobody can say who can approve a particular changePublish a delegation table and a RACI for key decisions
The sponsor is absent or delegates everythingAgree the sponsor's time commitment up front; escalate to the portfolio owner if it is not met
The project continues after the business case has failedRe-check the business case at every gate, and treat stopping as a valid outcome
Reports stay green until a sudden crisisDefine RAG ratings against tolerances and reward early bad news
The board discusses detail and avoids decisionsSend papers in advance with a clear decision requested and options
Small decisions take weeksRaise the project manager's tolerances and use management by exception
Decisions are made in corridors and forgottenKeep a decision log and confirm decisions in writing

Most of these problems are cultural as much as procedural. A framework only works if the sponsor and board take part, and if the project manager feels safe reporting problems early. Good stakeholder management helps with both.

Tools that support project governance

Governance mostly depends on people and clear rules, but software makes the information side easier. Look for portfolio dashboards, approval workflows, audit trails, permission controls and a single place for the RAID, change and decision logs.

Frequently asked questions

What is project governance?

Project governance is the framework of roles, decision rights, processes and controls that directs and oversees a project, so the organisation knows who decides what and whether the project is still worth doing.

What are the three pillars of project governance?

A widely used model describes three pillars: structure (the decision-making bodies), people (who fills each role) and information (the reports and reviews that inform decisions). It is a helpful checklist rather than a formal standard.

What is the difference between project governance and project management?

Governance sets direction, authorises funding and stages, and holds the project to account. Project management plans and delivers the work within the authority that governance delegates.

Who is responsible for project governance?

The sponsor is ultimately accountable, supported by a steering committee or project board. The PMO and assurance functions support governance, and the project manager works within it.

What is a project governance framework?

The documented rules for a specific project: roles, decision rights, tolerances, stage gates, escalation routes, reporting, logs and assurance. It is usually recorded in the project charter or initiation document.

What does a steering committee do in project governance?

It gives direction, approves stages and significant changes, resolves escalated issues and decides whether the project should continue. It should be small enough to make decisions quickly.

What is the difference between a PMO and project governance?

Governance is the decision-making framework. A PMO is a team that supports it by setting standards, consolidating reports and often running assurance, but it does not normally own the major decisions.

What is a stage gate?

A formal decision point at the end of a phase where the board reviews progress, risks and the business case before approving the next phase, changing direction or stopping.

What are tolerances in project governance?

Permitted deviations from plan, for example ±5% on cost or two weeks on a stage end date, within which the project manager can act without escalating. PRINCE2 also allows tolerances for scope, quality, benefits, risk and sustainability.

What is management by exception?

A principle, central to PRINCE2, where senior managers set tolerances and step in only when a forecast breaches them. It saves their time and speeds up routine decisions.

How does project governance work in Agile?

Agile governance funds outcomes over time, relies on the product owner for scope decisions, uses frequent reviews of working product as evidence and replaces long phase gates with lighter, more frequent decision points.

What is a decision log?

A record of significant decisions showing what was decided, by whom, when, which options were considered and why. It supports accountability, audits and lessons learned.

How much governance does a small project need?

Usually a named sponsor, a one-page charter with decision rights and tolerances, a short regular status update and a simple risk and issue log. Add more only if cost, risk or visibility justify it.

What happens to a project without governance?

Decisions stall or are made by the wrong people, risks go unescalated, scope grows without approval and projects can continue long after they have stopped being worthwhile.
