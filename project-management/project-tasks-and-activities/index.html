Project Tasks and Activities: Definitions and Examples

Project tasks and activities are the individual pieces of work you schedule, assign and track to produce a project's deliverables. An activity is a distinct, scheduled unit of work with a duration, resources and a clear output; "task" is often used to mean the same thing, or the smaller steps inside an activity. This guide explains the difference, shows how to define and size activities, and walks through a complete activity list.

Key takeaways

  • PMI defines an activity as a distinct, scheduled portion of work; it does not formally define "task". Most tools call every schedule line a task.
  • Activities come from work packages, the lowest level of the work breakdown structure. Work packages describe outputs; activities describe the work to produce them.
  • Name each activity with a verb and a noun, give it one owner and a completion criterion you can check.
  • Size activities so they can be estimated and tracked: a common rule of thumb is 8 to 80 hours of effort, and no longer than one reporting period.
  • Effort and duration are different. Convert one into the other using the owner's real availability.
  • Record attributes (ID, WBS code, predecessors, duration, resources, constraints) so the activity list can become a schedule.

What are project activities and tasks?

A project activity is a distinct, scheduled piece of work that is needed to produce a project deliverable. It has a clear start and finish, an estimated duration, the resources needed to do it and an output you can check. A project task is either another word for an activity or, where teams distinguish them, a smaller step within an activity.

The PMBOK Guide defines an activity as a distinct, scheduled portion of work performed during the course of a project. The word "task" is not a defined PMI term, but it is the word most software uses: Microsoft Project, Wrike, Asana and most other tools call each line in a plan a task. In practice you will meet three conventions.

Activity vs task: the short answer

ConventionHow the words are usedWhere you see it
InterchangeableActivity and task mean the same: a scheduled unit of workMost organisations and most scheduling tools
Activity contains tasksAn activity is scheduled and tracked; tasks are the steps inside it, often managed by the person doing the workLarger projects, PMI-style planning, many textbooks
Task contains activitiesA task is the scheduled item; activities are the things people do to complete itSome organisations and some software, less common

None of these is wrong. What matters is that your project uses one convention consistently. In this guide we use the second: activities are the scheduled units you estimate, link and report on, and tasks are the smaller steps the owner uses to get an activity done. If your team uses the words interchangeably, read "activity" and "task" as the same thing throughout.

Where activities and tasks fit in the work hierarchy

Activities sit between the scope of the project, described in the work breakdown structure, and the day-to-day work of the team.

LevelWhat it describesExampleTypically estimated in
ProjectThe whole temporary endeavourLaunch a new staff intranetMonths, total budget
DeliverableAn output the project must produceWorking search functionWeeks, cost
Work packageThe lowest level of the WBS, owned by one person or teamSearch index and results pageWeeks, cost and effort
ActivityScheduled work needed to produce the work packageConfigure search indexDays, hours of effort
TaskA smaller step inside an activitySet up synonym listHours
MilestoneA significant point or event, with zero durationSearch approved for launchA date, no effort

Work package vs activity

A work package is described by its output (a noun: "Training materials"). Activities are described by the work (a verb: "Write training slides"). The WBS stops at work packages; the activity list starts there. This separation keeps the WBS about scope and the activity list about time and effort.

Activity vs milestone

An activity consumes time and effort. A milestone marks a moment, such as an approval or a hand-over, and has no duration. Milestones are useful as checkpoints and for reporting, but they cannot be assigned work. If you find yourself writing "Milestone: build the database", it is an activity.

Types of activities and tasks

Not all activities behave the same way in a schedule. Knowing the type helps you estimate and track them correctly.

TypeWhat it isExampleHow progress is measured
Discrete effortWork that produces a specific, measurable outputWrite the user guideBy the output: drafted, reviewed, approved
Level of effort (LOE)Supporting work that has no output of its own and lasts as long as other workProject administration, site supervisionBy time passing, not by output
Apportioned effortWork that is a fixed proportion of another discrete activityQuality inspection at 10 per cent of build effortIn proportion to the activity it supports
MilestoneA significant event with zero durationDesign signed offAchieved or not
Summary taskA parent line that groups activities; its dates roll up from themPhase 2: BuildRolled up from its children
Hammock activityA summary that spans between two unrelated activitiesEquipment hire from delivery to removalCalculated from the activities it spans
Recurring taskA task that repeats on a scheduleWeekly status meetingEach occurrence done or not

The first three are the effort types used in the PMBOK Guide (6th edition) and in earned value management. Most activities on a typical project are discrete effort, and they are the ones worth planning in detail.

Activity attributes: what to record

Activity attributes are the details recorded about each activity. Early on there are only a few; more are added as planning progresses. The PMBOK Guide lists attributes such as these, and most scheduling tools have a field for each:

  • Unique ID and WBS code, so cost and progress roll up to the right work package.
  • Name (verb plus noun) and description of the work.
  • Completion criteria: how you will know it is finished.
  • Predecessors and successors, with the link type and any lead or lag; see predecessors and successors.
  • Effort and duration estimates, with the basis of estimate.
  • Resources and owner: the people, skills, equipment or materials needed, and the one person accountable.
  • Constraints and imposed dates, such as a supplier delivery date.
  • Assumptions, such as "subject expert available two days a week".
  • Location and activity type where relevant.

You do not need this level of detail for every activity on a small project. Record what you need to schedule and track the work, and add detail where the risk is highest.

How to define activities step by step

In PMBOK terms this is the Define Activities process, whose main outputs are the activity list, activity attributes and the milestone list. In plain terms, you are turning each piece of scope into a list of work you can schedule.

  1. Start from the WBS. Take each work package in turn. If you do not have a WBS yet, build one first; our work breakdown structure template gives you a starting format.
  2. Involve the people who will do the work. Ask them what steps are needed to produce the work package. They know the hidden work, such as set-up, reviews and rework.
  3. List the activities. Brainstorm first, then tidy. Include non-build work: reviews, approvals, procurement lead times, training and hand-over.
  4. Name each activity as verb plus noun, and write a completion criterion for it.
  5. Size each activity so it can be estimated and tracked (see the sizing rules below). Split activities that are too big; merge ones that are trivially small.
  6. Add milestones for key events: approvals, hand-overs, external deliveries, phase ends.
  7. Record the attributes you need now (at least ID, WBS code, name, owner) and leave space for those added during sequencing and estimating.
  8. Review the list with the work package owners. Check that completing every activity would produce the whole work package, and nothing outside it.
Tip

You do not have to define every activity at the start. Rolling wave planning, described in the PMBOK Guide, means planning near-term work in detail and later work at a higher level, then adding detail as you learn more. It is often the honest approach on longer projects.

Once the list exists, the next step is to link the activities in order.

How to name tasks and activities

A good name tells everyone what the work is and hints at when it is finished. Use a verb plus a noun, and be specific enough that two people would not interpret it differently.

Poor nameWhy it is weakBetter name
TestingNo verb; no scope; no end pointRun system tests on payment module
Ongoing supportNever finishesProvide go-live support (weeks 1 to 2)
Sarah's workNames a person, not the workDraft supplier contract
WebsiteThat is a deliverable, not an activityBuild product listing pages
Look at budgetUnclear outputApprove phase 2 budget
MeetingWhich one, and why?Hold design sign-off review

Pair each name with a completion criterion: a sentence describing the evidence that it is done, such as "report approved by the sponsor by email". If you cannot write one, the activity is probably too vague or too large.

How big should a task be?

Activities that are too big cannot be tracked: they show "in progress" for weeks and then either finish or suddenly do not. Activities that are too small create a plan nobody can maintain. These rules of thumb help you find the middle:

  • The 8/80 rule: an activity should take between 8 and 80 hours of effort. It is a planning heuristic, not a standard, so treat it as a guide.
  • The reporting-period rule: an activity should be no longer than the interval between progress reports, often one or two weeks, so each report shows real movement.
  • The one-owner rule: if an activity needs two people to be accountable, it is probably two activities.
  • The estimate rule: if the person doing it cannot estimate it with reasonable confidence, break it down further.
  • The usefulness rule: do not schedule work that is only minutes long. Keep that as tasks on the owner's own list.

Effort vs duration: a worked calculation

Effort is the number of person-hours of work. Duration is the number of working days from start to finish. They are equal only if someone works on the activity full time, which is rare. Convert effort to duration using the owner's real availability:

StepCalculationResult
Effort estimateDesigner estimates the slides and handouts36 hours
Working dayStandard day7.5 hours
AvailabilityDesigner is 60 per cent on this project7.5 x 0.6 = 4.5 hours a day
Duration36 / 4.58 working days

If you had scheduled the 36 hours as five days (36 / 7.5, rounded up), the plan would be wrong by three days before the work had even started.

Split or merge? Examples

Activity as draftedSizeDecision
Develop the new website600 hoursSplit by page type or feature into activities of 20 to 80 hours
Email the venue15 minutesMerge into "Book training venues"
Write and approve the policy30 hours writing plus 2 weeks waiting for the boardSplit: "Write policy" (activity) and "Board approves policy" (milestone or short activity with its own duration)
Install and configure 40 laptops60 hoursKeep as one activity if one technician owns it; use tasks for each batch

The policy example is worth noticing. Waiting time (approvals, supplier lead times, curing, shipping) needs its own line or a lag, otherwise it hides inside the writer's activity and makes them look late.

Worked example: from deliverable to activity list

A company is introducing a half-day customer service course for 200 staff. The deliverable is "Training programme ready to deliver". The WBS has three work packages: training materials, trainers, and logistics.

The programme is ready after 18 working days. Two paths are critical: A1 to A5, and A1 to A4 then B2, both of which pass through writing and reviewing the materials. Trainer selection has 10 days of float and the logistics chain has 11, so those can slip without moving the milestone. If you want the programme ready sooner, shortening A3 or A4 is the only thing that helps; speeding up venue booking would change nothing.

This is what a good activity list gives you: a realistic duration, a clear view of which work drives the date and where you have room. For the method behind the numbers, read our guide to the critical path method.

Step 4: break an activity into tasks

The instructional designer turns A3 into a personal task list. These are not scheduled separately; they help the owner manage the 36 hours:

  • Set up slide template and folder structure.
  • Write module 1 slides (greeting and listening).
  • Write module 2 slides (handling complaints).
  • Write module 3 slides (follow-up and escalation).
  • Write participant handout.
  • Self-check against the outline and send for review.

Project task examples by phase

Activities vary by project, but most projects contain similar work in each phase. Use this as a prompt list when you build your own.

PhaseExample activities and tasks
InitiationDraft project charter; Identify stakeholders; Hold kick-off meeting; Confirm sponsor and budget
PlanningBuild WBS; Define activities; Estimate effort; Develop schedule; Prepare risk register; Agree communication plan
ExecutionDesign solution; Build components; Procure equipment; Run tests; Train users; Migrate data
Monitoring and controlUpdate schedule weekly; Review issue log; Prepare status report; Assess change requests
ClosureObtain acceptance sign-off; Hand over to operations; Hold lessons learned review; Close contracts; Release team

Project management work itself, such as reporting and meetings, is usually planned as level-of-effort or recurring tasks rather than detailed activities. Include major reviews and decision meetings that other work depends on as activities or milestones.

From activity list to schedule

The activity list is the input to the rest of schedule planning. The usual sequence is:

  1. Sequence the activities by adding dependencies; see task dependencies.
  2. Estimate durations from effort and availability.
  3. Assign resources and check for over-allocation; see resource allocation.
  4. Develop the schedule, calculate the critical path and show it on a Gantt chart.
  5. Baseline the schedule once it is approved, so progress can be measured against it; see project baseline.

For the full process, see our guide to project scheduling.

Tasks in Agile projects

Agile teams use a different hierarchy, but the idea is similar. Large pieces of work are epics, which are split into user stories that deliver something of value. During sprint planning, the developers often break each story into tasks: the technical steps needed to complete it, such as "write API endpoint" or "update help text".

  • Stories are sized relative to each other (often in story points); tasks, if estimated at all, are usually in hours.
  • Tasks live in the sprint backlog and are owned by the developers, not assigned by a manager.
  • Tasks are created just in time for the sprint, which is rolling wave planning in a very short cycle.
  • Completion is judged against the definition of done, which plays the role of a completion criterion.

Managing tasks during the project

Once work starts, the activity list becomes a tracking tool. A few practices keep it honest:

  • Update weekly at least: record actual start and finish dates and the remaining duration, not just a percentage.
  • Be careful with percentage complete: "90 per cent done" can stay true for weeks. Many teams use simple conventions instead, such as 0/100 (no credit until finished) for short activities or 50/50 (half credit at start, half at finish).
  • Track against completion criteria: an activity is finished when its criterion is met, not when the owner stops working on it.
  • Re-plan, do not overwrite: if an activity is going to take longer, change its remaining duration and let the schedule show the effect, rather than editing dates by hand.
  • Keep ownership clear: a RACI matrix helps where several people contribute to an activity.

A spreadsheet works for small projects; our project tracker template has owner, status and date columns ready to use. For larger or multi-project work, a tool with dependencies, workload views and dashboards saves a lot of manual effort. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a project task and a project activity?

Many people use them interchangeably. Where a distinction is made, an activity is the scheduled unit of work you estimate, link and track, and tasks are smaller steps within it. PMI defines "activity" but not "task"; most software calls every schedule line a task.

What is an activity in project management?

A distinct, scheduled piece of work needed to produce a deliverable, with a start, a finish, a duration, resources and a checkable output.

What is an example of a project activity?

"Write slides and handouts" for a training course, "Run system tests on the payment module" or "Pour the ground-floor slab". Each has a clear output and can be estimated and assigned.

What is a project activity list?

A list of all the scheduled activities in the project with their IDs, names and scope descriptions. It is the starting point for sequencing, estimating and building the schedule.

What are activity attributes?

The details recorded for each activity, such as ID, WBS code, description, predecessors and successors, duration, resources, constraints, assumptions and owner. They grow as planning progresses.

How big should a project activity be?

Small enough to estimate and track reliably. Common rules of thumb are 8 to 80 hours of effort, and no longer than one reporting period.

What is the difference between a work package and an activity?

A work package is the lowest level of the WBS and describes an output. Activities are the work needed to produce it. See work package.

What is the difference between an activity and a milestone?

An activity takes time and effort. A milestone marks a significant event, such as an approval, and has zero duration.

How do you name project tasks?

Start with a verb and name the result, such as "Approve budget" or "Install servers", and pair the name with a completion criterion.

What is a summary task?

A parent task that groups subtasks; its dates, effort and cost roll up from them. A hammock activity is a related idea that spans between two unrelated activities.

What is a level of effort activity?

Supporting work with no output of its own that lasts as long as the work it supports, such as project administration. Progress is measured by time passing.

Should meetings be included as project tasks?

Include major reviews and decision meetings that other work depends on. Routine meetings are usually covered as recurring or level-of-effort tasks.

How do you break a project into tasks?

Build a work breakdown structure down to work packages, then ask the people doing the work what activities each package needs. Name, size and sequence them, then break activities into personal tasks where useful.

Are project tasks the same in Agile?

Agile teams break user stories into tasks during sprint planning. The tasks are owned by the developers and usually last hours rather than days.
