The 5 Phases of Project Management: Steps and Example

The five phases of project management are initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure. Together they form the project life cycle: you decide whether the project is worth doing, plan how to deliver it, do the work while tracking it against the plan, then close it formally. This guide explains what happens in each phase, the documents and decisions involved, and follows one example project from start to finish.

Key takeaways

  • The five phases are initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure.
  • Monitoring and controlling is not a separate stretch of time: it runs alongside execution and checks the work against the baseline.
  • Each phase ends with outputs and a decision, such as an approved charter, an approved baseline or formal acceptance.
  • Phase gates stop weak projects early and make sure the next phase starts with what it needs.
  • Agile projects repeat planning, delivery and review in short cycles, but still need a start-up and a close.
  • Scale the phases to the project: a two-week project needs a one-page version of each, not a stack of documents.

What are the phases of project management?

The phases of project management are the stages a project passes through from idea to completion. The most widely used model has five: initiation (decide and authorise), planning (define how), execution (do the work), monitoring and controlling (track and correct) and closure (finish formally). Together these phases are called the project life cycle.

Each phase has a purpose, a set of activities, outputs you can point to and a decision at the end.

PhaseKey questionMain activitiesKey outputsDecision at the end
1. InitiationShould we do this, and why?Define the need, test feasibility, write the business case, identify stakeholdersBusiness case, project charter, stakeholder registerAuthorise the project
2. PlanningHow will we deliver it?Define scope, break down the work, schedule, budget, plan risk, quality and communicationProject management plan and baselinesApprove the baseline
3. ExecutionAre we doing the work well?Lead the team, produce deliverables, manage suppliers and stakeholdersDeliverables, performance data, change requestsDeliverables ready for acceptance
4. Monitoring and controllingAre we on track, and what must change?Measure progress, manage changes, risks and issues, report statusStatus reports, approved changes, forecastsContinue, correct or escalate
5. ClosureIs it finished and what did we learn?Gain acceptance, hand over, close contracts, capture lessonsAcceptance sign-off, closure report, lessons learnedFormally close the project

The model describes a predictive project, where scope is fixed early and the work is planned in detail before it starts. It is the basis of Waterfall project management, but the same five concerns appear in every approach, including Agile.

Tip

Phases describe the order of the project. They do not mean you finish one completely before touching the next. Planning continues during execution as details emerge, and monitoring runs from the first day of delivery to the last.

Why break a project into phases?

Phases turn one large, uncertain undertaking into a series of smaller commitments:

  • Control points. A review at the end of each phase lets the sponsor confirm the project is still worth doing before more money is committed.
  • Funding in stages. A project that stops after initiation costs very little.
  • Earlier visibility of risk. Feasibility work and planning surface problems while they are still cheap to fix.
  • Clear roles and reporting. Everyone knows who decides what and when, and "the baseline goes to the board on 14 March" is easier to follow than a percentage complete.

Phase 1: Initiation

Initiation answers one question: should this project go ahead? It turns an idea, problem or opportunity into an authorised project with a clear objective, a sponsor and a project manager. It is the shortest phase on most projects, but decisions made here shape everything that follows.

Steps in the initiation phase

  1. Define the need. Write down the problem or opportunity in plain language and who it affects.
  2. Set objectives. Agree what success looks like, ideally in measurable terms linked to business goals.
  3. Test feasibility. For larger or riskier ideas, run a feasibility study covering technical, financial, legal, operational and schedule feasibility.
  4. Build the business case. Compare options, including doing nothing, with costs, benefits, risks and a recommendation.
  5. Identify stakeholders. List the people and groups affected, their interest and influence, and how you will engage them. See stakeholder management.
  6. Draft the charter. Summarise purpose, objectives, high-level scope, key milestones, budget estimate, main risks and the project manager's authority. A project charter template speeds this up.
  7. Get approval. The sponsor or project board signs the charter, which formally authorises the project and the use of resources.

Outputs and exit criteria

  • Approved business case (or a short justification for small projects).
  • Signed project charter, or a project initiation document in PRINCE2 environments.
  • Initial stakeholder register, named sponsor and project manager.
  • High-level assumptions, constraints and risks.

You are ready to leave initiation when the charter is approved, the objective is clear enough to plan against and funding for at least the planning phase is in place.

Phase 2: Planning

Planning answers how the project will be delivered: what exactly will be produced, by whom, in what order, at what cost and with what controls. The result is the project management plan and its baselines, which become the yardstick for the rest of the project.

Steps in the planning phase

  1. Collect requirements. Work with stakeholders to capture what the deliverables must do.
  2. Define scope. Write a scope statement that says what is in, what is out and the acceptance criteria.
  3. Break down the work. Create a work breakdown structure that splits deliverables into manageable work packages.
  4. Sequence and schedule. Turn work packages into activities, set dependencies and durations, and use the critical path method to find the activities that drive the end date.
  5. Estimate costs and set the budget. Estimate each work package and add contingency for known risks.
  6. Plan resources and roles. Confirm who is needed and when, and clarify responsibilities with a RACI matrix.
  7. Plan for risk. Identify risks, assess them and agree responses in a risk register. See project risk management.
  8. Plan quality, communication and procurement. Decide how quality will be checked, who receives which reports and what will be bought from suppliers.
  9. Set the baselines. Approve the scope, schedule and cost baselines.
  10. Hold a kickoff. Walk the team and key stakeholders through the plan so everyone starts from the same understanding.

The plan should be as detailed as the project needs and no more. A project plan template gives you the structure.

Phase 3: Execution

Execution is where the team does the work and produces the deliverables. It usually takes the most time and money, so most of the project manager's effort goes into coordinating people, removing obstacles and keeping stakeholders informed.

Steps in the execution phase

  1. Mobilise the team. Confirm availability, onboard new members and agree ways of working, tools and meetings.
  2. Assign and sequence work. Release work packages to owners in line with the schedule, using a board or tracker so everyone can see what is in progress.
  3. Lead and support. Hold short regular check-ins, resolve blockers and manage conflict.
  4. Manage suppliers. Place orders and contracts in line with the procurement plan.
  5. Assure quality. Apply the agreed reviews and tests as work is produced, not at the end.
  6. Engage stakeholders. Follow the communication plan and run demos or walk-throughs.
  7. Record performance data. Capture actual dates, hours, costs and completed deliverables for monitoring.
  8. Raise change requests. When someone asks for something outside the baseline, log it rather than absorbing it quietly. Unmanaged requests are the usual cause of scope creep.

Phase 4: Monitoring and controlling

Monitoring and controlling compares actual progress with the plan and acts on the differences. Although it is listed fourth, it starts as soon as execution starts and continues until closure. Think of execution and monitoring as two sides of the same period of work.

Steps in a monitoring and controlling cycle

  1. Collect actuals for dates, costs, effort and completed work at a fixed interval, often weekly.
  2. Compare with the baseline to find variances in scope, schedule and cost.
  3. Analyse causes and trends. Is the variance a one-off or a pattern? Will it affect a milestone or the end date?
  4. Review risks and issues. A RAID log keeps risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies together.
  5. Decide on action. Choose corrective or preventive actions, or raise a change request if the baseline itself must change.
  6. Process changes formally through change control: assess impact, get a decision and update the plan if approved.
  7. Report status. Share a concise project status report with RAG ratings, variances, decisions needed and next steps.

Common control measures

MeasureFormulaHow to read it
Schedule variance (SV)EV − PVNegative means behind schedule
Cost variance (CV)EV − ACNegative means over budget
Schedule performance index (SPI)EV ÷ PVBelow 1.0 means less work done than planned
Cost performance index (CPI)EV ÷ ACBelow 1.0 means each pound buys less than a pound of planned work
Milestone slippageForecast date − baseline datePositive values show late milestones

PV is planned value, EV is earned value and AC is actual cost. These measures come from earned value management; you do not need full EVM on every project, but the questions it asks are always useful.

Phase 5: Closure

Closure formally finishes the project or a phase of it. It confirms the deliverables are accepted, hands them over, releases resources and records what the organisation should learn. Skipping closure is common, and it leaves open contracts, unclear ownership and repeated mistakes.

Steps in the closure phase

  1. Confirm completion of every deliverable against the acceptance criteria.
  2. Obtain formal acceptance in writing from the sponsor or customer.
  3. Hand over deliverables, documentation, training and support arrangements to operations or the client.
  4. Close contracts and finances. Settle final invoices and close cost codes.
  5. Release the team with feedback and recognition.
  6. Run a lessons-learned review. Capture what worked and what to do differently. See lessons learned.
  7. Write the closure report covering results against objectives, budget and schedule, open items and benefits still to be realised.
  8. Archive final documents where future projects can find them.

Closure also applies to cancelled projects. Stopping a project that no longer makes sense is a sound decision, and closing it properly protects the organisation.

Worked example: an office relocation in five phases

To show how the phases fit together, here is a hypothetical example: a 60-person company moving to a new office when its lease ends. The figures are illustrative.

1. Initiation (weeks 1–3)

The current lease ends in nine months. The operations director prepares a short business case comparing three options: renew at a higher rent, move to a similar-sized office nearby, or move to a smaller office with hybrid working. It recommends the smaller office, because the fit-out cost pays back through lower rent within the lease term. The managing director, as sponsor, signs a one-page charter: move all staff into the new office by 30 June with no more than one working day of disruption, within a budget of £180,000. A project manager is appointed and stakeholders listed (staff, IT, facilities, landlord, removal firm, visiting clients).

2. Planning (weeks 4–8)

The WBS has six branches: fit-out, IT and telecoms, furniture, move logistics, communications, and exit from the old office. The critical path runs through the fit-out contract and IT cabling, because desks cannot go in until cabling is done. The budget is £165,000 of estimated costs plus £15,000 contingency. The risk register includes fit-out delay, data loss during the server move and staff resistance to hot-desking. A RACI matrix makes the IT manager accountable for the network cut-over. The sponsor approves the baseline at a gate review in week 8.

3. Execution (weeks 9–24)

The contractor starts on site, IT orders equipment and the team runs two staff workshops on the new seating plan. Weekly 30-minute check-ins cover progress, blockers and decisions. Halfway through, the sales team asks for video-conferencing kit in two meeting rooms. It was not in scope, so the project manager logs a change request.

4. Monitoring and controlling (weeks 9–26)

At the end of week 16, planned value is £90,000. The work completed is worth £81,000 at budgeted rates (earned value) and £88,000 has been spent (actual cost).

MeasureCalculationResultMeaning
Schedule variance£81,000 − £90,000−£9,000Behind schedule
Cost variance£81,000 − £88,000−£7,000Over budget for the work done
SPI81,000 ÷ 90,0000.9090% of the planned work done
CPI81,000 ÷ 88,0000.9292p of planned work per £1 spent

The cause is a two-week cabling delay after a late landlord approval. Because cabling is on the critical path, the move date is at risk. The project manager agrees with the contractor to run cabling and decorating in parallel in different zones, recovering ten days. The sponsor approves the video-conferencing change at £6,500 from contingency, leaving £8,500 for remaining risks. Two weeks later the status returns from amber to green.

5. Closure (weeks 25–28)

Staff move over a weekend and start in the new office on Monday 29 June, a day early. The sponsor signs acceptance once the snagging list is cleared. Warranties pass to facilities, the old office is returned to the landlord and the final cost is £176,400. Lessons learned: secure landlord approvals before signing the fit-out contract, and add meeting-room technology to the standard requirements list. The closure report notes that finance will track the rent savings over the next year.

Tip

Notice how the phases overlap: planning detail kept emerging during execution, and monitoring started in week 9, not after the work was done. That is normal.

Phase gates: how to move from one phase to the next

A phase gate (also called a stage gate or decision point) is a formal checkpoint at the end of a phase where a decision-maker reviews the results and decides whether the project may continue. Gates stop projects drifting into the next phase without the inputs they need.

How to run a phase gate review

  1. Agree the gate criteria in advance, ideally in the charter or plan, so everyone knows what "ready" means.
  2. Prepare a short gate pack: phase outputs, status against objectives, updated business case, top risks and the plan for the next phase.
  3. Circulate it to decision-makers a few days before the meeting.
  4. Hold the review, focusing on risks, open questions and whether the business case still holds.
  5. Record the decision: go, go with conditions, hold and rework, or stop.
  6. Release funding and resources for the next phase if approved.
Tip

A gate that has never said "stop" or "hold" is probably just a meeting. Its value comes from being willing to pause or end a project when the evidence says so.

Phases vs process groups

Project phases and process groups are often confused because they share the same five names:

  • A phase is a period of the project with its own deliverables, such as design or construction. Phases are specific to the type of project and are usually sequential.
  • A process group is a family of management activities (initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, closing) that can happen within every phase. A design phase has its own start-up, planning, execution, control and close.

The five-phase model in this guide treats the process groups as phases. That works well for small and medium projects. On a large project you usually define industry-specific phases (for example concept, design, build, commission) and apply the five process groups inside each.

What the main standards say

StandardHow it describes the life cycle
PMBOK Guide (PMI)Up to the sixth edition, PMI organised the work into five process groups and ten knowledge areas. The seventh edition (2021) replaced them with principles and performance domains. Types of project life cycle

The phases can be arranged in different ways depending on how certain the requirements are. Choosing the life cycle is one of the most important tailoring decisions you make.

Life cycleHow the phases runBest suited to
Predictive (Waterfall)Each phase happens once, in order; scope is fixed earlyStable requirements, regulated or contract-heavy work, construction
IterativeThe product is refined through repeated cycles of planning, building and feedbackRequirements that become clearer as people see early versions
IncrementalThe product is delivered in usable pieces, each passing through the phasesProjects where early partial value is worth having
Adaptive (Agile)Short iterations combine iterative and incremental delivery with continuous planningHigh uncertainty and frequent change, such as software
HybridPredictive for some parts, adaptive for othersMixed projects, such as a hardware rollout with Agile software development

Our advice: choose the life cycle project by project, based on how stable the requirements are, rather than applying one approach to everything.

Project phases in Agile and hybrid projects

In Agile projects the five concerns still exist, but planning, execution, monitoring and a small close happen in every iteration rather than once. A short start-up and a final close usually wrap around the iterations.

Classic phaseAgile or Scrum equivalent
InitiationProduct vision, initial product backlog, release goal, team formation
PlanningRelease planning, then sprint planning every sprint; backlog refinement throughout
ExecutionThe sprint: building and testing increments that meet the Definition of Done
Monitoring and controllingDaily Scrum, burndown and velocity, sprint review with stakeholders
ClosureSprint retrospective each sprint; final release, handover and retrospective at the end

Hybrid projects often keep a predictive initiation and closure with phase gates for governance, and run execution in sprints. See the Scrum process and phases.

How to scale the phases for small projects

The five phases apply to a two-week website update as much as to a two-year programme; only the weight changes. For a small project, initiation can be a one-paragraph objective and a yes from whoever pays; planning a task list with owners, dates and main risks; execution a simple board and a weekly check-in; monitoring a weekly comparison with the plan; and closure an acceptance email and a 15-minute lessons conversation.

The test is proportionality: each phase should cost much less than the problems it prevents.

Frequently asked questions

What are the 5 phases of project management?

Initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure. Initiation decides whether to do the project, planning defines how, execution produces the deliverables, monitoring and controlling tracks progress and manages change, and closure formally finishes the project.

What are the 4 phases of project management?

Some models merge monitoring and controlling into execution, giving initiation, planning, execution and closure. The activities are the same; tracking is treated as part of doing the work.

Which phase of project management is the most important?

Initiation and planning have the most influence on success because changes are cheap early and expensive later. Monitoring and controlling is what keeps a well-planned project on track once work starts.

Are the project management phases sequential?

Initiation and closure normally happen once. Planning, execution and monitoring overlap, and in Agile projects they repeat in every iteration.

What is the difference between project phases and process groups?

A phase is a period of the project with its own deliverables, such as design or build. Process groups are types of management activity that can happen within every phase. The five-phase model treats the process groups as phases for simplicity.

What is the project life cycle?

The series of phases a project goes through from start to finish. The common model is initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closure, but industries often define their own phases.

What happens in the initiation phase?

You define the need, set objectives, test feasibility, build a business case, identify stakeholders and get a project charter approved.

What documents are produced in the planning phase?

The project management plan, typically with a scope statement, WBS, schedule, budget, resource plan, risk register, communication plan, change control process and approved baselines.

What is a phase gate in project management?

A formal review at the end of a phase where a sponsor or board decides whether the project continues, continues with conditions, reworks the phase or stops.

How long should each project phase take?

There is no fixed rule. Execution usually takes the most time and budget. Initiation and planning should be long enough to make sound decisions: hours for a small project, months for a major programme.

Do Agile projects have phases?

Yes, in a different shape: a short start-up, repeated iterations that each include planning, delivery and review, then a final release and close.

What is the difference between the project life cycle and the product life cycle?

The project life cycle ends when the deliverables are accepted and handed over. The product life cycle covers the product from concept to retirement and can contain several projects.
