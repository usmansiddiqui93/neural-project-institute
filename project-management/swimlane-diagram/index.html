Swimlane Diagram: What It Is, Examples and How to Make One

A swimlane diagram is a flowchart divided into lanes, one for each person, team or system involved in a process, so every step sits in the lane of whoever performs it. You can see at a glance who does what, in what order, and where work is handed from one party to another. This guide covers the symbols, a step-by-step method, two worked examples and how to use the finished diagram to make a process faster.

Key takeaways

  • A swimlane diagram is a flowchart with lanes that show who is responsible for each step.
  • Every arrow that crosses a lane boundary is a handoff, and handoffs are where most waiting and miscommunication happen.
  • Map the process as it really works today, with the people who do it, before designing an improved version.
  • Adding work time and waiting time to each step turns the diagram into a measurement tool, not just a picture.
  • Keep to roughly three to seven lanes and one consistent level of detail so the diagram stays readable.
  • Use a swimlane diagram for the flow of work, a RACI matrix for detailed accountability and a Gantt chart for timing.

What is a swimlane diagram?

A swimlane diagram is a process flowchart arranged in parallel lanes, where each lane represents one participant: a person, role, team, department or system. Each step is drawn in the lane of the participant who performs it, and arrows show the sequence. The result shows both what happens in a process and who is responsible for each step.

The name comes from the lanes of a swimming pool: each participant stays in their own lane, and you can see when work crosses from one lane to the next. You will also see swimlane diagrams called cross-functional flowcharts, deployment flowcharts, Rummler-Brache diagrams or simply swimlane process maps. They all describe the same idea.

Process charts with separate columns for different departments existed long before modern diagramming software. The format became widely known through Geary Rummler and Alan Brache's 1990 book *Improving Performance: How to Manage the White Space on the Organization Chart*, which argued that the biggest problems in organisations often sit in the gaps between departments. That is still the main reason to draw one today: a swimlane diagram makes those gaps visible.

An ordinary flowchart answers "what are the steps?". A swimlane diagram also answers "who does each step?" and "where does work change hands?", which matters because delays usually come from work waiting for the next person, not from slow work.

Parts of a swimlane diagram

Most swimlane diagrams use a small set of standard flowchart symbols. Keeping to these makes your diagram readable by anyone, whatever tool they use.

ElementUsual shapeWhat it means
LaneHorizontal or vertical band with a labelThe person, role, team or system responsible for the steps inside it
Phase (optional)Column or row crossing all lanesA stage of the process, such as Request, Assess, Decide, Deliver
Start or endRounded rectangle or ovalThe trigger that starts the process and the outcome that ends it
ActivityRectangleA single step, named with a verb and a noun, such as 'Approve invoice'
DecisionDiamondA question with two or more exits, usually labelled Yes and No
DocumentRectangle with a wavy baseA form, report or record that is produced or used
Data or systemCylinder or parallelogramA database, file or system that stores or supplies information
Flow arrowSolid arrowThe order in which steps happen
HandoffAny arrow crossing a lane boundaryWork or information passing to another participant

Keep activity names short. If a box needs a paragraph to explain it, it is probably several steps, or the detail belongs in a supporting procedure.

Pools and lanes in BPMN

Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN), the process modelling standard maintained by the Object Management Group, uses the same idea with stricter rules. A pool represents a whole participant, usually an organisation such as your company, a customer or a supplier. Lanes subdivide a pool into roles or departments. Sequence flows (solid arrows) stay inside a pool, while communication between pools is shown with message flows (dashed arrows).

For most project processes a plain swimlane diagram is easier for stakeholders to read. Use BPMN when the process will be automated or your organisation already uses it as a standard.

Swimlane diagram vs flowchart and other process maps

Swimlane diagrams are one of several ways to describe a process. Each answers a different question, and they work well together.

TechniqueWhat it showsBest for
Basic flowchartSteps, decisions and orderSimple processes with one owner, or explaining logic
Swimlane diagramSteps, order and who does each stepProcesses that cross teams, with handoffs and approvals
BPMN diagramSteps, events, messages and participants in a formal notationProcesses that will be automated or formally documented
Value stream mapFlow of value with lead time, process time and waitingFinding waste across an end-to-end flow
SIPOCSuppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers at a high levelAgreeing the boundaries of a process before mapping it
RACI matrixWho is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and InformedDetailed accountability, not sequence
Project network diagramDependencies between project activitiesScheduling and critical path analysis

A useful rule of thumb: if the question is "who does what, and in what order?", draw a swimlane diagram. If it is "who is accountable and who must be consulted?", build a RACI matrix. If it is "when will it be finished?", you need a schedule such as a Gantt chart.

When to use a swimlane diagram (and when not to)

Swimlane diagrams earn their place whenever responsibility for a process is spread across more than one person or team. Typical uses include:

  • Approval processes, such as change requests, purchase approvals or document sign-off.
  • Onboarding and offboarding, where HR, IT, facilities and line managers all have a part to play.
  • Service and support processes, such as incident handling or customer complaints.
  • Project handovers, for example from delivery to operations or from sales to the project team.
  • Designing a new workflow for a project deliverable before the team starts using it.
  • Training and audits, where people need a clear picture of their role and controls.
  • Process improvement, to find delays, duplicate work and unnecessary handoffs.

They are less helpful in some situations:

  • One person does every step. A checklist or simple flowchart is enough.
  • You need dates and durations across a whole project. Use a schedule, not a process map.
  • The process is mostly unstructured knowledge work, such as creative exploration, where the order of steps changes every time.
  • You need to show degrees of responsibility, such as who must be consulted. Pair the diagram with a RACI matrix.

Horizontal vs vertical swimlanes

Lanes can run across the page (horizontal) or down it (vertical). Neither is more correct; pick the layout that fits your process and your medium.

LayoutHow it readsWorks best when
Horizontal lanesLeft to right, like a timelineThere are many steps and a moderate number of participants; slides and wide screens
Vertical lanesTop to bottom, like a page of textThere are many participants and fewer steps; printed A4 documents and procedure manuals

You can also choose what the lanes represent. Be consistent within one diagram.

Lane typeExample lanesUse when
RolesRequester, Project manager, Technical leadSeveral people in one team share a process
DepartmentsSales, Finance, OperationsYou are looking at cross-department flow
SystemsCRM, Finance system, EmailAutomated steps matter as much as human ones
External partiesCustomer, Supplier, RegulatorThe process crosses organisational boundaries
Phases (as columns)Request, Assess, Decide, DeliverYou want to show stages as well as owners

How to make a swimlane diagram: step by step

The method below works whether you draw on a whiteboard, in a spreadsheet or in a diagramming tool. The most important work happens before you draw anything.

  1. Choose one process and set its boundaries. Write down the trigger that starts it and the outcome that ends it, for example 'starts when a change request is raised; ends when the requester is told the decision'.
  2. Decide what the lanes will represent. Roles are usually the most useful for project processes. Systems get a lane only if they perform steps on their own.
  3. List the participants. Aim for three to seven lanes. If you have more, combine minor participants or split the process into two diagrams.
  4. Get the people who do the work in the room. Managers often describe how a process should work; the people doing it know how it actually works.
  5. Capture the steps on sticky notes or a list. Use verb-and-noun names and one level of detail. Ask 'what happens next?' and 'what happens if not?' at every step.
  6. Place each step in the right lane, in sequence. Steps that happen at the same time sit side by side in their own lanes.
  7. Add decisions and exception paths. Show the main path first, then the important alternatives, such as rejection, rework or escalation.
  8. Connect the steps with arrows and mark the handoffs. Every arrow that crosses a lane line is a handoff; highlight or count them.
  9. Add time data. For each step, note the typical work time and the typical wait before the next step starts.
  10. Validate, then version it. Walk through the diagram with each lane owner, fix gaps, date it and store it where the team can find it.

Setting the scope

Most swimlane diagrams fail because the scope is too big. 'Project delivery' is not one process; 'raising and approving a change request' is. If you cannot describe the start and end in one sentence each, narrow the scope. A high-level SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers) can help you agree the boundaries with stakeholders first.

Interviewing the people who do the work

Run a short workshop with one representative from each lane, or one-to-one interviews if people are spread out. Ask them to describe the last real case they handled, not the ideal one: what triggered their step, what information they needed, how long it waited before they picked it up, and what usually goes wrong.

When drawing, keep the main flow moving in one direction and reorder lanes so the busiest handoffs sit next to each other, which avoids crossing arrows. If you prefer to work in a spreadsheet, our workflow template records each step with its owner, inputs, outputs, tools, handoffs and time, which gives you the data behind the diagram.

Worked example: a project change request process

This illustrative example shows how a swimlane diagram turns into a measurement and improvement tool. A project team wants to know why change requests take so long to approve. They map the current (as-is) process with five lanes: Requester, Project manager, Technical lead, Change board and Delivery team. Times assume a 7.5-hour working day.

The as-is process

#LaneStepWork timeWait before next step
1RequesterEmail change request to project manager0.5 h2 days (sits in inbox)
2Project managerLog request in change log and check it is complete0.5 hNone
3Project managerAsk technical lead for an impact estimate0.25 h3 days (lead busy with sprint work)
4Technical leadEstimate effort and technical impact3 h1 day
5Project managerAssess impact on scope, schedule, cost and risk; prepare board paper2 h5 days on average (fortnightly board)
6Change boardDecision: approve the change?0.75 h1 day
7Project managerYes: update plan and baseline. No: inform requester1.5 hNone (end)

Drawn as a swimlane diagram, the flow zig-zags between lanes: Requester to Project manager, Project manager to Technical lead, back to the Project manager, up to the Change board, back to the Project manager and finally out to the requester or delivery team. That is six handoffs for a seven-step process.

Measuring the as-is process

  • Total work time: 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 3 + 2 + 0.75 + 1.5 = 8.5 hours (about 1.1 days).
  • Total waiting time: 2 + 3 + 1 + 5 + 1 = 12 working days (90 hours).
  • Elapsed time: 90 + 8.5 = 98.5 hours, or about 13 working days from request to decision.
  • Flow efficiency: work time ÷ elapsed time = 8.5 ÷ 98.5 = about 9%.

In other words, a change request spends around 91% of its life waiting. The diagram makes it obvious where: the inbox, the technical lead's queue and the fortnightly board. None of those delays would show up in a task list, because no one is doing anything during them. See flow efficiency for more on this measure.

The to-be redesign

The team agrees four changes, each aimed at a specific wait or handoff shown on the diagram:

  1. Replace email with a change request form whose required fields feed the change log directly, removing the separate logging step.
  2. Agree a two-day turnaround for impact estimates and reserve capacity for them in each sprint.
  3. Delegate minor changes to the project manager: under three days of effort, no movement of a milestone and no budget increase.
  4. Move the change board from fortnightly meetings to a weekly slot, with urgent items decided asynchronously.

The redesigned diagram has two paths after the impact assessment: minor changes go straight to the delivery team, and major changes still go to the board. Assuming half a day for triage, the two-day estimate turnaround, half a day before the assessment, an average wait of 2.5 days for the weekly board and half a day after it, the numbers compare as follows.

MeasureAs-isTo-be (minor change)To-be (major change)
Steps756
Handoffs646
Work time8.5 h7.5 h8.25 h
Waiting time12 days3 days6 days
Elapsed timeabout 13 daysabout 4 daysabout 7 days
Flow efficiencyabout 9%about 25%about 15%

Notice that work time barely changes. The improvement comes almost entirely from removing waiting and one handoff, which is typical of process improvement work. The new design also needs to be written into the project's change control procedure and communicated to everyone in each lane.

Tip

Mark each handoff with a number on the diagram, then ask the receiving lane: how do you find out that work is waiting for you? If the answer is 'someone chases me', you have found an improvement target.

Second example: new starter IT access with parallel lanes

The first example was largely sequential. Many processes have steps that happen in parallel, and a swimlane diagram shows that well. In this illustrative example, a new employee must have a laptop, accounts and access to a finance system on their first day.

LaneStepsTypical duration
Hiring manager1. Confirm start date and role. 6. Welcome new starter on day one0.5 day
HR2. Create employee record and trigger IT request0.5 day
IT service desk3a. Order and configure laptop5 working days (supplier delivery)
Identity system3b. Create email and core accounts automatically from the HR recordSame day
Finance system owner4. Approve finance system access after 3b3 working days
IT service desk5. Hand over laptop and credentials0.5 day

Steps 3a and 3b start at the same time in different lanes, so they are drawn side by side. The laptop branch takes 5 days; the accounts-plus-approval branch takes about 3.5 days. The longer branch sets the duration, exactly as the critical path does in a project schedule. The diagram therefore gives HR a simple rule: trigger the request at least six working days before the start date, or the new starter arrives without a laptop.

It also shows why a system can deserve its own lane. Step 3b involves no person, but if the HR record is incomplete it fails silently. Giving the system a lane prompts the question: who is told when this step fails?

How to analyse a swimlane diagram for improvements

Once the as-is diagram is validated, look for the patterns below. Each one points to a likely improvement.

What you see on the diagramWhat it usually meansPossible fix
Many arrows crossing lanesWork bounces between people; each handoff adds a waitCombine steps in one lane; give one person end-to-end ownership
Long waits before a stepA queue, meeting cycle or overloaded personService-level targets, more frequent decisions, reserved capacity
Loops back to earlier stepsRework caused by missing information or unclear criteriaBetter intake forms, checklists, clear acceptance criteria
Several approvals in a rowApproval stacking; nobody trusts the previous checkDelegate approval thresholds; approve once at the right level
One lane with far more stepsA bottleneck or single point of failureRedistribute work, cross-train, automate routine steps
Steps in no clear laneOwnership gaps; things fall through the cracksAssign an owner and record it in a RACI matrix
Same information entered twiceDuplicate data entry between systemsIntegrate systems or use one shared record

Ask three questions of every step: does it add value for the customer of the process, does it need to be done by this lane, and does it need to happen in this order? Steps that fail all three are candidates for removal.

If you need to find the root cause of a recurring problem, such as frequent rework at one step, a fishbone diagram is a good next tool. For broader waste reduction, swimlane analysis fits naturally with Lean project management and Six Sigma methods.

Swimlanes in project management

In projects, swimlane diagrams usually appear in four places:

  • Project processes. Change control, risk escalation, procurement approvals and reporting cycles can all be documented as swimlanes in the project management plan, so everyone knows the route a request will take.
  • Roles and responsibilities. A swimlane diagram shows sequence; a RACI matrix shows accountability. Draw the swimlane first, then use each step as a row in your RACI matrix template.
  • Designing deliverables. When a project creates a new service or system, the future-state swimlane diagram is often a deliverable in its own right, agreed with stakeholders before the build starts.
  • Operational handover. At project closure, swimlane diagrams help the operations team understand the processes they are inheriting.

The word 'swimlane' also appears in two other planning tools. On a Kanban board, swimlanes are horizontal rows that separate types of work, teams or priority levels on the same board; see Kanban swimlanes. On a Gantt chart, swimlanes group tasks by team or workstream so you can see parallel work at a glance. Both borrow the same idea of lanes, but neither is a process map.

Common swimlane diagram mistakes

  • Mapping the ideal process instead of the real one. You cannot improve a process you have not seen as it is. Map the as-is first.
  • Scope too wide. A diagram of 'everything the PMO does' helps no one. Map one process with a clear start and end.
  • Too many lanes. Beyond about seven, the diagram becomes hard to follow. Merge minor participants or split the process.
  • Mixed levels of detail. 'Deliver project' next to 'Click Submit' confuses readers. Keep every step at the same level.
  • Only the happy path. Rejections, rework and escalations are where the time goes. Show the important exceptions.
  • Unlabelled decisions. Every diamond needs a question and labelled exits.
  • Lanes named after individuals. People change roles. Use role names, such as 'Finance approver'.
  • No time data. Without work and wait times you can describe the process but not improve it.
  • Drawing it alone. A diagram built without the people in each lane will be wrong and will not be trusted.

Swimlane diagram checklist

Before you share or publish a swimlane diagram, check that:

  • The title, start trigger and end outcome are clear.
  • There are no more than about seven lanes, named by role, team or system.
  • Every step sits in one lane, and every decision has labelled exits.
  • Important exceptions, loops and escalations are shown, and handoffs are numbered.
  • Work and wait times are noted for the as-is version.
  • Each lane owner has agreed it, and it has an owner, version and review date.

Revisit the diagram after each significant process change and as part of your lessons learned review, so it stays a working document rather than a one-off drawing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a swimlane diagram?

A swimlane diagram is a flowchart split into lanes, one for each person, team or system, showing who carries out each step in a process and where work is handed from one participant to another.

Why is it called a swimlane diagram?

The lanes resemble the lanes of a swimming pool. Each participant stays in their own lane, and you can see when work crosses into another lane.

What is another name for a swimlane diagram?

It is also called a cross-functional flowchart, a deployment flowchart, a Rummler-Brache diagram or a swimlane process map.

What is the difference between a flowchart and a swimlane diagram?

A standard flowchart shows the steps and decisions in a process. A swimlane diagram shows the same steps arranged in lanes, so it also shows who performs each step and where handoffs happen.

What is the difference between a pool and a lane?

In BPMN, a pool represents a whole participant, usually an organisation, and lanes divide a pool into roles or departments.

Should swimlanes be horizontal or vertical?

Either works. Horizontal lanes suit processes with many steps; vertical lanes suit many participants and fewer steps, and fit printed documents better.

How many lanes should a swimlane diagram have?

As few as you need. Three to seven lanes is a practical range; with more than that, consider merging minor participants or splitting the process into separate diagrams.

What symbols are used in a swimlane diagram?

Standard flowchart symbols: ovals for start and end, rectangles for activities, diamonds for decisions, a document shape for forms and arrows for the flow.

Can a swimlane diagram show time?

Yes. Note the work time and waiting time on each step, or add phase columns, so you can calculate elapsed time and flow efficiency.

How do you show a loop or rework in a swimlane diagram?

Draw an arrow from the decision or check back to the earlier step that has to be repeated, and label it with the reason, such as 'information missing'. Frequent loops are a sign of an improvement opportunity.

Can I make a swimlane diagram in Excel?

Yes. Use shaded rows or columns as lanes and shapes or cells for steps, or record the steps, owners and times in a structured sheet such as our workflow template and draw the diagram from that data.

What are swimlanes in a Kanban board?

They are horizontal rows that separate different types of work, teams or priority levels on the same board. They organise the board rather than map a process.

What are swimlanes in a Gantt chart?

They are groupings of tasks by team, workstream or owner, used to show parallel streams of work on a project timeline.
