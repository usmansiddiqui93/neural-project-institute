Project Integration Management: Processes and Examples

Project integration management is the work of coordinating every part of a project (scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources, communications and stakeholders) so that the plans and decisions in each area fit together and serve the same objectives. It is the project manager's own job. This guide explains the seven PMBOK processes, how newer editions of the PMBOK Guide treat integration, a step-by-step method, integrated change control and a fully worked example.

Key takeaways

  • Integration management joins up every other area of the project: one plan, one set of baselines, one change process and one view of performance.
  • The PMBOK Guide sixth edition defines seven integration processes, from Develop Project Charter to Close Project or Phase.
  • The seventh and eighth editions drop knowledge areas, but integration still runs through them, mainly through systems thinking and the Governance performance domain.
  • Integrated change control means assessing every change for its effect on all areas before anyone approves it.
  • Accountability for integration stays with the project manager, even though specialists own their own plans.
  • In Agile teams much of the integration happens through the backlog and short iterations, while a delivery lead integrates across teams, funding and governance.

What is project integration management?

Project integration management is the set of processes and activities that identify, combine, unify and coordinate the work of a project. It turns separate plans for scope, time, cost, quality, risk and resources into one consistent plan, keeps them aligned during delivery, manages changes as a whole and closes the project in an orderly way.

Most project management activities focus on one area. Integration looks across all of them. A decision in one area almost always affects another: adding scope changes cost and time, cutting testing raises quality risk, and moving a milestone changes who is needed when. Integration is how the project manager sees those links and makes balanced decisions. In practice it comes down to four verbs:

  • Unify. Combine the subsidiary plans into a single project management plan that uses the same assumptions, dates and priorities.
  • Coordinate. Make sure teams, suppliers and workstreams hand work to each other smoothly and on time.
  • Decide. Weigh competing objectives and agree trade-offs with the sponsor when they conflict.
  • Control. Track performance against the project baseline and manage changes as one system rather than area by area.

Integration at three levels

The PMBOK Guide sixth edition describes the project manager as performing integration at three levels. It is a useful way to see that integration is more than paperwork:

LevelWhat it meansExample
Process levelMaking the project management processes work together, so outputs of one feed inputs of anotherThe approved change log updates the schedule, budget and risk register on the same day
Cognitive levelUsing judgement, experience and leadership to make sense of the whole projectSpotting that a supplier delay and a new compliance rule together threaten the go-live date
Context levelTaking account of the organisation, technology and environment the project sits inAdjusting reporting for a new portfolio governance process or a move to Agile delivery
Tip

Team members and specialists can own the risk plan, the schedule or the budget. The project manager is the person accountable for integrating them, and that accountability cannot be delegated away.

Why integration management matters

Projects rarely fail because one plan is poor. They struggle because good plans do not fit together: the schedule assumes people the resource plan does not provide, the budget ignores risk contingency, or a change is approved on cost alone and quietly breaks the testing window. Integration management exists to catch those gaps.

Without integrationWith integration
Each workstream reports in its own format, so nobody sees the whole pictureOne status report draws on a single set of baselines and metrics
Changes are approved by whoever is asked firstEvery change goes through one process and is assessed across all areas
Plans are written once and filedPlans are kept aligned and re-baselined only through approved change
Trade-offs are made implicitly, often by the team under pressureTrade-offs are made explicitly, with the sponsor, and recorded
Lessons are gathered at the end, if at allKnowledge is captured and reused throughout the project
Closure is an email saying the project is doneFormal acceptance, handover, release of resources and archived records

Integration is also where the classic project constraints meet. When scope, time, cost and quality pull against each other, the integration role is to show the options and their consequences, not to absorb the pressure silently.

The 7 project integration management processes

The sixth edition of the PMBOK Guide treats integration as one of ten knowledge areas and gives it seven processes. Later editions reorganised the content (see the next section), but these processes are still the clearest map of the work and they remain common in training and exam preparation.

ProcessProcess groupPurposeKey outputs
1. Develop Project CharterInitiatingFormally authorise the project and give the project manager authorityProject charter, assumption log
2. Develop Project Management PlanPlanningDefine, prepare and coordinate all plan components into one planProject management plan, baselines
3. Direct and Manage Project WorkExecutingLead the work and implement approved changesDeliverables, work performance data, issue log, change requests
4. Manage Project KnowledgeExecutingUse existing knowledge and create new knowledgeLessons learned register
5. Monitor and Control Project WorkMonitoring and ControllingTrack, review and report overall progress against the planWork performance reports, change requests
6. Perform Integrated Change ControlMonitoring and ControllingReview, approve or reject and manage all change requestsApproved change requests, change log
7. Close Project or PhaseClosingFinalise all activities and hand overFinal report, transitioned product, archived records

1. Develop Project Charter

The project charter authorises the project, names the project manager and sets out the purpose, objectives, high-level scope, milestones, budget, key risks and success criteria. Its main inputs are business documents such as the business case and benefits plan, plus any agreements. The charter is the reference point for every later trade-off: if a change does not serve the charter's objectives, that is a strong reason to reject it.

2. Develop Project Management Plan

Here you bring the subsidiary plans together: scope, requirements, schedule, cost, quality, resource, communications, risk, procurement and stakeholder engagement plans, plus the change and configuration management plans. The output is one integrated plan with three baselines (scope, schedule and cost). Integration in this process means checking that the plans agree, for example that the schedule includes the review cycles in the quality plan and the budget includes the contingency in the risk plan.

3. Direct and Manage Project Work

This is the execution engine: assigning work, removing blockers, managing suppliers and implementing approved changes, corrective actions, preventive actions and defect repairs. It produces the deliverables and the raw work performance data (hours, dates, costs, defects). Problems that need action go into the issue log.

4. Manage Project Knowledge

This process covers both explicit knowledge (documents, data) and tacit knowledge (what people know but rarely write down). Typical activities are short reviews after milestones, pairing experienced and new staff, and keeping a lessons learned register that is used during the project, not just filed at the end.

5. Monitor and Control Project Work

You compare actual performance with the baselines, forecast where the project will finish and decide whether action is needed. Techniques include variance analysis, trend analysis and earned value management. The output is a set of work performance reports, often a project status report, and any change requests needed to bring the project back on track.

6. Perform Integrated Change Control

Every change request, whatever its source, is logged, assessed for its effect on all areas and decided by the person or board with the authority to do so. Approved changes update the plan and baselines; every decision is recorded in the change log. This process links closely to configuration management, which controls versions of the deliverables and documents themselves.

7. Close Project or Phase

Closure confirms that the work is complete and accepted, transfers the product to its owners, closes contracts, releases people, finalises the lessons learned and archives records. Phase closure follows the same steps at the end of each phase.

Integration management in PMBOK 7 and PMBOK 8

If you studied the sixth edition, the newer editions can look as if integration has vanished. It has not; it has been reorganised.

  • Seventh edition (2021). PMI replaced the knowledge areas and process groups with twelve principles and eight performance domains. There is no integration knowledge area. Integration shows up as a way of working: one principle asks project teams to recognise, evaluate and respond to system interactions (systems thinking), and the performance domains are designed to be used together rather than in isolation.
  • Eighth edition (published by PMI in November 2025). The eighth edition brings processes back in a non-prescriptive form. According to published summaries, it has six principles, seven performance domains (Governance, Scope, Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders, Resources and Risk), five focus areas that mirror the old process groups (Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring and Controlling, Closing) and 40 processes. Much of the former integration work, such as integrating and aligning project plans, sits within the Governance domain, and one of the six principles is to adopt a holistic view.
EditionStructureWhere integration lives
6th (2017)10 knowledge areas, 5 process groups, 49 processesIntegration knowledge area with 7 processes
7th (2021)12 principles, 8 performance domains, no processesSystems thinking principle; the domains working together
8th (2025)6 principles, 7 performance domains, 5 focus areas, 40 processesMainly the Governance domain, plus the holistic view principle

The practical conclusion is the same whichever edition you use: someone has to join up the plans, decisions and changes, and that someone is the project manager.

Tip

For the exam, check the current PMP Examination Content Outline rather than any single edition of the PMBOK Guide. The updated PMP exam that took effect on 9 July 2026 is organised into three domains (People 33%, Process 41%, Business Environment 26%), and integration topics such as planning, change and closure appear across them. How to manage project integration step by step

The processes describe what has to happen. The steps below describe how most project managers actually do it, with the artefact you should have at each stage.

  1. Agree the charter. Confirm purpose, objectives, success criteria, high-level scope, budget and your authority with the sponsor. Artefact: signed charter (start from our project charter template).
  2. Map the interfaces. List workstreams, suppliers and other projects that depend on yours, and the hand-offs between them. Artefact: dependency list or RAID log.
  3. Build one integrated plan. Have each owner draft their plan, then reconcile them in a planning workshop: same dates, same assumptions, same priorities. Artefact: project management plan.
  4. Set and approve baselines. Fix scope, schedule and cost baselines and agree tolerances (for example, plus or minus 5% on cost before escalation). Artefact: baselined schedule and budget.
  5. Set up one change process. Define who can raise changes, who assesses them, who decides at which threshold and how decisions are communicated. Artefact: change management plan and change log.
  6. Run a regular control cycle. Weekly or fortnightly, collect performance data, compare with baselines, forecast, and decide on action. Artefact: status report and updated forecasts.
  7. Keep risks, issues and changes connected. When a risk becomes an issue, or an issue needs a change, move it between logs explicitly so nothing is lost. Artefact: linked risk register, issue log and change log.
  8. Capture knowledge as you go. Hold short reviews after each milestone and act on them in the next phase. Artefact: lessons learned register.
  9. Close formally. Obtain acceptance, hand over, close contracts, release people and archive. Artefact: closure report.

Steps 6 and 7 are where integration either works or fails. A project with a tidy plan and no real control cycle drifts; a project with a control cycle but separate, unconnected logs makes decisions on partial information.

Integrated change control step by step

Integrated change control is the most visible part of integration management. The word integrated matters: a change is assessed across every area, not only the one that raised it. Our change control guide covers the wider process; this is the integrated core.

  1. Log the request. Record who raised it, what is requested, why and by when. Give it a unique ID in the change log.
  2. Check it is a change. Some requests are clarifications of agreed scope or defects. Route those correctly rather than through the board.
  3. Assess the impact across all areas. Scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources, procurement and stakeholders. Ask each owner for their part and consolidate.
  4. Identify options. Usually at least three: approve as requested, approve a reduced or phased version, or reject. Include the effect of doing nothing.
  5. Decide at the right level. Changes inside the project manager's tolerances can be decided by the project manager; larger ones go to the change control board or sponsor.
  6. Update the plan and baselines. For approved changes, re-baseline only the elements affected and record the version.
  7. Communicate the decision. Tell the requester, the team and affected stakeholders, using the communication plan.
  8. Implement and verify. Track the change as work, then confirm it delivered what was approved.
DecisionWhen it fitsWhat happens next
ApproveBenefit clearly outweighs impact and funding is availableUpdate baselines, schedule the work, communicate
Approve with conditionsWorthwhile but only if scope, timing or cost is adjustedAgree the conditions, then update baselines
DeferValuable but not now, for example after go-liveAdd to a later release or phase backlog
RejectDoes not serve the charter objectives or costs too muchRecord the reason and tell the requester
EscalateExceeds the board's authority or affects other projectsRefer to the sponsor, programme or portfolio board
Tip

Agree change thresholds before the project starts. A common pattern is that the project manager can approve changes under a set cost and with no effect on milestones, the change board handles the rest, and anything that breaches the project's overall tolerances goes to the sponsor. Worked example: integrating a CRM replacement project

The figures in this example are illustrative, but the steps are the ones you would follow on a real project.

1. The charter and integrated plan

A distribution company is replacing its customer relationship management (CRM) system. The charter sets a budget of £480,000, a go-live date of 2 March and a success measure of all 120 sales and service users working in the new system within four weeks of launch. Planning reconciles five subsidiary plans into one:

Baseline or planKey contentIntegration check made
ScopeConfigure CRM, migrate 8 years of customer data, integrate with the finance system, train 120 usersData migration and integration both appear in the WBS with owners
ScheduleDesign 8 weeks, build 12 weeks, test 6 weeks, train and cut over 4 weeksTraining dates fit the sales team's quiet period in February
Cost£440,000 planned work plus £40,000 contingencyContingency matches the top risks in the risk register
QualityTwo test cycles and a user acceptance sign-offBoth test cycles have time and testers in the resource plan
RiskTop risks: poor data quality, finance integration delays, low adoptionEach risk has an owner and a response task in the schedule

2. A change request arrives

In week 14 the sales director asks for a mobile app for field sales staff to be included at launch. The project manager logs it as CR-07 and gathers an impact assessment from each owner:

AreaImpact of adding the mobile app at launch
ScopeNew deliverable: configure and test the vendor's mobile app for 45 field users
ScheduleAbout 3 weeks of extra build and test; go-live moves from 2 March to 23 March if nothing else changes
Cost£36,000 for configuration, testing and device management setup
QualityIf the date is held, the second test cycle shrinks from 3 weeks to 1
RiskNew risk: device security policy not yet approved by IT security (rated high)
ResourcesLead CRM consultant already at full capacity in weeks 15 to 20
StakeholdersSales strongly in favour; finance concerned about using most of the contingency

3. Options and decision

OptionCostGo-liveMain risk
A. Add now, move go-live£36,00023 MarchMisses the quiet period for training; year-end data migration clashes
B. Add now, hold the date£36,000 plus overtime2 MarchReduced testing; high chance of defects at launch
C. Go live on 2 March, mobile app as release 2 in April£38,000 (small re-planning cost)2 March, app in AprilField users wait six weeks for mobile access

The change board chooses option C. The scope, schedule and cost baselines for release 1 stay intact. The mobile app becomes a separate, funded release with its own small plan, and the security policy risk is assigned to IT security with a due date before release 2 starts. That is integration management in one decision: the request was judged across every area, the options were explicit and the sponsor made an informed choice.

4. Closing the loop

  • The change log records CR-07 as approved with conditions, with the decision date and approver.
  • The schedule gains a release 2 phase; the release 1 baseline is unchanged.
  • The budget is increased by £38,000 from a separate change budget, leaving the original contingency for release 1 risks.
  • The risk register adds the device security risk; the communication plan adds a field-sales update.
  • The lessons learned register notes that mobile needs were missed in requirements and should be asked about earlier on future CRM projects.

Integration in Agile and hybrid projects

In Agile project management much of the detailed integration happens inside the team. Short iterations, a single ordered backlog and a shared definition of done keep scope, quality and schedule aligned by design. The Product Owner makes most scope trade-offs through backlog ordering, so many changes never need a formal change request.

Integration needPredictive approachAgile approach
One planIntegrated project management planProduct goal, roadmap and ordered backlog
Change handlingChange requests and change boardBacklog refinement and reordering by the Product Owner
Performance viewBaselines, variances, earned valueIncrements, velocity or throughput, burn-up charts
Cross-team coordinationIntegrated schedule and interface managementRelease planning, PI planning, dependency boards
KnowledgeLessons learned registerRetrospectives every iteration

Integration at the project and programme level does not disappear. A delivery lead or project manager still integrates funding and contracts, dependencies between teams, governance and reporting, release and deployment planning, and communication with stakeholders who do not work in sprints. In hybrid projects, a common pattern is a predictive outer plan (budget, milestones, contracts) with Agile delivery inside it, joined by a lightweight change process for anything that affects the outer baselines.

Common integration management mistakes

  • Plans that never reference each other. Fix: hold one reconciliation workshop and keep shared assumptions in a single log.
  • Approving changes on cost alone. Fix: require an impact assessment covering schedule, quality, risk and resources for every change above the threshold.
  • Several change routes. Fix: one entry point, even if small changes are fast-tracked.
  • Re-baselining to hide variance. Fix: re-baseline only through approved changes and keep the original baseline for comparison.
  • Different reporting formats for each workstream. Fix: one status template and one set of metrics.
  • Leaving knowledge and closure to the end. Fix: review after each milestone and close each phase formally.
  • Confusing integration with doing everything. Fix: specialists own their plans; the project manager owns how they fit together.
  • Ignoring other projects. Fix: check shared resources and dependencies with the PMO or programme regularly.

Integration management checklist

Use this checklist at the start of a project and at each phase gate:

  • The charter is signed and states objectives, success criteria and your authority.
  • Every subsidiary plan uses the same dates, assumptions and priorities.
  • Scope, schedule and cost baselines are approved, with tolerances agreed.
  • There is one documented change process with clear decision thresholds.
  • Risks, issues, assumptions and dependencies are logged and linked.
  • Performance is reported in one format against the baselines.
  • Lessons are being captured and used during the project.
  • Closure criteria and the handover owner are known.

Integration management sits within a wider framework of decision rights and approvals.

Frequently asked questions

What is project integration management?

It is the coordination of all project management areas and activities (scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, resources, communications and stakeholders) so that the project works as one system and meets its objectives.

What are the 7 processes of project integration management?

In the PMBOK Guide sixth edition they are Develop Project Charter, Develop Project Management Plan, Direct and Manage Project Work, Manage Project Knowledge, Monitor and Control Project Work, Perform Integrated Change Control, and Close Project or Phase.

Why is project integration management important?

Because decisions in one area affect others. Without integration, each team optimises its own part, changes are assessed in isolation and the project as a whole suffers.

Who is responsible for project integration management?

The project manager. Team members and specialists contribute and own their own plans, but accountability for integrating the project sits with the project manager.

What is the first process in integration management?

Develop Project Charter, which formally authorises the project and gives the project manager authority to apply resources.

What is integrated change control?

A single process for reviewing every change request, assessing its effect on scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk and other areas, deciding on it and recording the decision before the plan and baselines are updated.

What is a change control board?

A group with authority to approve or reject change requests above an agreed threshold. It usually includes the sponsor or their delegate, the project manager and representatives of the main affected areas.

What is the difference between integration management and scope management?

Scope management defines and controls what work is included. Integration management coordinates scope with every other area, so a scope change is assessed for its effect on time, cost, quality and risk too.

Does PMBOK 7 or PMBOK 8 still include integration management?

Neither has an integration knowledge area. The seventh edition covers integration through principles such as systems thinking and performance domains used together; the eighth edition, published in November 2025, places much of the work, such as integrating and aligning plans, in the Governance performance domain.

Is integration management on the PMP exam?

Yes, although not as a separate section. The exam is organised by the People, Process and Business Environment domains, and integration topics such as planning, change, knowledge and closure appear throughout.

What are the main outputs of integration management?

The project charter, the project management plan and baselines, deliverables, work performance reports, the issue log, the change log, the lessons learned register and the final report.

How does integration management work in Agile?

Teams integrate continuously through a single backlog, short iterations and a shared definition of done, while a delivery lead integrates cross-team dependencies, funding, governance and release planning.

What tools help with project integration management?

A work management tool that holds plans, tasks, requests and dashboards in one place, plus linked documents such as the charter, plan, change log, RAID log and lessons learned register.
