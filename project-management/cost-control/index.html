Cost Control in Project Management: Process and Example

Cost control in project management is the regular cycle of measuring what the work done has really cost, comparing it with the approved cost baseline, forecasting the final cost and acting early enough to change the outcome. It is not the same as cutting costs or checking spend against budget. This guide sets out a ten-step monthly process and the techniques behind it, then works through a complete month-end cost review with real numbers.

  • Last updated 25 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • Cost control compares actual cost with the value of work completed (earned value), not just spend with budget.
  • Run it as a fixed monthly (or sprint) cycle: collect, measure, analyse, forecast, decide, record.
  • Track committed costs as well as actuals: purchase orders can lock in an overrun before any invoice arrives.
  • Split a variance into price and quantity effects to find out what you can actually change.
  • Use reserves only for what they were set up for, and change the baseline only through change control.
  • The forecast (EAC) matters more than the variance to date, because it is the number you can still influence.

What is cost control in project management?

Cost control is the process of monitoring project costs against the approved cost baseline, analysing variances, forecasting the final cost and taking corrective or preventive action so the project finishes within its authorised budget. Good cost control measures cost against the work actually completed, and it manages changes to the baseline through formal change control.

Cost control is the monitoring part of project cost management. Planning, cost estimation and building the project budget happen first and produce the cost baseline. Cost control then runs from the first day of work until the final invoice is paid.

In PMI's *PMBOK Guide* sixth edition this was a named process, Control Costs, whose tools included earned value analysis, forecasting, the to-complete performance index and reserve analysis. The seventh edition (2021) replaced processes with principles and performance domains. The eighth edition, published in November 2025, has seven performance domains including Finance, and brings back process guidance in a non-prescriptive form; the Finance domain includes a process for monitoring and controlling finances. The names have changed, but the work is the same. Our PMBOK Guide overview explains the editions.

What cost control is not

  • It is not cost cutting. Cost control can lead to savings, but its job is to make the final cost predictable and agreed, not as low as possible.
  • It is not comparing spend with the budget to date. A project can be "under budget" simply because it is behind schedule. Cost control asks what the completed work should have cost.
  • It is not an accounting report. Finance systems record what has been paid. Cost control also needs accruals, commitments, progress and a forecast.
Tip

A quick test: if your cost report has no measure of progress and no forecast of the final cost, it is a spending report, not a cost control report.

Cost control vs cost management, budgeting and cost reduction

These terms are often used as if they mean the same thing. They do not, and mixing them up leads to the wrong conversations with sponsors.

TermQuestion it answersWhen it happensMain outputs
Cost managementHow will we plan, fund and control costs on this project?Whole life cycleCost management plan, estimates, budget, baseline, reports
Cost estimatingWhat will each piece of work cost?Planning, then re-estimatingCost estimates and their basis
BudgetingHow much money is authorised, and when is it needed?PlanningCost baseline, funding profile, reserves
Cost controlAre we getting the value we planned for the money spent, and where will we finish?Execution, every periodVariances, forecasts (EAC), actions, change requests
Cost reductionHow can we make this cheaper?Any time, often after an overrunSavings options, value engineering, scope trade-offs

In short, cost management is the umbrella, the budget is the yardstick and cost control is the regular act of measuring against it. Your cost management plan should say how cost control will be done: the reporting cycle, the progress rules, the thresholds and who can approve what.

What you need before you can control costs

Cost control fails most often because the groundwork was never laid. Before the first status date, make sure you have:

  • An approved cost baseline: the time-phased budget, broken down to control accounts or work packages. See project baseline.
  • A work breakdown structure with every cost assigned to a work package, so there is nowhere for costs to hide.
  • Progress measurement rules agreed in advance: 0/100 for short tasks, 50/50 or weighted milestones for longer ones, units completed for repetitive work.
  • Cost data feeds at the same level of detail: timesheets with rates, supplier invoices, purchase orders and accruals.
  • Reserves defined: a contingency reserve for identified risks (usually held by the project manager) and a management reserve for unknown risks (usually held by the sponsor).
  • Thresholds and authority levels: what size of variance triggers a report, an escalation or a change request.
  • A change control process so that approved scope changes update the baseline and unapproved ones do not slip in. See change control.

Earned value figures built on vague progress rules or incomplete cost data look precise but tell you very little, so fix any gaps first.

The cost control process step by step

The cycle below suits most projects reporting monthly. Short or fast-moving projects can run it every fortnight or every sprint. Each step has a clear output, so the cycle is easy to audit.

  1. Fix the status date. Everyone reports progress and cost as at the same cut-off, such as the last working day of the month. *Output: status date.*
  2. Collect actual costs. Pull labour from timesheets at the agreed rates, invoices from suppliers, and accruals for work done but not yet invoiced. *Output: actual cost (AC) per work package.*
  3. Record commitments. List open purchase orders and contract balances that are signed but not yet incurred. *Output: commitments register.*
  4. Measure progress. Apply the agreed rules to each work package and convert progress into earned value: EV = % complete x budget. *Output: EV per work package.*
  5. Calculate the indicators. Cost variance (CV = EV - AC), cost performance index (CPI = EV / AC), schedule variance and SPI, for each package and in total. *Output: variance table.*
  6. Compare with thresholds. Flag packages and totals outside the agreed limits. *Output: exceptions list.*
  7. Analyse the exceptions. For each one, find the root cause and split price effects from quantity effects. Decide whether the cause will continue. *Output: variance explanations.*
  8. Forecast the final cost. Recalculate the estimate at completion using more than one method, check it against commitments, and work out the TCPI. *Output: EAC, VAC, TCPI.*
  9. Decide and act. Agree corrective or preventive actions with owners and dates, draw on reserves where the rules allow, and raise change requests where the baseline can no longer be met. *Output: action log, reserve log, change requests.*
  10. Report and follow up. Publish the results in the project status report and check at the next cycle whether the actions worked. *Output: cost report.*

Steps 2 to 4 are data collection, steps 5 to 8 are analysis, and steps 9 and 10 are where the value lies. Automate the data collection wherever you can so the review meeting is spent on decisions.

Worked example: month-end cost review for an office fit-out

A company is refurbishing two floors of an office for 180 staff. The work is planned over ten months. The budget is set up as follows:

Budget elementAmountHeld by
Work package budgets (budget at completion, BAC)$600,000Control account managers
Contingency reserve for identified risks$45,000Project manager
Management reserve for unknown risks$30,000Sponsor
Total authorised funding$675,000

The control thresholds in the cost management plan are: report any work package with a CPI below 0.95 or a CV worse than -$5,000, and escalate to the sponsor if the project CPI falls below 0.90 or the forecast exceeds the work package budgets by more than the remaining contingency.

Planned value (PV) at month five is $290,000: the budget for the work scheduled to be done by now.

Reading the figures

  • CV = 249,500 - 271,000 = -$21,500, or -8.6% of the work done. The completed work has cost $21,500 more than it was budgeted to cost.
  • CPI = 249,500 / 271,000 = 0.92: about 92 cents of planned work for every dollar spent. This is below the 0.95 reporting threshold but above the 0.90 escalation threshold.
  • SV = 249,500 - 290,000 = -$40,500 and SPI = 0.86: the project is also behind schedule.
  • Packages 2, 3 and 4 breach the package threshold. Package 5 looks healthy on CPI, but see the commitments check below.
  • A simple budget-versus-spend view would say: "We planned to spend $290,000 and have spent $271,000, so we are $19,000 under budget." That is wrong. The project is spending less only because it is doing less.

Variance analysis: what is driving each overrun

The project manager meets each package owner and finds three different causes:

For partitions, the variance is split into its price and quantity parts. The package covers 3,000 square metres at a budget of $50 per square metre: $20 for materials and $30 for labour (0.75 hours at $40 per hour). By month five, 900 square metres are complete.

This matters because the two halves need different responses. The price rise will continue for the remaining 2,100 square metres (another $8,400) and was a known risk. The lost hours are caused by poor sequencing with the M&E contractor, which the team can fix.

Checking commitments

Earned value only looks backwards. The commitments register shows money that is already promised:

PackageBACAC to dateOpen commitmentsAC + commitmentsUncommitted budget
M&E$180,000$101,000$46,000$147,000$33,000

Two findings stand out. First, IT and AV is already overcommitted by $6,000, even though its CPI is 1.06. The facilities manager asked the supplier for extra meeting-room screens, and the purchase order was amended without a change request. Earned value would not have shown this for months. Second, the project has committed or spent 85% of its budget while completing only 42% of the work, so only $91,000 of the budget is still free to redirect.

Forecasting the final cost

The project manager calculates three forecasts and compares them:

Forecast methodCalculationEACAssumption
Remaining work at budgeted ratesAC + (BAC - EV) = 271,000 + 350,500$621,500The overruns were one-offs
Current efficiency continuesBAC / CPI = 600,000 / 0.921$651,700The month-five CPI holds to the end
Bottom-up re-estimate by packageSum of package owners' forecasts (below)$639,400Specific causes, specific fixes

The bottom-up forecast is built package by package: design $57,000 (complete), demolition $53,000 (complete), M&E $196,000 (another $5,000 of rework expected on floor two even after the fix), partitions $164,400 ($8,400 more price impact, labour back to plan), IT and AV $101,000 (committed) and furniture and move $68,000 (purchase order plus a removals quote).

The to-complete performance index to finish within the original $600,000 is (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC) = 350,500 / 329,000 = 1.07. The project would need to become 7% more efficient than planned on the remaining work, while it is currently running at 0.92. That is not credible, so the conversation moves to reserves and decisions.

Reserves and decisions

Plasterboard price rise ($12,000 total)Approved: identified risk R-11 occurredContingency reserve+$12,000 to partitions

After the transfers, the work package budgets total $626,000. The bottom-up forecast of $639,400 gives a variance at completion of -$13,400, made up of M&E (-$16,000) and partitions labour (-$2,400), partly offset by design (+$3,000) and furniture (+$2,000). Contingency of $25,000 and management reserve of $24,000 remain. The project is still expected to finish within its total authorised funding, but the sponsor now knows exactly why, and which risk is left: M&E performance on floor two.

Tip

Notice that the M&E overrun was not paid for from contingency. Contingency exists for the risks it was calculated for. Using it to cover poor performance hides the problem and leaves nothing for the risks that are still ahead. Record the variance honestly and fix the cause.

Tracking the trend month by month

A single month's figures tell you where you are. The trend tells you whether things are getting better or worse, and when they changed. Here are the cumulative figures for the office fit-out, with the period CPI (that month's EV divided by that month's AC).

MonthPVEVACCumulative CVCumulative CPIPeriod CPISPI

The period CPI dropped sharply in month three, when demolition and M&E started. The cumulative CPI drifted down more slowly, because it carries the good early months with it. That is why you should watch both: the period figure shows a problem quickly, and the cumulative figure is the better basis for forecasts. The slight rise in the month-five period CPI (0.90) is the first sign that the resequencing is helping.

Plot cumulative PV, EV and AC on one chart and the story is visible at a glance.

Cost control techniques and when to use them

Many articles on this topic list estimating methods such as analogous or three-point estimating. Those belong to planning. The techniques below are the ones you use to control costs once work has started.

TechniqueWhat it tells youUse it when
Earned value management (EVM)Cost and schedule performance against the value of work doneAny project with a budget and measurable progress
Variance analysisWhy a variance happened, split into price and quantityA package breaches a threshold
Trend analysisWhether performance is improving or getting worseEvery period, using CPI and SPI over time
Forecasting (EAC, ETC, VAC)Where the project is likely to finishEvery period; re-estimate bottom-up when something changes
To-complete performance index (TCPI)How efficient the remaining work must be to hit a targetTesting whether a budget is still achievable
Commitment trackingMoney already promised but not yet spentProjects with significant purchasing or subcontracting
Reserve analysisWhether remaining contingency matches remaining riskAfter each risk review and each drawdown
Change controlWhether a cost change is approved and fundedAny change to scope, specification or baseline
Value engineeringWays to deliver the same function for lessBefore committing major spend, or to recover an overrun
Procurement controlsPrices, terms and approvals on bought-in costsBefore purchase orders are raised or amended
Timesheet and rate controlsAccurate labour cost by work packageLabour-heavy projects such as IT and consulting

On small projects, EVM can be done in a spreadsheet at work package level. On large or government projects, it is often a contractual requirement with a formal earned value management system. Either way, the techniques work as a set: EVM finds the problem, variance analysis explains it, forecasting sizes it and change control deals with it.

Setting cost control thresholds

Thresholds stop the project manager from reporting every small variance while making sure significant ones are never missed. They belong in the cost management plan and should be agreed with the sponsor before work starts.

There is no industry-wide standard. The right levels depend on estimate accuracy, contract type and the organisation's tolerance for risk. The table below is an illustration to adapt:

LevelExample triggerRequired response
GreenCPI 0.95 to 1.05 and package CV within plus or minus $5,000Report figures only
AmberCPI 0.90 to 0.95 or 1.05 to 1.10, or package CV beyond $5,000Written variance explanation and action plan
RedCPI below 0.90 or above 1.10, or forecast overrun greater than remaining contingencyEscalate to the sponsor; revised EAC; consider a change request
  • Use both a ratio and a money amount. A ratio alone flags tiny packages; a money amount alone misses steady drift on large ones.
  • Set favourable thresholds too. A CPI well above 1 often means missing costs, overstated progress or padded estimates.
  • Tighten thresholds as the project matures. Early on, estimates are less reliable and there is time to recover; late on, there is not.
  • Include a forecast threshold. A forecast that exceeds the remaining reserves should always be escalated, whatever today's CPI says.

How to bring an over-budget project back on track

When the forecast is worse than the budget, work through the options roughly in this order, starting with those that do not need anyone else's approval.

OptionEffectTrade-off or approval needed
Fix the root causeImproves efficiency on remaining workNone, but it needs a real diagnosis, not a guess
Resequence or re-plan the workRemoves waiting time and reworkMay affect the schedule
Change the resource mixLower rates, or more experienced people who work fasterCheck the effect on quality and on other projects
Renegotiate supplier termsLowers bought-in costsDepends on the contract; can strain relationships
Value engineeringSame function for lessNeeds design and stakeholder agreement
Use contingency reserveFunds identified risks that have occurredOnly for the risks it was set up for
Reduce or defer scopeLowers the remaining costChange request; sponsor and customer approval
Request more budgetChanges the baselineChange request; sponsor or board approval

To find the root cause, ask the people doing the work, not just the reports. A fishbone diagram helps when the cause is not obvious. Whatever you decide, record it, assign an owner and check the result at the next cycle.

When to change the cost baseline

Change the baseline only when an approved change alters the scope or the funding, or when the existing baseline is so far from reality that it no longer helps anyone measure performance (a re-baseline). Both go through change control. Never move the baseline quietly to make a variance disappear: you lose the history and the sponsor loses trust in every number that follows. Unmanaged scope creep is the most common reason a baseline drifts out of date.

Who is responsible for cost control?

RoleResponsibilities
Project managerRuns the cycle, owns the forecast and contingency, proposes actions and change requests, reports to the sponsor
Control account or work package managersReport progress and forecasts for their packages, explain variances, carry out actions
Project controller or PMOCollects data, calculates EVM measures, checks consistency, maintains the change and reserve logs
FinanceProvides actual costs, accruals and commitments, and reconciles project and company records
ProcurementControls purchase orders, contract changes and supplier claims
SponsorApproves baseline changes and use of management reserve; decides when forecasts exceed tolerance
Team membersBook time accurately and raise issues early

Make package owners responsible for their own forecasts: when the person closest to the work has to say what it will cost to finish, problems surface earlier.

Cost control in Agile projects

Agile teams usually work with a fairly fixed cost per sprint and flexible scope, so cost control focuses on the cost of value delivered rather than the cost of individual tasks. The same ideas apply at release level.

Example. A team of six costs $48,000 per two-week sprint. The release has a budget of $480,000 (ten sprints) and a backlog of 400 story points. A contractor was added in sprints three and four at $3,000 per sprint. After four sprints:

MeasureCalculationResult
Actual cost (AC)4 x 48,000 + 2 x 3,000$198,000
Planned value (PV)4 / 10 x 480,000$192,000
Earned value (EV)136 of 400 points done = 34% x 480,000$163,200
CPI163,200 / 198,0000.82
SPI163,200 / 192,0000.85
Cost per point198,000 / 136 (planned 1,200)$1,456
EAC if nothing changes480,000 / 0.824about $582,400

Instead of asking for $100,000 more, the product owner reviews the backlog with stakeholders and removes 60 points of low-value items, leaving 340 points. At the current cost per point, the remaining 204 points cost about $297,000, giving a forecast of $495,000: within $15,000 of budget and a decision the sponsor can make with full information.

Points only work as a progress measure if the team sizes the backlog consistently and the total is updated when the backlog changes. Our guide to Scrum velocity covers how to use velocity for forecasting.

Common cost control mistakes

  • Comparing spend with budget without measuring progress. It hides overruns on projects that are behind schedule.
  • Waiting for month-end finance figures that arrive two or three weeks late. Use timesheets, accruals and commitments to get a current view.
  • Ignoring committed costs. Purchase orders and contract changes can lock in an overrun long before invoices arrive.
  • Subjective percent complete, such as tasks stuck at 90% for weeks. Agree objective progress rules.
  • Spending contingency on performance problems. It leaves nothing for the risks still ahead.
  • Moving the baseline to hide overruns. Change it only through change control, and keep the history.
  • Reporting variances without a forecast. The sponsor needs to know where the project will finish, not just where it is.
  • Treating symptoms instead of causes. Overtime to recover a schedule often makes the cost position worse.

Tools and templates for cost control

For small and medium projects, a well-built spreadsheet is enough: one row per work package with BAC, percent complete, EV, AC, commitments, CV, CPI and EAC. Start your baseline from our project cost estimate template, track monthly planned and actual spend in the budget template, log realised risks in the risk register template and use the change request template for baseline changes. Buying-heavy projects should also follow the controls in project procurement management.

For larger portfolios, look for project management software that links timesheets, budgets and progress, so actual cost and earned value are captured as the work happens rather than rebuilt each month.

Frequently asked questions

What is cost control in project management?

Cost control is the process of monitoring project costs against the approved cost baseline, analysing variances, forecasting the final cost and taking action so the project finishes within its authorised budget. It measures cost against the work actually completed, not just against the budget to date.

What are the steps in the cost control process?

Fix the status date, collect actual costs and accruals, record commitments, measure progress as earned value, calculate CV and CPI, compare with thresholds, analyse the causes of exceptions, forecast the final cost, agree actions and change requests, then report and follow up.

What is the difference between cost control and cost management?

Cost management is the whole discipline: planning, estimating, budgeting and controlling costs. Cost control is the part that happens during execution, where actual performance is measured against the baseline and corrected.

Is cost control the same as cost reduction?

No. Cost reduction aims to make something cheaper. Cost control aims to make the final cost predictable and within the agreed budget. Cost control may lead to cost reduction when a project needs to recover from an overrun.

What is the main technique used for project cost control?

Earned value management is the most widely used, because it compares actual cost with the value of work completed and separates cost performance from schedule performance. It works best alongside variance analysis, forecasting and commitment tracking.

What are committed costs and why do they matter?

Committed costs are amounts promised through signed purchase orders or contracts but not yet incurred or invoiced. They matter because they can lock in an overrun before it appears in actual costs, as in the IT and AV package in our example.

What are control thresholds?

Agreed limits on variances, such as a CPI below 0.95 or a package overrun above a set amount, that trigger a written explanation, escalation or a change request. They are set in the cost management plan; there is no universal standard.

How often should project costs be reviewed?

At every reporting period, which is usually monthly. Short or high-risk projects benefit from fortnightly or per-sprint reviews. The key is a fixed status date so that progress and costs relate to the same point in time.

How do you bring an over-budget project back on track?

Validate the data, find the root cause, then consider fixing that cause, resequencing work, changing the resource mix, renegotiating with suppliers or value engineering. If those are not enough, use contingency for identified risks, reduce scope or request more budget through change control.

When should the cost baseline be changed?

Only when an approved change alters scope or funding, or when the baseline is so unrealistic that a formal re-baseline is needed. Both go through change control, and the original baseline and the reasons for the change are kept.

Who is responsible for cost control?

The project manager runs the process and owns the forecast. Work package owners report progress and explain variances, finance and procurement supply cost and commitment data, and the sponsor approves baseline changes and the use of management reserve.

What is the difference between contingency reserve and management reserve?

Contingency reserve covers identified risks and is usually controlled by the project manager as part of the cost baseline. Management reserve covers unforeseen work within scope, sits outside the baseline and needs sponsor approval to use.

How does cost control work in Agile projects?

Agile teams usually have a known cost per sprint, so they track cost per story point and release-level earned value, then control cost mainly by reprioritising the backlog. Release EV is the share of planned points completed multiplied by the release budget.
