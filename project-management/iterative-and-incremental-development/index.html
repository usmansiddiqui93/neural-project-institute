Iterative and Incremental Development: Examples and Steps

Iterative and incremental development is a way of delivering a project in small, usable pieces (increments) while improving the product through repeated cycles of building, getting feedback and adjusting (iterations). It is the delivery pattern behind Agile frameworks such as Scrum, but it is much older than Agile and works well beyond software. This guide explains the difference between the two ideas, shows how they combine, works through two examples, gives a step-by-step planning process and helps you decide when to use the approach.

Key takeaways

  • Iterative means improving the same thing through repeated cycles of feedback. Incremental means delivering the solution in usable pieces that add up over time.
  • Used together, they give users something valuable early and let the team learn and correct course with every cycle. Agile and Scrum are built on this combination.
  • PMI's Agile Practice Guide describes four life cycles: predictive, iterative, incremental and agile. Agile combines iterative and incremental working.
  • Plan by slicing work into thin, end-to-end increments ordered by value and risk, then run short, fixed-length cycles that each end with a review and a decision.
  • The approach suits uncertain requirements and work that can be used in parts. It needs available stakeholders, a clear definition of done and active control of scope.
  • It is not new: Larman and Basili trace iterative and incremental development to engineering programmes of the 1950s and 1960s.

What is iterative and incremental development?

Iterative and incremental development is a delivery approach in which a team builds a solution in a series of short cycles. Each cycle adds a usable piece of the solution (the increment) and uses feedback on what already exists to refine and correct it (the iteration). The aim is early value, fast learning and lower risk.

The two words describe different things, and it helps to separate them:

  • Iterative development means repeating a cycle of building, reviewing and improving the same piece of work until it is good enough. Each pass makes it more correct. PMI's Agile Practice Guide (2017) describes an iterative life cycle as one that uses feedback on unfinished work to improve and modify it.
  • Incremental development means delivering the solution in parts, each of which works on its own and adds to what came before. Users get something they can use before the whole solution is complete. The same guide describes an incremental life cycle as one that provides finished deliverables the customer may be able to use immediately.

A simple analogy: writing a long report. Working iteratively, you draft the whole report roughly, ask for comments, then redraft it several times. Working incrementally, you finish and publish the executive summary first, then each chapter in turn. Working iteratively and incrementally, you publish a useful first chapter, collect reactions, improve it, and use what you learned to shape the next chapter.

Iterative vs incremental: the key differences

IterativeIncremental
Core ideaRefine the same thing through repeated cyclesAdd new, usable pieces one at a time
What each cycle producesA better version of existing workAdditional working functionality or deliverables
When users get valueOften only at the end, unless combined with incrementsAfter the first increment
Feedback focusIs this right? How can we improve it?Is this useful? What should come next?
Main benefitQuality and fit improve as the team learnsEarlier value and visible progress
Main riskEndless refinement without deliveringBuilding the wrong pieces well, or pieces that do not fit together
ExampleA prototype checkout redesigned after three rounds of user testingRelease login, then search, then payments

The two answer different questions. Iteration asks how do we make this right? Increments ask how do we deliver this in pieces? You can do either without the other, but each covers the other's weakness: increments get value out early, and iteration stops those increments being built on wrong assumptions.

Predictive, iterative, incremental and agile life cycles

PMI's Agile Practice Guide (2017), published with the Agile Alliance, compares four types of project life cycle. The table below summarises its comparison in plain English:

Life cycleRequirementsActivitiesDeliveryMain goal
PredictiveFixed earlyEach performed once for the whole projectSingle delivery at the endManage cost
IterativeDynamicRepeated until the solution is rightSingle deliveryCorrectness of the solution
IncrementalDynamicPerformed once for each incrementFrequent smaller deliveriesSpeed
AgileDynamicRepeated until right, within each incrementFrequent small deliveriesCustomer value through frequent delivery and feedback

Agile, in other words, is iterative and incremental. PMI's guidance also treats these as points on a continuum from predictive to adaptive, with hybrid approaches in between: for example, a fixed-scope construction phase run predictively alongside an iteratively designed software component. Our guides to waterfall project management and Agile vs waterfall cover the predictive end in detail.

How iterative and incremental development work together

In practice, most teams combine both in one repeating cycle:

  1. Plan the next small increment, choosing the most valuable or riskiest piece.
  2. Build it to a finished, usable standard.
  3. Review it with users and stakeholders.
  4. Adapt: refine the increment and update the plan for what comes next.
  5. Release when there is enough value, then repeat.

In Scrum

Scrum makes this cycle explicit. Each Sprint, a fixed timebox of one month or less, produces at least one Increment. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes an Increment as a concrete stepping stone towards the Product Goal: it adds to all previous Increments, must be usable and must meet the Definition of Done. Several Increments can be created within one Sprint.

The Sprint Review is the iterative part: the team and stakeholders inspect the outcome and adapt the Product Backlog, so the next Sprint builds on what was learned. The Sprint Retrospective applies the same idea to the way the team works, improving the process as well as the product.

Note that the Sprint is the iteration and the Increment is the output. A team can release Increments to users during a Sprint; the Sprint Review is a point to inspect progress, not a gate that holds back releases. See our guide to the Increment in Scrum.

Increment then iterate, or iterate then increment

Teams combine the two in different orders depending on uncertainty:

  • Increment, then iterate: split the product into pieces, deliver the first and improve it based on feedback while building the next. This is the usual pattern when the overall direction is clear.
  • Iterate, then increment: run a few rapid iterations on a prototype or spike to settle the direction, then deliver in increments. This suits work where even the shape of the solution is uncertain.

What changes for the project manager

Iterative and incremental delivery does not remove project management; it changes when and how planning, control and reporting happen. The table compares the main activities:

ActivityPredictive projectIterative and incremental project
PlanningDetailed plan for the whole project at the startOutline roadmap for the whole project; detailed plans for the next one or two cycles only
ScopeFixed and controlled through change requestsPrioritised backlog; scope is the main variable within a fixed time and budget
BudgetEstimated bottom-up and approved onceOften funded per release or per period, with a review before each new tranche
Progress reportingPercent complete against the baselineWorking increments delivered, plus burnup or throughput trends
Risk managementRisk register reviewed at milestonesRiskiest assumptions tested in early increments; risks reviewed every cycle
GovernanceStage gates between phasesRelease or funding decisions informed by what has been delivered
QualityTesting phase near the endTesting and acceptance in every cycle, against a definition of done

The project manager's job shifts from protecting a baseline to keeping the outcome, budget and backlog aligned, removing obstacles and making sure stakeholders turn up to reviews. In a hybrid project you may do both: run fixed contracts and milestones predictively while the team delivers increments within them.

Tip

Report progress in delivered value, not activity. 'Releases 1 and 2 are live and phone bookings are down 20%' tells a sponsor far more than '45% complete'. (Illustrative figures.)

Worked example: an online booking system

This illustrative example follows a team building an online booking system for a chain of physiotherapy clinics, using two-week iterations and releasing every second iteration.

ReleaseAdded (incremental)Refined from feedback (iterative)
1 (weeks 1–4)View available slots and book one clinic, one practitioner; confirmation emailBooking form cut from 12 fields to 6 after test users abandoned it
2 (weeks 5–8)All clinics and practitioners; cancel and rebookSlot display changed from a list to a calendar after reception staff feedback
3 (weeks 9–12)Online payment of deposits; SMS remindersReminder timing moved from 48 to 24 hours before, based on no-show data
4 (weeks 13–16)Practitioner diary sync and reporting for clinic managersPayment flow simplified after a spike in failed card payments

Each release is usable on its own, and each one also improves what was released before. Compare when users first get something they can use under four approaches to the same 16-week project:

ApproachFirst usable deliveryWhat happens to the booking form problem
Big bang (predictive)Week 16Found after launch, when it is expensive to fix
Purely iterativeWeek 16, after several internal prototypesFound in a prototype review, but no one benefits until the end
Purely incrementalWeek 4Found in release 1 but not revisited, because the plan moves on to the next piece
Iterative and incrementalWeek 4Found in release 1 and fixed before most users ever see it

The combined approach is not faster at building everything. Its advantage is that value starts sooner and mistakes are caught when they are cheap.

Worked example outside software: a staff training programme

Iterative and incremental development works for any deliverable that can be used in parts. In this illustrative example, an HR team must deliver a six-module management training programme to 200 new line managers over six months.

CycleIncrement deliveredWhat was learned and changed
Month 1Module 1 (giving feedback) piloted with 12 managersSessions shortened from a full day to two half-days; more role-play
Month 2Module 1 for all; module 2 (one-to-ones) pilotedPre-reading replaced with a short video after low completion
Months 3–4Modules 2–4 rolled outModule 4 split in two after attendees asked for more on absence management
Months 5–6Modules 5–6 and a refreshed module 1Module 1 updated with examples drawn from earlier cohorts

Managers started benefiting in month one, the expensive mistake of a full-day format was found with 12 people rather than 200, and the programme ended up closer to what managers needed than the original design. The same pattern suits marketing campaigns, policy rollouts, research projects and the design stages of construction projects.

How to plan iterative and incremental delivery, step by step

  1. Define the outcome and a measure of success, not just a list of features. For example: 'Clients can book online, and phone bookings fall by half.'
  2. Identify the biggest uncertainties and risks: technical, user, commercial and regulatory. These shape what you build first.
  3. Break the work into thin, end-to-end increments. Each should be usable and deliver something of value, not a layer such as 'the database'.
  4. Order the increments by value and risk. Early increments should either deliver the most value or retire the biggest risk, ideally both.
  5. Choose a fixed cycle length and a release rhythm, and agree when stakeholders will review each cycle.
  6. Agree a definition of done so every increment is genuinely finished: tested, documented and ready to use.
  7. Run the cycle: build, review with real users, refine and update the plan.
  8. Release as soon as an increment is worth releasing, rather than waiting for a big launch. Agile release planning helps you group increments into meaningful releases.
  9. Re-plan after every review. Keep a longer-term roadmap, but treat everything beyond the next few cycles as a forecast.

Ways to slice work into increments

Slicing approachExample (booking system)
By user journey stepFind a slot, then book, then pay, then get reminded
By user groupExisting clients first, then new clients, then clinic managers
By location or segmentOne clinic first, then all clinics
Simple case before complexSingle appointment before repeat course bookings
Happy path before exceptionsSuccessful booking before cancellations and refunds
Manual first, automated laterStaff confirm bookings by hand until automatic confirmation is built

Each slice still crosses every layer needed to work end to end. Writing them as user stories keeps the focus on what the user can do.

Choosing an iteration length

Iteration lengthSuitsWatch out for
1 weekFast-changing products, small teams, support workPlanning and review overhead feel heavy
2 weeksMost product and project teams; a common defaultLarge items that do not fit must be split
3–4 weeksWork with longer lead times, such as hardware or regulated contentSlower feedback; mistakes run longer

Shorter is usually better while requirements are uncertain. Keep the length fixed once chosen so that the team's rhythm and forecasts stay stable.

A short history of iterative and incremental development

Iterative and incremental development is often presented as a product of the Agile movement, but Craig Larman and Victor Basili's paper 'Iterative and Incremental Development: A Brief History' (IEEE Computer, 2003) traces it back to the mid-1950s. Highlights from their account:

  • 1950s: the X-15 hypersonic aircraft programme used iterative and incremental development. It was not a software project, but some of its people later worked on NASA's Project Mercury.
  • Late 1950s to early 1960s: Project Mercury's software team used half-day, time-boxed iterations and wrote tests before each small increment.
  • 1970: Winston Royce's paper, often cited as the origin of waterfall, actually recommended building a pilot version first and doing the job twice.
  • 1970s: IBM's Federal Systems Division built the command and control system for the US Navy's Trident submarine in four roughly six-month iterations, and the LAMPS helicopter system in 45 one-month iterations.
  • 1976 and 1988: Tom Gilb introduced 'evolutionary' delivery in 1976 and set out his Evo method in a 1988 book.
  • 1988: Barry Boehm's spiral model was published in IEEE Computer.
  • 2001: the Agile Manifesto brought these ideas together under one name. One of its principles is to deliver working software frequently, from every couple of weeks to every couple of months, preferring the shorter timescale.

Advantages of iterative and incremental development

AdvantageWhy it happens
Earlier valueUsers can use the first increment months before the full solution is finished
Lower riskBig uncertainties are tested early, in small and cheap experiments
Better fit to real needsEach review lets users correct misunderstandings before they are built on
Visible progressWorking increments are harder to misreport than percent-complete estimates
Easier changeNew information changes the next increment, not a signed-off master plan
Better qualityIntegration and testing happen every cycle rather than in one late phase
Option to stop earlyIf enough value has been delivered, the sponsor can end the project with something useful

The last point is easy to overlook. On a predictive project, stopping halfway usually leaves nothing usable. On an iterative and incremental project, every completed increment works, so the sponsor can stop, pause or change direction at any release with value already in hand.

Disadvantages and risks, and how to manage them

RiskHow to manage it
Scope keeps growing because every review generates new ideasKeep a clear outcome, a prioritised backlog and an agreed budget or end date; see scope creep
Early shortcuts make the design hard to extendInvest in architecture and refactoring in every cycle, not only at the start
Endless refinement of one featureTimebox iterations and agree in advance what good enough means
Fixed-price, fixed-scope contracts sit awkwardly with changing scopeFix time and cost, and make scope the variable; or contract per increment
Stakeholders are not available to reviewBook reviews at the start of the project and make them short and regular
Overhead of repeated planning, testing and releaseAutomate testing and deployment, and keep ceremonies proportionate
Some work cannot be usefully split, such as pouring a foundationUse a hybrid: predictive for indivisible work, iterative for design and fit-out

Treat these as project risks and track them like any other. See our guide to project risk management.

When to use iterative and incremental development

FactorFavours iterative and incrementalFavours a predictive approach
RequirementsUncertain or likely to changeClear, stable and agreed
Can the work be used in parts?YesNo; value only exists when everything is complete
Cost of change late onLow or moderateVery high, as with physical construction
Stakeholder availabilityCan review regularlyAvailable only at set milestones
Regulation and contractsAllow staged approvalRequire full upfront specification
Team experienceFamiliar with short cyclesFamiliar with phased delivery only

A quick test: for each row, mark which column describes your project. If most marks fall on the left, plan iterative and incremental delivery from the start. If most fall on the right, a predictive plan is likely to be simpler, though you may still prototype the riskiest parts. If they are mixed, consider a hybrid that runs each part of the project in the way that suits it.

Most real projects score on both sides, which is why hybrid approaches are common. Our guide to project management methodologies helps you choose and tailor an approach.

Common mistakes

  • Calling it iterative when nothing changes. If reviews never alter the plan, you are running mini-waterfalls.
  • Delivering layers instead of slices. 'Build the database' is not an increment; nobody can use it.
  • Increments that are not really finished. Without a definition of done, unfinished work piles up for later.
  • Reviewing without users. Feedback from the project team alone misses what matters to customers.
  • No long-term direction. Short cycles still need a roadmap and an outcome to aim for.
  • Never releasing. Increments that sit unreleased deliver no value and no real feedback.
  • Changing the iteration length constantly, which makes planning and forecasting unreliable.
  • Ignoring technical debt until it slows every later cycle.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between iterative and incremental development?

Iterative development improves the same thing through repeated cycles of feedback. Incremental development delivers a solution in usable pieces that add up over time. Agile frameworks combine both.

What is an example of iterative development?

Building a prototype checkout page, testing it with users, redesigning it and testing again until it works well. Each cycle improves the same feature.

What is an example of incremental development?

Releasing a booking system in stages: first booking at one clinic, then all clinics, then payments, then reminders. Users can use each piece as soon as it is released.

Is Agile iterative or incremental?

Both. Agile delivers in small usable increments and uses feedback from each cycle to refine the product and the plan. PMI's Agile Practice Guide describes agile life cycles as both iterative and incremental.

Is iterative development the same as Agile?

No. Agile uses iterative and incremental development, but it also rests on values and principles such as customer collaboration, responding to change and self-organising teams. A team can iterate without working in an Agile way, for example by iterating on a design inside a predictive project.

What is a vertical slice?

A vertical slice is an increment that cuts through every layer needed to work end to end, such as screen, logic and data, so users can actually use it. It is the opposite of building one layer, such as the database, for everything first.

Is Scrum iterative or incremental?

Both. Each Sprint of one month or less produces at least one usable Increment, and the Sprint Review and Retrospective feed what is learned into the next Sprint.

Is waterfall iterative or incremental?

Classic waterfall is neither: it completes each phase once and delivers at the end. Some predictive projects add iterations within design phases or deliver in planned stages.

Is an MVP iterative or incremental?

A minimum viable product is the first increment: the smallest usable version that lets you learn from real users. What you do with that learning is iterative.

What is the incremental model in software engineering?

A model in which requirements are divided into builds, and each build is designed, developed and tested before being added to the system. It is incremental, and may be more or less iterative depending on how much each build is revisited.

What is the spiral model?

A risk-driven iterative model described by Barry Boehm and published in IEEE Computer in 1988. Each loop of the spiral sets objectives, analyses and reduces risk, develops and tests, and plans the next loop.

How long should an iteration be?

Usually one to four weeks, with two weeks a common default. Scrum limits Sprints to one month or less. Shorter iterations give faster feedback but add planning overhead.

Can iterative and incremental development be used outside software?

Yes. Training programmes, marketing campaigns, policy rollouts, research and the design stages of construction can all be delivered in usable stages and improved from feedback.

How old is iterative and incremental development?

Larman and Basili (2003) trace it to the mid-1950s, including the X-15 programme and NASA's Project Mercury, long before the Agile Manifesto of 2001.

Can waterfall projects use iterative development?

Yes. Many predictive projects use iterations within design or prototyping, or run iterative parts alongside predictive ones in a hybrid approach.
