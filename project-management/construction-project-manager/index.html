Construction Project Manager: Duties, Skills and Career

A construction project manager plans, coordinates and controls a building or infrastructure project from the first feasibility study to handover, managing cost, programme, quality, contracts and safety on behalf of the client or the contractor. This guide explains what the role involves at each stage, how a typical day runs, the skills and qualifications employers look for, how to get into the job and how it differs from site managers and construction managers.

Key takeaways

  • A construction project manager owns the whole project (brief, design coordination, procurement, construction and handover), not just the site.
  • Duties differ depending on whether you work client-side, for a contractor or for a project management consultancy.
  • The programme, cost plan, risk register and change (variation) register are the four controls you will use every week.
  • Long-lead items, permissions, ground conditions and late design information drive most delays.
  • Employers value site and commercial experience plus professional membership (such as MCIOB or RICS) or certification (such as CCM or PMP).
  • Most people reach the role through site engineering, assistant project manager or quantity surveying posts.

What is a construction project manager?

A construction project manager is the person responsible for delivering a construction project to the agreed scope, cost, time and quality. They turn the client's needs into a brief and budget, coordinate designers and contractors, manage contracts and change, oversee safety arrangements and hand over a finished, compliant building. They lead the whole project, not just the site.

The title covers three slightly different jobs, depending on who employs you:

  • Client-side project manager. Works for the owner or developer (or for a consultancy appointed by them). Manages the brief, the design team, procurement and the contractor, and protects the client's budget and programme.
  • Contractor-side project manager. Works for the main contractor. Delivers the works under the building contract, managing subcontractors, site teams, buying, cost and the contractor's programme, while protecting the contractor's margin.
  • Project management consultant. An independent professional appointed to act as the client's project manager, common on public sector and larger private projects.

If you want the discipline rather than the job (methods, phases and tools for running construction projects), read our guide to construction project management.

What does a construction project manager do?

The core duties are broadly the same on a house extension or a hospital. What changes is the scale, the contract and how much is delegated.

  • Develop the brief and business case with the client, including scope, quality expectations, budget and the target completion date, often supported by a feasibility study.
  • Build and manage the construction programme: the project schedule, usually a Gantt chart with dependencies and the critical path.
  • Coordinate the design team: architects, structural, civil and building services engineers, cost consultants and specialist designers.
  • Run procurement: choose the procurement route and form of contract, prepare tender documents, evaluate bids and appoint contractors (see project procurement management).
  • Secure permissions and approvals: planning or zoning consent, building control or permits, utility connections and third-party agreements.
  • Control cost: cost plans, budgets, monthly valuations, cash flow forecasts and final account.
  • Manage change: log, value and agree variations (change orders) and their effect on time.
  • Oversee health and safety arrangements, making sure legal duty holders are appointed and doing their jobs.
  • Manage risk: keep a risk register and a contingency, and act on early warnings.
  • Report progress, cost and risk to the client or board.
  • Lead handover and close-out: commissioning, snagging, as-built information, operation and maintenance manuals, defects period and lessons learned.

How this maps to the RIBA Plan of Work

In the UK, many teams describe stages using the RIBA Plan of Work 2020: 0 Strategic Definition, 1 Preparation and Briefing, 2 Concept Design, 3 Spatial Coordination, 4 Technical Design, 5 Manufacturing and Construction, 6 Handover and 7 Use. A client-side project manager is usually most involved from stage 0 to stage 6; a contractor-side project manager typically joins at stage 4 or 5, depending on the procurement route.

Client-side vs contractor-side project managers

Job adverts often say "construction project manager" without saying which side of the contract the role sits on. Check, because the day-to-day work is quite different.

Tip

Under many standard contracts, only a named person (for example the contract administrator, employer's agent or, under NEC4, the Project Manager) can issue instructions. Know exactly what authority your appointment gives you before you instruct anything.

A typical day and week

No two days are identical, but most construction project managers settle into a rhythm built around site visits, meetings and paperwork. The example below is for a contractor-side project manager on a mid-sized building.

TimeActivity
07:30Walk the site with the site manager: progress, safety, quality, logistics
08:30Review the look-ahead programme and today's deliveries; chase a late steel delivery
09:30Respond to requests for information (RFIs) and review subcontractor drawings and submittals
11:00Design coordination meeting on the mechanical and electrical services layout
13:30Cost review with the quantity surveyor: valuations, committed costs, forecast final cost
15:00Price a client variation and draft the notification of its time effect
16:30Update the risk register and plan tomorrow's priorities

Across a week, expect these fixed points:

  • Weekly progress meeting with subcontractors, comparing actual progress against the programme.
  • Client or design team meeting, usually fortnightly or monthly on larger projects.
  • Programme update with a two- to six-week look-ahead for the site team.
  • Cost and change review: variations, early warnings and the forecast final cost.
  • Health and safety inspection and review of incidents and near misses.

Worked example: a four-classroom school extension

The figures below are illustrative, but the steps are the ones a client-side project manager would follow.

1. Back-schedule from the fixed date

A council needs four new classrooms open for the start of the school year. The opening date cannot move, so the project manager works backwards:

PhaseDurationComment
Fit-out, commissioning and handover12 weeksIncludes furniture, IT and fire alarm testing
Main construction24 weeksSteel frame, envelope and services
Tender and contract award8 weeksTwo-stage tender to bring the contractor in early
Design and planning permission16 weeksPlanning decision is the main uncertainty
Total60 weeksApproval was given 64 weeks before opening

That leaves only four weeks of float on the whole project. Every activity is on, or very close to, the critical path.

2. Protect the critical path

  1. Submit the planning application as early as the design allows and hold a pre-application meeting with the planning authority.
  2. Bring the contractor in through a two-stage tender so they can advise on buildability and order long-lead items early.
  3. Place the structural steel and electrical switchgear orders as soon as the contract is signed, and track them on a separate long-lead items list.
  4. Schedule noisy and disruptive work (piling, steel erection) for school holidays.
  5. Agree a phased handover: classrooms first, external works can finish during the first weeks of term.

3. Set the budget and contingency

The construction cost plan is £2,400,000. The project manager recommends a client contingency of 10% (£240,000) because the site investigation shows variable ground conditions next to the existing building. The contingency sits in the client's budget, not in the contractor's price, and drawing on it needs the sponsor's approval.

4. Value a variation

During construction the school asks for a covered canopy between the extension and the main building. The project manager handles it like this:

  1. Records the request in the variation register and confirms whether it is essential (it is: it shelters the main route between buildings).
  2. Asks the contractor for a quotation covering both price and time.
  3. The contractor quotes £38,000 and one extra week. The cost consultant checks it against the contract rates: measured work of £31,500 plus one week of site overheads (preliminaries) of £6,000, a total of £37,500.
  4. The project manager agrees £37,500 and confirms the canopy can be built during fit-out without delaying the classrooms, so the one-week extension applies to the external works only.
  5. Issues a formal instruction under the contract and updates the budget: contingency remaining is £240,000 − £37,500 = £202,500, and project float on the classrooms stays at four weeks.
Tip

Track long-lead items on their own list with order date, lead time and required-on-site date. A late lift, switchgear panel or steel order can delay a whole building.

Skills a construction project manager needs

Technical skills

  • Reading drawings, specifications and survey information.
  • Understanding construction methods, sequencing, temporary works and logistics.
  • Building programmes with dependencies, Gantt charts and critical path analysis.
  • Knowing building regulations, codes and the permits your projects need.

Commercial and contract skills

  • Cost plans, valuations, cash flow and forecast final cost.
  • Procurement routes (traditional, design and build, management contracting, two-stage) and standard forms of contract such as JCT and NEC in the UK, or AIA and ConsensusDocs in the US.
  • Valuing variations and assessing delay, and using earned value or similar measures on larger jobs.
  • Negotiation and dispute avoidance.

People and leadership skills

  • Leading teams you do not directly employ: designers, subcontractors and client staff.
  • Clear written records: instructions, meeting minutes and early warnings.
  • Calm decision-making when weather, suppliers or ground conditions upset the plan.
  • Visible health and safety leadership on site.

Soft skills often decide whether a technically strong candidate becomes a good project manager, so build them deliberately, for example by chairing meetings and leading client presentations early in your career.

Qualifications and certifications

Most employers look for a degree or equivalent in construction management, civil or structural engineering, building surveying, quantity surveying or architecture. Experienced site staff without a degree do reach the role, especially on the contractor side, usually with professional membership gained through a work-based route.

Safety training

Site safety training is expected in most countries. Examples include a site management safety course and a CSCS card for site access in the UK, and OSHA 30-hour construction training in the US. These are usually expected rather than optional for anyone who regularly visits site.

How to become a construction project manager

  1. Get a relevant qualification such as a degree, degree apprenticeship or higher technical qualification in construction management, engineering or surveying.
  2. Start in an entry role: site engineer, assistant site manager, trainee quantity surveyor, project coordinator or assistant project manager.
  3. Build site experience. Learn sequencing, temporary works, logistics and how trades interact. Field credibility matters.
  4. Build commercial experience. Spend time on valuations, variations and subcontract packages, even if you start on the technical side.
  5. Take ownership of a package (for example the envelope or the mechanical and electrical services) and then a small project.
  6. Work towards professional membership (for example MCIOB or MRICS) or a certification such as CCM or PMP.
  7. Step up to project manager on a single project, then to senior project manager running larger or multiple projects.

Health, safety and legal duties

Safety duties are set by national law and the project manager must know who holds them on each project.

  • Great Britain: the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 (CDM) place duties on the client, principal designer, designers, principal contractor and contractors. According to HSE, a project must be notified to HSE if construction work will last longer than 30 working days with more than 20 workers on site at the same time, or exceed 500 person-days.
  • England, higher-risk buildings: under the Building Safety Act 2022, higher-risk residential buildings need approval from the Building Safety Regulator before construction starts (Gateway 2) and before occupation (Gateway 3), so build these approvals into the programme.
  • United States: OSHA standards for construction apply, alongside state and local building codes and permits.

Whatever the jurisdiction, the project manager's role is to make sure the right duty holders are appointed early, that safety is considered in design and that site arrangements are checked regularly. Take specialist advice where you are not the legal duty holder.

Construction project manager salary

Pay depends on country, sector, project value and whether you work client-side or for a contractor. For the US, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual wage of $114,990 for construction managers in May 2025, with employment projected to grow 9% from 2025 to 2035. That category includes construction project managers as well as other construction management roles.

In other countries, use professional body salary surveys (for example from the CIOB or RICS), recruitment agency reports and current job adverts for your region and sector. Compare like with like: a project manager on a large infrastructure scheme and one on residential refurbishments can earn very different amounts.

Common mistakes new construction project managers make

  • Trusting the programme over the site. Walk the site often; the programme is only as good as its last update.
  • Weak records. Undocumented instructions and verbal agreements become disputes later. Confirm everything in writing.
  • Late notification. Most contracts set time limits for notifying delays and changes. Miss them and you can lose entitlement.
  • Ignoring long-lead items until the design is complete.
  • Spending contingency quietly. Report every drawdown so the client always knows what is left.
  • Treating handover as an afterthought. Plan commissioning, training and O&M information from the start.
  • Not asking for help. Use experienced site managers and surveyors; they have seen most problems before.

Structured change control solves several of these at once.

Software and tools for construction project managers

Construction project managers usually combine several types of tool:

  • Scheduling software for detailed critical path programmes.
  • Cost and commercial systems for budgets, valuations and variations.
  • Common data environments and document control for drawings, RFIs and submittals.
  • Work management platforms for coordinating design, procurement and client tasks, approvals and reporting across several projects.

For the work management layer, Wrike suits teams that need Gantt charts with dependencies, resource views and approvals across many projects, and Smartsheet suits teams who prefer a spreadsheet-style interface.

Frequently asked questions

What does a construction project manager do?

A construction project manager plans and controls a building or infrastructure project from feasibility to handover. They coordinate designers and contractors and manage cost, programme, quality, contracts, change, risk and safety arrangements.

Is a construction project manager the same as a construction manager?

Not always. In the US the terms are often used interchangeably, and construction management firms may act for the owner. In the UK a construction manager usually runs construction on site, while the project manager oversees the whole project including design, procurement and handover.

Is a construction project manager the same as a site manager?

No. A site manager runs day-to-day work on site: trades, safety, quality and logistics. The project manager oversees the whole project, including design, procurement, budget, contracts and client relations.

What qualifications does a construction project manager need?

Commonly a degree in construction management, engineering, surveying or architecture, or equivalent experience, plus professional membership such as MCIOB or MRICS, or a certification such as CCM or PMP. Site safety training is also expected.

Do you need a degree to become a construction project manager?

A degree is the most common route, but not the only one. Many people progress from site or trade roles, and bodies such as the CIOB offer work-based routes to chartered membership for experienced practitioners without a degree.

Which certification is best for construction project managers?

It depends on your market. In the UK, chartered membership of the CIOB or RICS is widely recognised. In the US, the CCM from CMCI (CMAA) is construction-specific. The PMP is a recognised cross-industry credential that complements either.

Is the PMP useful for a construction project manager?

Yes. It is recognised in many markets and covers planning, risk and stakeholder management. Construction employers often value industry-specific credentials and chartered status as much or more, so many people hold both.

How long does it take to become a construction project manager?

It varies. A common path is a degree followed by several years as a site engineer, assistant project manager or quantity surveyor. Chartered membership routes, for example, typically expect three to five years of relevant experience after a degree.

How much do construction project managers earn?

Pay depends on country, sector and experience. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual wage of $114,990 for construction managers in May 2025. Elsewhere, check professional body surveys and current job adverts.

Is construction project management stressful?

It can be, because of fixed deadlines, cost pressure, weather and many parties to coordinate. Good records, early warnings, realistic programmes and a strong site team reduce the pressure considerably.

How does a construction project manager handle variations?

They log the change, get a quotation for price and time, check it against the contract rates, agree it, issue a formal instruction under the contract and update the budget, contingency and programme.

What is a construction programme?

The construction programme is the project schedule, usually a Gantt chart showing activities, durations, dependencies, milestones and the critical path. The project manager uses it to plan, coordinate and track progress.

What software do construction project managers use?

Scheduling software for programmes, cost and document control systems, and work management platforms such as Wrike or Smartsheet for coordination and reporting. See our project management tools rankings.

Can a construction project manager use Agile?

Mostly in design and pre-construction, through short planning cycles, visual boards and Lean methods such as the Last Planner System. Construction itself remains largely predictive because of contracts and sequencing.
