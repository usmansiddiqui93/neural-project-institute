Project Constraints: Types, Trade-offs and Worked Example

Project constraints are the limits a project must work within, such as a fixed go-live date, a capped budget, a team of a given size or a legal requirement. The best-known model is the triple constraint of scope, time and cost, but most projects also have to respect limits on quality, resources, risk, benefits and regulation. This guide explains each type, shows how to tell a true constraint from a negotiable target, and works through a costed trade-off decision using tolerances.

Key takeaways

  • A constraint is a known limit on how a project can be carried out; an assumption is something treated as true without proof, and the two need different handling.
  • The triple constraint (scope, time and cost, with quality at the centre) is a model of trade-offs, not a complete list of a project's limits.
  • Not everything called a constraint is fixed. Test each one by asking what happens if it is breached, then classify it as fixed, a key objective or flexible.
  • Tolerances turn constraints into decision rules: within tolerance the project manager acts; outside it the sponsor decides.
  • Agile approaches such as DSDM and Scrum usually fix time, cost and quality and let scope flex, but they still respect hard external limits.
  • Trade-offs are rarely linear. Adding people costs money and can slow work at first, so cost every option before recommending one.

What are project constraints?

A project constraint is any limit that restricts how, when or with what a project can deliver its outputs. The PMI Lexicon of Project Management Terms (version 4.0) defines a constraint as "a limiting factor that affects the execution of a portfolio, program, project, or process". Typical examples are a launch date set by a trade show, a budget approved by the investment committee and a rule that patient data must stay in the UK.

Constraints come from many places: the business case, the project charter, contracts, regulators, the organisation's own policies and the physical world. Some are decided by the sponsor and could in principle be renegotiated. Others, such as legislation or the availability of a specialist supplier, are outside the project's control entirely.

Recording constraints matters because every later estimate depends on them. A schedule built without knowing that the warehouse cannot change systems during the peak season will look feasible on paper and fail in practice. Constraints are normally written into the charter and the project scope statement, and they feed directly into risk identification and the project baseline.

The word also has two narrower meanings that this guide does not cover in depth. In the theory of constraints, a constraint is the single bottleneck that limits a system's throughput. In scheduling software, a constraint is a date rule attached to a task, which is discussed in a later section.

The triple constraint and where it came from

The triple constraint, also called the project management triangle or iron triangle, says that scope, time and cost are linked: change one and at least one of the others usually has to move. Quality is often drawn in the centre, because squeezing all three sides at once tends to show up as defects, rework or an exhausted team.

CornerWhat it coversTypical way it is fixed
ScopeThe deliverables and the work needed to produce themScope statement, requirements, contract schedule
TimeDuration, milestones and the delivery dateApproved schedule baseline, external deadline
CostBudget for labour, materials, suppliers and reservesApproved budget and cost baseline
Quality (centre)Fitness for purpose and conformance to requirementsAcceptance criteria, standards, regulations

The Association for Project Management (APM) credits one of its founding members, Dr Martin Barnes, with inventing the triangle, sometimes called the Barnes triangle. His original corners were time, cost and quality. Later versions replaced quality with scope and moved quality to the middle, and a 2018 review of 45 years of literature by Pollack, Helm and Adler found that time and cost are consistently identified as part of the triangle, while the status of quality remains contested.

The triangle is useful for one thing above all: it makes the sponsor face the fact that "more, sooner and cheaper" cannot all be granted at once. It is less useful as a complete description of a project. It says nothing about risk, benefits, people or the rules a project must obey, and it implies trade-offs are smooth and proportional when they rarely are.

Constraints or objectives? A useful distinction

In a 2017 article on the APM website, Paul Naybour argued that time, cost and scope are better described as competing success criteria than as constraints. A sponsor can move a launch date or approve more budget. A sponsor cannot override a ban on heavy demolition work next to a school during exams, or a rule that limits work near a site of special scientific interest. Those are true constraints.

Karl Wiegers makes a similar point with his five project dimensions: features, quality, schedule, cost and staff. On any project, each dimension plays one of three roles:

RoleMeaningHow to recognise it
ConstraintA fixed limit with no flexibilityBreaching it makes the result unusable, illegal or unaffordable
DriverA key objective or success criterion with only a little flexibilityMissing it makes the result less valuable but not worthless
Degree of freedomA dimension the project manager can adjust within agreed limitsAdjusting it within the agreed limits needs no escalation

Wiegers' test is simple: ask what happens if the deadline slips. If the honest answer is that the product becomes useless, the date is a constraint. If it is merely less attractive, it is a driver. His second rule is just as important: all dimensions cannot be constraints, and they cannot all be drivers. A project where scope, date, budget, quality and team size are all fixed has no room to absorb a single surprise.

Running this test with the sponsor at initiation replaces the vague instruction "everything is a priority" with an explicit statement of what will give first.

Types of project constraints

Beyond the triangle, most guidance recognises a wider set. PRINCE2 has long set tolerances for time, cost, scope, quality, benefits and risk, and PRINCE2 7 (2023) added sustainability as a seventh performance target. J. M. Siegelaub's 2007 PMI Global Congress paper argued for agreeing ranges for the same six dimensions rather than relying on the triangle alone.

RiskNo change may put customer orders at risk during trading hoursSponsor, operations director

Organisational policyAll suppliers must come from the approved frameworkProcurement

Internal and external constraints

Internal constraints come from inside the organisation: the budget, the people available, policies, existing systems and the sponsor's priorities. They can be changed by someone senior enough, even if it is uncomfortable. External constraints come from customers, regulators, suppliers, the market, the weather or the law. The project has to plan around them because nobody inside the organisation can change them.

Hard and soft constraints

A hard constraint cannot be breached without the project failing or breaking a rule: a statutory deadline, a safety standard, a contractual completion date with heavy damages. A soft constraint is a strong preference that could move if the case is good enough, such as an internal target date chosen because it fits the financial year. Label each constraint hard or soft in the charter, because the difference decides whether a problem goes to the sponsor as an option or as an exception.

Constraints, assumptions, risks and dependencies

These four terms are often confused, and the confusion causes real problems. An assumption written as if it were a constraint never gets tested; a risk recorded as a constraint never gets a response.

TermDefinitionExampleWhat you do with it
ConstraintA known limit on how the project is carried out (see the PMI definition above)Budget capped at £240,000Plan within it; agree tolerance; escalate breaches
AssumptionA factor in the planning process considered to be true, real or certain without proof or demonstration (PMI)The carrier API will be stable by week 12Give it an owner and a date to confirm it
RiskAn uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, affects objectives (PMI)The API may change during testing, causing reworkAssess it and plan a risk response
DependencyA logical relationship where one activity relies on another (PMI)Label printing cannot start until the API is liveSequence it in the schedule and track it
IssueA current condition that may have an impact on objectives (PMI)The test environment is downResolve it now

The categories move over time. An assumption that proves false often becomes an issue; a dependency on another team becomes a schedule constraint once its date is fixed. Keep assumptions, issues and dependencies in a RAID log and keep constraints in the charter and scope statement, where they are visible to the people who approve the plan. Treat any assumption that underpins a constraint, such as "the budget will be released in April", as a candidate risk for the project risk register.

How to identify and manage project constraints

  1. Collect candidate constraints. Read the business case, charter, contracts, regulations and organisational policies, and interview the sponsor, senior users and suppliers. Ask directly: what must not happen, what is not negotiable, and where did each figure come from?
  2. Test each one. For every candidate, ask who set it, whether it can change, and what happens if it is breached. Wiegers' slip test separates constraints from drivers; anything that fails both tests is a degree of freedom.
  3. Classify and rank. Mark each constraint as hard or soft, internal or external, and agree which dimension gives first. A one-page flexibility table, like the one in the worked example below, is enough.
  4. Record them where decisions are made. Write constraints in measurable form (a date, a sum, a named standard) in the charter and scope statement, with the source of each.
  5. Set tolerances. Agree with the sponsor how far time, cost and other dimensions may deviate before the decision must be escalated, for example plus or minus 5% on cost.
  6. Plan within them. Build the schedule and budget inside the constraints. Use resource leveling when people or equipment are the limit, and check the critical path against any fixed dates.
  7. Monitor against them. Track forecast completion and cost at completion, not only spend to date. Earned value management shows early whether cost or schedule is heading outside tolerance.
  8. Trade off through change control. When a change or problem threatens a constraint, cost the options, recommend one and let the person with the right authority decide through change control.
  9. Revisit at stage boundaries. Constraints change: a budget is cut, a regulator moves a date, a key person leaves. Review the list at every major milestone.
Tip

Write constraints as numbers or named rules. "Limited budget" cannot be tested; "£240,000 including contingency, +5% tolerance" can.

Worked example: costing a trade-off within tolerances

Illustrative example. A distribution company is replacing its warehouse management system. The figures below are invented to show the method; they do not describe a real project.

The baseline and its constraints

  • Time: go-live planned for the end of week 26. The operations director has imposed a change freeze for the peak season from the start of week 29, so go-live must be complete by the end of week 28. Time tolerance: +2 weeks.
  • Cost: budget £240,000. Cost tolerance agreed with the sponsor: +5%, which is £240,000 × 0.05 = £12,000.
  • Resources: four developers. At five days a week and an assumed 80% of time on project work, capacity is 4 × 5 × 0.8 = 16 person-days per week.
  • Run rate: the whole delivery team costs about £5,500 for each week the project runs.
  • Scope: prioritised with MoSCoW. Two Could-have items remain: a custom reporting dashboard (30 person-days) and a redesign of handheld scanner labels (18 person-days).

The sponsor and project manager agreed this flexibility table at initiation:

DimensionRoleLimit or objective
ScheduleConstraint (hard, external to the project)Live before the week 29 freeze
QualityConstraintNightly stock reconciliation with zero unexplained differences
CostDriver£240,000, tolerance +5%
StaffDriverFour developers; contractors only with sponsor approval
ScopeDegree of freedomCould-have items may be deferred to a later release

The change

In week 10 the commercial team asks for integration with a new parcel carrier, estimated at 48 person-days of development. The project manager prepares four options.

Option A: extend the schedule

Extra duration = work ÷ capacity = 48 person-days ÷ 16 person-days per week = 3 weeks. Go-live moves from the end of week 26 to the end of week 29, inside the peak-season freeze. Extra cost = 3 weeks × £5,500 = £16,500, which is 6.9% of the budget and £4,500 above the cost tolerance. Option A breaches a hard constraint and both tolerances.

Option B: add a contractor

A contractor at £550 a day will be less productive on an unfamiliar system. Assuming 80% productivity, delivering 48 person-days of output needs 48 ÷ 0.8 = 60 billable days, costing 60 × £550 = £33,000. That is 13.75% of the budget and 2.75 times the cost tolerance. Sixty days of work fits inside the 16 weeks left before planned go-live, so the date holds, but onboarding will take time from the existing developers. This is the non-linear trade-off the triangle hides: Fred Brooks argued in The Mythical Man-Month (1975) that adding people to a late software project tends to make it later, because newcomers need training and every extra person adds communication overhead.

Option C: defer Could-have scope

Deferring the dashboard (30 person-days) and the label redesign (18 person-days) frees exactly 48 person-days. Time and cost are unchanged and both tolerances stay untouched. The price is in benefits: managers lose the dashboard until a later release.

Option D: combine time tolerance and partial deferral

Using the full two weeks of time tolerance adds 2 × 16 = 32 person-days at a cost of 2 × £5,500 = £11,000. Deferring only the label redesign adds 18 person-days, for 50 person-days in total, enough for the 48 needed. Go-live moves to the end of week 28, just before the freeze. Cost rises by 4.6% of the budget, within tolerance but using 92% of it, and the time tolerance is fully consumed.

OptionTime impactCost impactScope impactWithin tolerance?
A. Extend+3 weeks (into freeze)+£16,500NoneNo: breaches hard date and cost tolerance
B. ContractorNone planned+£33,000NoneNo: 2.75 times cost tolerance
C. Defer Could-havesNoneNoneDashboard and labels deferredYes, with all tolerance kept
D. Hybrid+2 weeks+£11,000Labels deferredYes, but no time reserve and £1,000 of cost tolerance left

Interpreting the result

Options A and B are outside the limits the sponsor delegated, so the project manager cannot choose them alone; in PRINCE2 terms they would be raised as an exception. Option D is permissible but leaves no time reserve and only £1,000 of cost tolerance for the 16 weeks of delivery still ahead, during which data migration and testing are likely to produce surprises. Option C keeps every reserve intact and changes only the dimension both parties agreed was the degree of freedom.

The project manager therefore recommends Option C through the change request, sets out Option D as the fallback if the business values the dashboard more than schedule resilience, and lets the sponsor decide. The flexibility table agreed at initiation did most of the work: the decision rule was settled before the pressure arrived.

The calculation has limits. Capacity of 16 person-days per week, contractor productivity of 80% and a flat weekly run rate are assumptions; real productivity varies, and some costs, such as licences, do not scale with duration. The estimate of 48 person-days is itself uncertain, so a three-point estimate or a PERT range would show how much of the tolerance the change could really consume.

Managing time, cost and resource constraints

Each constraint type has its own set of techniques. Choose the one that matches the dimension under pressure and the dimension you are allowed to flex.

Constraint under pressureTechniquesWhat you usually give up
Fixed dateSchedule compression (fast tracking and crashing), scope deferral, using total floatCost (crashing) or risk (fast tracking)
Fixed budgetValue engineering, phasing, cheaper suppliers, reducing contingency only with sponsor approvalScope, time or risk reserve
Limited resourcesResource leveling or smoothing, critical chain buffers, cross-skillingTime, usually
Fixed quality standardEarlier testing, inspections, definition of done, stronger acceptance criteriaTime or scope
Fixed scopeContingency reserve, schedule buffers, more people early rather than lateTime and cost

Date constraints in scheduling software

Scheduling tools use "constraint" for a date rule on a single task. Microsoft Project, for example, offers eight types: As Soon As Possible, As Late As Possible, Start No Earlier Than, Start No Later Than, Finish No Earlier Than, Finish No Later Than, Must Start On and Must Finish On. The last two pin a task to a date regardless of its predecessors.

Use them sparingly and only for real external dates, such as a regulator's submission window. Every hard date overrides the logic of your network, and when the logic cannot meet the date the schedule shows negative float, which is the amount of time you must recover.

Project constraints in Agile and hybrid projects

Agile approaches do not remove constraints; they choose in advance which one flexes. The DSDM Agile Project Framework, published by the Agile Business Consortium, fixes time, cost and quality at the end of its Foundations phase and manages contingency by varying the features delivered, using MoSCoW priorities. The framework states that quality is never allowed to become a variable.

Scrum applies the same idea at the scale of a Sprint. The 2020 Scrum Guide describes Sprints as fixed-length events of one month or less, and states that during the Sprint no changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal, quality does not decrease, and scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as more is learned.

ApproachUsually fixedUsually flexed
Traditional predictive planScope, from the requirementsTime and cost, through change control
DSDMTime, cost and qualityFeatures (MoSCoW)
Scrum SprintSprint length, quality, Sprint GoalDetailed Sprint scope
Hybrid (common practice)Milestones and budget for governanceBacklog content below the milestone level

Jim Highsmith went further in 2009 with the Agile triangle, which puts value and quality at two corners and collapses scope, schedule and cost into a single "constraints" corner. His argument is that the goal is a valuable, releasable product; staying inside scope, schedule and cost is a boundary, not the measure of success.

Be careful with the slogan "Agile fixes time and cost and flexes scope". It describes how many Agile teams plan, not a rule that applies to every Agile project. A regulated product may have scope that cannot flex at all, and then time or cost must give instead. Our comparison of Agile vs Waterfall explains how each approach handles a change in the triangle.

Common mistakes with project constraints

  • Treating every limit as fixed. If scope, date, budget, quality and team are all non-negotiable, the first problem has nowhere to go except quality or overtime. Agree a degree of freedom at the start.
  • Recording vague constraints. "Tight deadline" and "limited budget" cannot be tested. State dates, sums and named standards, with the source.
  • Confusing constraints with assumptions. "The supplier will deliver in March" is an assumption until it is in a contract. Give it an owner and a confirmation date.
  • Assuming trade-offs are linear. Doubling the team rarely halves the duration, and extending the schedule usually costs money, as Options A and D above show.
  • Letting quality absorb the pressure silently. When scope, time and cost are squeezed together, testing and documentation are the first things cut. Make quality an explicit constraint or driver.
  • Ignoring benefits. A project can meet every time, cost and scope target and still fail if the market has moved. Review the business case at each stage.
  • Using hard date constraints in the schedule for convenience. Pinned tasks hide the real critical path. Reserve them for external dates.
  • Agreeing a trade-off verbally. If a constraint changes, update the baseline and the change log, or the project will be measured against a target nobody still believes in. Unapproved drift is scope creep.

Frequently asked questions

What are project constraints?

Limits a project must work within, such as a deadline, a budget, the people available, quality standards, regulations or technology rules. True constraints are limits nobody on the project can change; targets such as a preferred date can be renegotiated.

What is the triple constraint?

A model saying that scope, time and cost are linked, so changing one usually forces a change in at least one of the others. Quality is often shown in the centre because it suffers when all three are squeezed.

Is the iron triangle the same as the triple constraint?

Broadly, yes. APM credits Dr Martin Barnes with the original time, cost and quality triangle; most modern versions use scope, time and cost with quality in the middle.

What are the six project constraints?

A common list is scope, time, cost, quality, resources and risk. PRINCE2 instead sets tolerances for time, cost, scope, quality, benefits and risk, and PRINCE2 7 adds sustainability as a seventh performance target.

Which project constraint is the most important?

There is no universal answer. Agree it with the sponsor for each project by asking what happens if each limit is breached; the one that makes the result worthless or illegal is the true constraint.

What is the difference between a constraint and an assumption?

A constraint is a known limit you must work within. An assumption is something treated as true without proof, such as a supplier's delivery date, and it should be tested because it may turn out false.

What is the difference between a constraint and a risk?

A constraint exists now and is certain. A risk is an uncertain event that may or may not happen. A constraint can create risks, for example a fixed date raises the risk of rushed testing.

What are internal and external project constraints?

Internal constraints come from the organisation, such as its budget, staff and policies, and can be changed by someone with authority. External constraints come from regulators, customers, suppliers or the environment and must be planned around.

How do Agile projects handle constraints?

Many Agile approaches fix time, cost and quality and let scope flex. DSDM does this explicitly using MoSCoW priorities, and Scrum keeps Sprint length and quality fixed while detailed scope can be renegotiated with the Product Owner.

What is a tolerance in project management?

The permitted deviation from a target before a decision must be escalated, for example +5% on cost or +2 weeks on time. Within tolerance the project manager acts; outside it the sponsor or board decides.

Can project constraints change?

Yes. Budgets are cut, dates move and people leave. Soft constraints can be renegotiated through change control; hard constraints such as laws usually cannot, so the plan must change around them.

Where should project constraints be documented?

In the project charter and the scope statement, written as measurable limits with their source. Tolerances belong with the project manager's authority, and related assumptions go in the RAID log.
