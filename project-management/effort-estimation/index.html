Effort Estimation: Techniques, Formula and Examples

Effort estimation is the process of predicting how much work, measured in person-hours or person-days, a task or project will need. It is the foundation of both the schedule (how long it will take) and the budget (how much it will cost), so getting it roughly right matters more than almost any other planning step. This guide explains the main techniques, the formulas, a step-by-step method and a full worked example from task estimates to duration and cost.

Key takeaways

  • Effort is the amount of work in person-hours or person-days; duration and cost are calculated from it, not the other way round.
  • Choose the technique to match what you know: analogous or top-down early on, bottom-up and three-point once the work is broken down.
  • Three-point (PERT) estimates give a range and a measure of uncertainty, which is more honest than a single number.
  • Convert effort to duration using realistic productive hours and availability, not a full working day.
  • Estimates should get more accurate as the project progresses; state the accuracy range you are working to.
  • Comparing actual effort with estimates gives you a calibration factor that improves every future estimate.

What is effort estimation?

Effort estimation is the process of forecasting the total amount of work needed to complete a task, deliverable or project, usually expressed in person-hours, person-days or person-months. It is based on the scope of the work, the skills of the people doing it and data from similar past work, and it is the main input for the project schedule and labour budget.

In a typical planning sequence, you first define the project scope and break it into a work breakdown structure. You then estimate the effort for each piece of work, and only after that do you build the schedule and budget. Estimating effort before the work is broken down is possible, but the result is a rough order of magnitude rather than a plan.

An effort estimate answers "how much work is this?" It does not, on its own, answer "when will it be finished?" or "how much will it cost?" Those depend on who is available, for how many hours, and at what rate.

Effort vs duration vs cost

Mixing up effort and duration is the most common estimating error. The table shows the difference.

MeasureWhat it meansUnitExample
EffortTotal amount of workPerson-hours or person-days120 person-hours of development
DurationWorking time from start to finishWorking days or weeks10 working days
Elapsed timeCalendar time, including weekends and holidaysCalendar days14 calendar days
CostMoney needed to pay for the effortCurrency120 hours × £55 = £6,600

The relationships are simple:

  • Duration = effort ÷ (number of people × productive hours per person per day)
  • Labour cost = effort × hourly rate (or effort in days × day rate)

Forty person-hours of effort could be one week for one person, half a week for two, or two weeks for one person who is only available half the time. The effort is the same in each case; the duration is not. For the money side, see our guide to cost estimation.

Units of effort

Use the smallest unit that suits the level of detail. State the conventions you use, because "a person-month" means different things in different organisations.

UnitTypical useNote
Person-hourTask-level estimates, time trackingThe most precise and least ambiguous unit
Person-dayWork packages and small projectsState the hours in a day (for example 7.5 or 8)
Person-weekPhases and resource plansUsually 5 person-days
Person-monthLarge projects and early estimatesConventions vary; the COCOMO II cost model, for example, uses 152 hours
Full-time equivalent (FTE)Staffing and capacity plansA rate of effort over time, such as 2.5 FTE for six months
Story pointsAgile teamsRelative size, not time; converted to dates through velocity

To convert effort into staffing, divide it by the hours one full-time person works in the period. For example, 900 person-hours over three months, at about 150 hours per person-month, needs 2 FTE.

Why effort estimation matters

  • Realistic schedules. Durations and the critical path are calculated from effort and availability.
  • Accurate budgets. On most service, IT and knowledge-work projects, labour is the largest cost.
  • Resource planning. Effort shows how many people with which skills you need and when.
  • Go/no-go decisions. An early estimate tells sponsors whether an idea is worth pursuing.
  • Baselines for control. Once approved, effort estimates become part of the project baseline used to measure progress, for example with earned value management.
  • Stakeholder trust. Transparent estimates with stated assumptions make later conversations about change easier.

Effort estimation techniques

There is no single best technique. Most experienced estimators use two or more and compare the results. The table summarises eight common techniques; each is explained below with a short example.

Expert judgmentExperienced people estimate from their knowledgeAny stage; quick checksLow
AnalogousScale the actual effort of a similar past projectEarly, with little detailLow
ParametricMultiply a unit rate by a quantityWork is repetitive and measurableLow to medium

Bottom-upEstimate each task and add them upScope is broken downHigh
Three-point (PERT)Optimistic, most likely and pessimistic valuesTasks are uncertainMedium

Expert judgment

People who have done similar work estimate it directly. A senior developer might say an integration is "about two weeks for one person", which you would record as roughly 60 to 75 person-hours. It is fast but depends on who you ask, so record the assumptions and, where possible, get more than one opinion.

Analogous estimation

You take the actual effort from a similar past project and adjust it for differences. Last year's intranet redesign took 400 person-hours. The new one has about 25% more pages but reuses an existing design system, which you judge saves 10%: 400 × 1.25 × 0.9 = 450 person-hours. Analogous estimates are only as good as the similarity and your records of actual effort.

Parametric estimation

You multiply a measured unit rate by the quantity of work. If your team averages 1.5 hours to write and run a test case and there are 40 test cases, the effort is 40 × 1.5 = 60 person-hours. Parametric estimates are accurate when the rate comes from reliable historical data and the work really is similar each time.

Top-down estimation

You start from a total, often from an analogous estimate or a fixed budget, and divide it across phases using historical proportions. A 1,000-hour project might be split 15% analysis, 45% build, 25% testing and 15% deployment. Top-down is useful for early budgets and for checking whether a fixed budget is realistic, but it can hide missing work.

Bottom-up estimation

You break the work into small tasks or work packages, estimate each one with the people who will do it, and add them up. It is the most accurate approach for a well-defined scope and the most time-consuming. Its main risk is forgetting work that is not on the list, such as reviews, rework and coordination.

Three-point and PERT estimation

For each task you estimate three values: optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P). Two formulas are used:

  • Triangular mean: E = (O + M + P) ÷ 3
  • PERT (beta) mean: E = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6, with standard deviation SD = (P − O) ÷ 6

For a task estimated at O = 24, M = 40 and P = 80 hours, the triangular mean is 48 hours and the PERT mean is 44 hours, with an SD of about 9.3 hours. PERT weights the most likely value more heavily, so it is less pulled by the pessimistic tail. For the network diagram technique that shares the name, see our guide to PERT charts.

Wideband Delphi

A facilitator gives a group of experts the scope, and each estimates anonymously. The facilitator shares the spread of results, the group discusses the reasons for the differences (usually different assumptions), and everyone re-estimates. For example, a first round of 90, 120, 180 and 200 hours might converge to 140 to 170 hours once the group agrees that data migration is in scope. Anonymity reduces anchoring on the most senior voice.

Relative estimation in Agile

Agile teams usually estimate in story points or T-shirt sizes, comparing items with each other rather than with the clock, often using planning poker. Story points become a time forecast through the team's measured velocity. This is covered in more detail below.

Choosing a technique by project stage

Estimates should become more accurate as you learn more. A useful idea here is the cone of uncertainty: Construx, Steve McConnell's consultancy, describes estimates at the initial concept stage as potentially ranging from 0.25 times to 4 times the actual result, narrowing only as decisions remove variability. In other words, the cone does not narrow by itself; it narrows because you make decisions about scope and design.

The PMBOK Guide gives typical accuracy ranges for estimates: a rough order of magnitude (ROM) estimate of −25% to +75%, and a definitive estimate of −5% to +10%. Organisations often set their own ranges for each stage gate. The table is a practical guide based on those ideas.

StageWhat you knowSuitable techniquesTypical accuracy
Idea or business caseA goal and a rough scopeAnalogous, top-down, expert judgmentRough order of magnitude (for example −25% to +75%)
Initiation and planningMain deliverables and a high-level WBSAnalogous plus parametric, Wideband DelphiBudget estimate (narrower range)
Detailed planningWBS down to work packages and tasksBottom-up with three-point estimatesDefinitive (for example −5% to +10%)
ExecutionActual progress and productivityRe-estimate remaining work; forecastingImproves as work completes
Tip

Always quote an estimate with its accuracy range and its key assumptions. "About 900 hours, −10% to +25%, assuming the client supplies content by March" is far more useful, and safer, than "900 hours".

How to estimate effort step by step

The method below works for most projects. On a small piece of work you can do it in an hour; on a large project it may take several workshops.

  1. Define the scope. Agree what is in and out, the deliverables and the acceptance criteria. Unclear scope is the main cause of bad estimates.
  2. Break the work down. Decompose the scope into a WBS until each task is small enough to estimate with confidence; as a rule of thumb, roughly 8 to 80 hours of effort per task.
  3. Define "done" for each task. Include design, review, testing, documentation and handover, so nobody estimates only the "doing" part.
  4. Choose the technique. Use parametric where you have reliable unit rates, three-point where tasks are uncertain, and analogous as a cross-check.
  5. Ask the people who will do the work. They know the hidden steps. The project manager facilitates and challenges; they should not impose numbers.
  6. Use historical data. Compare with actual effort on similar tasks, from your lessons learned records or time tracking.
  7. Record assumptions and risks. Skills, availability, tools, client inputs and anything you have excluded.
  8. Add management effort and contingency once, openly. Include project management and coordination as tasks, and hold contingency as a visible reserve rather than padding every task.
  9. Review, approve and revisit. Have a peer or the team review the estimate, baseline it, and re-estimate remaining work as you learn.

Worked example: bottom-up three-point estimate

A team is building a small online booking feature: a specification, a database change, a payment integration, eight new screens, testing, documentation and coordination. Each task has been estimated by the people who will do it, in person-hours.

TaskOMPPERT meanSD
Requirements workshops and specification16244025.34.0
Database changes20325634.06.0
Payment provider integration24408044.09.3
Eight screens (parametric: 8 × 6 h most likely)36487250.06.0
Testing and fixes24366038.06.0
Documentation and handover8122012.72.0
Coordination, reviews and meetings14203221.03.0
Total142212360225.0about 15

How the totals are calculated

  1. Expected effort. Add the PERT means: 25.3 + 34 + 44 + 50 + 38 + 12.7 + 21 = 225 person-hours.
  2. Combined uncertainty. Standard deviations do not add up directly. Square each SD to get its variance, add the variances (16 + 36 + 87.1 + 36 + 36 + 4 + 9 = 224.1) and take the square root: √224.1 ≈ 15 hours.
  3. Range. Assuming the tasks are independent and the total is roughly normally distributed, there is about a 68% chance the effort falls between 210 and 240 hours (±1 SD), and about a 95% chance it is under 225 + 1.645 × 15 ≈ 250 hours.

Notice that simply adding the most likely values gives 212 hours, 13 hours less than the PERT total. Because the pessimistic values are further from the most likely than the optimistic ones, single-point estimates tend to be biased low. This is one reason projects using only "most likely" estimates often overrun.

Tip

The range calculation assumes tasks are independent. In reality, one shared problem (an unclear requirement, a difficult supplier) can push many tasks late together, so treat the range as optimistic and keep a separate risk reserve. For a more realistic picture on larger projects, use Monte Carlo simulation.

Converting effort to duration and cost

From effort to duration

Duration depends on how many people work on the task and how many productive hours they have each day. Nobody does eight hours of project work in an eight-hour day: meetings, email, support and context switching all take time. Many planners assume about five to six productive hours per person per day, but this is a planning assumption, not a standard; measure it for your own team.

In the example, two developers each have six productive hours a day, giving 12 hours a day between them:

  • Expected duration = 225 ÷ 12 = 18.75, so about 19 working days.
  • At the 95% figure, 250 ÷ 12 ≈ 21 working days.
  • Allow for dependencies: the specification must be finished before most of the build starts, so the real schedule is usually longer than effort ÷ people suggests. Sequence the tasks in a project schedule to find the true duration.

Adding people does not shorten duration in proportion. Fred Brooks observed in The Mythical Man-Month (1975) that adding people to a late software project makes it later, because new people need onboarding and every extra person adds communication. Treat extra people as extra effort for coordination, not just extra capacity.

From effort to cost

At a blended rate of £55 per hour:

BasisEffort (hours)Cost
Expected (PERT mean)225£12,375
About 95% confidence250£13,750
Contingency (difference)25£1,375

A common approach is to budget the expected cost for the planned work and hold the difference as a visible contingency reserve, controlled by the project manager.

Effort estimation in Agile projects

Agile teams usually avoid estimating every task in hours up front. Instead, they size backlog items relative to each other in story points and let the team's measured velocity convert size into a forecast. This has three advantages: relative sizing is quicker, it is less affected by who does the work, and velocity automatically absorbs meetings, interruptions and other overheads.

  • Sizing: the team compares a new item with items it has already completed and gives it a point value, often using planning poker so everyone estimates independently before discussing.
  • Forecasting: if the team completes 30 to 36 points per sprint and the release backlog is 180 points, it needs roughly 5 to 6 sprints.
  • Hours still help for sprint-level task planning on some teams, for fixed-price contracts and when a budget must be expressed in money. Convert using the team's cost per sprint rather than an assumed hours-per-point ratio.

Common effort estimation mistakes

  • Confusing effort with duration and promising a date from an effort figure.
  • Assuming a full productive day of seven or eight hours per person.
  • Estimating only the build, and forgetting analysis, review, testing, documentation, deployment and handover.
  • Padding every task instead of holding one visible contingency, which hides risk and tends to be used up (Parkinson's law).
  • Giving a single number without a range or assumptions, which is then treated as a commitment.
  • Letting the manager estimate for the team, or anchoring the team with a number before they estimate.
  • Ignoring history. Not checking how long similar work actually took.
  • Never re-estimating, even after the scope or team changes.
  • Adding people to recover a late task without accounting for onboarding and coordination effort.

How to improve your estimates over time

The single most useful improvement is to track actual effort and compare it with estimates. Over time, this gives you a calibration factor for your team and type of work.

Worked example: calibration ratio

Over the last ten completed tasks, a team estimated a total of 180 person-hours and actually spent 225. The ratio of actual to estimate is 225 ÷ 180 = 1.25. Until the ratio improves, the project manager multiplies new bottom-up estimates by 1.25 as a cross-check, and the team discusses which kinds of tasks are underestimated most, usually testing and integration.

  • Keep a simple estimating database: task type, size, estimate, actual and notes.
  • Hold short estimation retrospectives at milestones: which estimates were furthest out, and why?
  • Use more than one technique on important estimates, and investigate big differences.
  • Track variances during the project with tools such as earned value and estimate at completion, so you learn early, not at the end.
  • Record assumptions with every estimate so you can see which ones proved wrong.

Frequently asked questions

What is effort estimation in project management?

Predicting the total amount of work, in person-hours, person-days or person-months, needed to complete a task or project. It is the basis for the schedule and the labour budget.

What is the formula for effort estimation?

There is no single formula. Common ones are parametric (effort = quantity × unit rate) and PERT (expected effort = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6). Duration = effort ÷ (people × productive hours per day), and cost = effort × rate.

What is the difference between effort and duration?

Effort is the amount of work; duration is how long it takes in working time. Forty hours of effort is one week for one full-time person or half a week for two.

What are the main types of estimating?

Analogous, parametric, bottom-up and three-point are the four most commonly cited, alongside expert judgment, top-down, Wideband Delphi and Agile relative estimation.

How do you calculate person-hours?

Multiply the number of people by the hours each works on the task. Three people working 20 hours each is 60 person-hours. To estimate, add the person-hours of every task in the work breakdown structure.

How many productive hours a day should I assume?

It varies by organisation and role. Many planners assume around five to six productive hours per person per day rather than the full working day, but you should measure your own team's actual figure.

What is the difference between three-point and PERT estimation?

Both use optimistic, most likely and pessimistic values. The simple three-point (triangular) mean is (O + M + P) ÷ 3; PERT weights the most likely value four times: (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6.

What is Wideband Delphi?

A group technique in which experts estimate anonymously, discuss the reasons for differences and re-estimate in rounds until the estimates converge.

How accurate should an effort estimate be?

It depends on the stage. The PMBOK Guide gives −25% to +75% for a rough order of magnitude estimate and −5% to +10% for a definitive estimate. Always state the range you are working to.

What is FTE in effort estimation?

Full-time equivalent expresses effort as a number of full-time people over a period. 900 person-hours over three months, at about 150 hours per person-month, is 2 FTE.

Who should estimate effort?

The people who will do the work, facilitated by the project manager and checked against historical data. Estimates imposed from above are usually optimistic and weakly owned.

Do Agile teams estimate effort in hours?

Usually not at backlog level. They size items in story points and use velocity to forecast. Some teams still break sprint items into tasks estimated in hours.

Why do effort estimates go wrong?

Unclear scope, missing tasks, optimism, pressure to hit a target, interruptions, multitasking and changes that are not re-estimated are the usual causes.
