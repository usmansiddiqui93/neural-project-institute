Fishbone Diagram: Steps, Categories and Examples

A fishbone diagram, also called an Ishikawa or cause-and-effect diagram, is a visual tool that helps a team list and organise the possible causes of a problem so it can find the root causes rather than the first obvious one. This guide explains the categories to use, an eight-step method, two worked project examples, a workshop agenda and how to confirm causes with data before you act.

Key takeaways

  • A fishbone diagram puts the problem at the head and groups possible causes on bones by category, such as people, methods, tools and inputs.
  • Start with a specific, measurable problem statement; a vague or solution-shaped statement produces a vague diagram.
  • Ask "why does this happen?" repeatedly to move from symptoms to causes you can actually change.
  • Everything on the diagram is a hypothesis until data supports it: prioritise, then check with a check sheet or Pareto chart.
  • Choose categories that fit the work. The 6Ms suit manufacturing; projects and services often need categories such as requirements, communication and suppliers.
  • Finish with owned, dated actions in your issue log or action plan, and measure whether the problem actually improves.

What is a fishbone diagram?

A fishbone diagram is a cause-and-effect diagram that shows the possible causes of a single problem, grouped into categories. The problem is written at the "head" of the fish, a horizontal line forms the spine, and each major bone represents a category of causes, with smaller bones for more detailed sub-causes. Its purpose is to help a team explore causes widely and systematically before deciding what to fix.

PartWhat it holdsExample
HeadThe problem or effect, stated specificallyUser-reported defects per release have more than doubled
SpineThe line linking causes to the effect
Major bonesCategories of causesPeople, process, tools, requirements
Minor bonesIndividual causes within a categoryAcceptance criteria are unclear
Sub-bonesDeeper causes found by asking whyProduct owner has no time to review stories

Other names for a fishbone diagram

  • Ishikawa diagram, after Kaoru Ishikawa, the Japanese quality expert who popularised it.
  • Cause-and-effect diagram, the descriptive name used in many quality standards and textbooks.
  • Herringbone diagram, another reference to its shape.

A short history

Ishikawa popularised the diagram in the 1960s while developing quality management practices, including at the Kawasaki shipyards, and included it in his book *Guide to Quality Control*, first published in Japan in 1968. It became one of the seven basic quality tools, alongside the check sheet, control chart, histogram, Pareto chart, scatter diagram and stratification (some lists include a flowchart instead). Today it is used well beyond manufacturing, in software, healthcare, services, construction and project management.

When to use a fishbone diagram (and when not to)

A fishbone diagram is most useful when a problem keeps coming back and nobody is sure why. Typical project situations include:

  • A recurring defect, delay or complaint, such as late deliverables or repeated rework.
  • Several entries in the issue log that seem to share a cause.
  • A post-incident review after an outage, a failed release or a missed milestone.
  • A lessons learned session at a phase end or at project closure.
  • A team that disagrees about the cause and needs to see all the possibilities before collecting data.
  • Risk identification, when you want to ask "what could cause this threat to happen?"

It is less useful in some cases:

  • The cause is already known and proven. Go straight to fixing it.
  • The problem is very simple. A quick 5 whys conversation may be enough.
  • You need to rank causes by frequency or cost. Use a Pareto chart, ideally after the fishbone has suggested what to count.
  • There are several unrelated problems. Draw one diagram per problem, not one diagram for everything.
Tip

A fishbone diagram generates hypotheses; it does not prove them. Plan time after the session to check the most likely causes with data.

Fishbone diagram categories

The categories (the major bones) are prompts that stop the team focusing on one area. Pick a set that suits your work, rename bones freely and use four to seven of them. Too few and you miss areas; too many and the diagram becomes hard to read.

The 6Ms (manufacturing and operations)

CategoryPromptsExample causes
Man / peopleSkills, training, workload, handovers, staffingNew operators not trained on the changeover
MachineEquipment, software, maintenance, capacityCalibration overdue on a filling machine
MethodProcedures, work instructions, sequenceTwo shifts follow different set-up steps
MaterialRaw materials, parts, information receivedNew supplier's packaging film is thinner
MeasurementGauges, inspection, data accuracyWeight check uses an unverified scale
Mother nature / environmentTemperature, humidity, location, cultureLine runs hotter in summer

Some teams add management and money (budget, priorities, investment), or maintenance, giving 7Ms or 8Ms. Many people now use neutral terms such as "people" and "environment" rather than "man" and "mother nature".

The 4Ss and 8Ps (services and marketing)

Service and marketing teams often prefer categories that fit their work:

  • 4Ss: surroundings, suppliers, systems and skills.
  • 8Ps: product (or service), price, place, promotion, people, process, physical evidence and performance (sometimes productivity and quality).

Categories for project teams

Project problems often come from requirements, communication, planning and suppliers rather than machines and materials. These sets work well as a starting point:

Project typeSuggested categories
Software and ITPeople, process, tools and environments, requirements, code and architecture, measurement, suppliers
ConstructionLabour, plant and equipment, materials, design, methods and sequencing, site conditions, subcontractors
Marketing and creativeBrief, people, process and approvals, tools, data, channels, external factors
EventsVenue, suppliers, people, planning and timeline, communication, budget, external factors
General projectsPeople, process, tools, inputs and information, planning, communication, management, environment

If a problem relates to scope, for example repeated unplanned work, consider bones such as requirements, change control and stakeholder expectations. Our guide to scope creep lists common causes worth prompting for.

How to make a fishbone diagram in 8 steps

You need a whiteboard, sticky notes or a digital whiteboard, the right people and about 60 to 90 minutes. The steps below follow the widely used procedure described by quality bodies such as ASQ, with our additions on prioritising and validation.

  1. Agree the problem statement. Write a specific, measurable description of the effect in the head of the fish, such as "User-reported defects rose from an average of 6 to 14 per release over the last four releases". See the next section for how to write a good one.
  2. Draw the spine. Draw a horizontal arrow pointing to the head.
  3. Add the major bones. Choose four to seven categories and draw them as diagonal lines off the spine, each labelled.
  4. Brainstorm causes. Ask "why does this happen?" and place each idea on the bone it fits best. A cause can appear on more than one bone if it genuinely fits several. Have people write ideas silently first so everyone contributes.
  5. Ask why again. For each cause, ask why it happens and add the answers as smaller bones. Stop when you reach something the team can act on or measure. This is where the 5 whys technique fits in.
  6. Fill the gaps. Look for bones with few ideas and prompt for them deliberately, for example "what about measurement?" Thin bones often hide causes the group has not thought about.
  7. Prioritise likely root causes. Use multi-voting: give each person three votes to place on the causes they think contribute most. Circle the top three to five.
  8. Validate and act. Check the top causes with data, then agree actions with owners and dates and record them in your issue log, RAID log or an action plan. Measure the problem again after the actions to confirm it improved.
Tip

Keep the diagram visible and dated. Taking a photo or exporting the digital board and attaching it to the issue gives you a record of the reasoning for later reviews.

How to write a good problem statement

The problem statement drives everything else. A good one describes the effect, not a cause or a solution, and includes a measure, a scope and a time frame.

Weak statementWhy it is weakBetter statement
"Testing is rubbish"Blames a team and assumes the cause"User-reported defects rose from 6 to 14 per release between releases 4 and 8"
"We need a new reporting tool"A solution in disguise"Monthly client reports are delivered an average of four working days late"
"Communication problems"Too vague to analyse"In Q3, 9 of 12 design changes reached the site team after work had started"
"The project is over budget"Too broad; many separate problems"Subcontractor variations added 8% to phase 2 costs against the baseline"

If you are not sure how to phrase it, ask: what is happening, where, since when, how often and compared with what? Our free problem statement template walks through these questions.

Worked example: rising release defects on a software project

A product team ships a release every two weeks. Over the last four releases, defects reported by users have risen from an average of 6 per release to 14, and the support team is struggling. The project manager runs a 75-minute fishbone session with eight people: developers, testers, the product owner, a support analyst and the release manager.

Step 1: the diagram

Head: "User-reported defects rose from an average of 6 to 14 per release over releases 5 to 8." The team chose seven bones. The main causes and sub-causes were:

BoneCausesSub-causes found by asking why
PeopleTwo new developers joined; testers stretched across two teamsNo onboarding guide for the legacy billing module
ProcessCode reviews skipped near release dayRelease date fixed regardless of readiness
RequirementsAcceptance criteria unclear or missingProduct owner has little time to refine stories
Tools and environmentsTest environment differs from productionConfiguration copied manually
Code and architectureLegacy billing module has few automated testsRegression tests never written when module was built
MeasurementDefects not tagged by component or causeSupport tool has no cause field
ManagementRelease frequency doubled without extra test capacityDecision made to hit a marketing date

Step 2: prioritise

Each of the eight participants placed three votes (24 in total):

CauseVotes
Unclear acceptance criteria7
Few automated regression tests on billing module6
Test environment differs from production4
Code reviews skipped near release day3
New developers not onboarded on billing module2
Release frequency doubled without extra test capacity2

Step 3: check with data

Because defects were not tagged by cause, a tester and the support analyst reviewed all 56 user-reported defects from releases 5 to 8 and classified each one. The result, shown as a Pareto table:

CauseDefectsShareCumulative
Unclear or missing acceptance criteria2137.5%37.5%
No regression tests on billing module1628.6%66.1%
Environment configuration differences916.1%82.1%
Code review skipped610.7%92.9%
Other47.1%100%
Total56100%

The data broadly confirmed the vote, and showed that the top two causes explained about two thirds of the defects. It also gave the team a baseline to measure against.

Step 4: actions

ActionRoot cause addressedOwnerDue
Add a definition of ready: no story enters a sprint without testable acceptance criteriaUnclear acceptance criteriaProduct ownerNext sprint
Write regression tests for the 20 most-used billing scenariosNo regression testsLead developerWithin 3 sprints
Automate environment configuration from production settingsEnvironment differencesRelease managerWithin 2 sprints
Add a cause field to the support tool and tag every defectMeasurement gapSupport analystThis week

The team tracked user-reported defects per release on its dashboard and in the project status report, and agreed to repeat the Pareto check after four more releases to confirm whether the actions worked. Note how the fishbone widened the discussion (the measurement gap would otherwise have been missed), while the data narrowed it to the causes that mattered most.

Worked example 2: late monthly client reports

A shorter example from a service project. Problem: "Monthly client reports are delivered an average of four working days late." The team's diagram included these causes:

BoneCauses found
PeopleOne analyst produces all reports; no cover during leave
MethodsNo agreed cut-off for data; reviews done one after another rather than in parallel
ToolsReport template rebuilt by hand each month
InputsFinance data arrives on working day 5 instead of day 2
MeasurementNobody tracks how long each step takes
ManagementReport deadline not shown in team planning

Two months of timing data showed that late finance data and manual template work explained most of the delay. The team agreed a day-2 data cut-off with finance and automated the template, and trained a second analyst as cover. The remaining causes were logged as smaller improvements.

How to read a fishbone diagram

If someone hands you a finished diagram, read it in this order:

  1. Start at the head. Make sure you understand the problem, its measure and its time frame.
  2. Scan the major bones. See which categories were considered and whether any obvious area is missing.
  3. Look at the depth. Causes with several levels of sub-bones have been explored; single words on a bone may be symptoms rather than causes.
  4. Look for repeats. A cause that appears on several bones, or that sits behind several others, is often a strong root-cause candidate.
  5. Check what is marked. Circled or voted causes show what the team thinks matters most; check whether data supports them.

A dense bone shows where the team had most to say, which is not the same as where the biggest cause lies. Treat the shape of the diagram as a guide to discussion, not as evidence.

Running a fishbone workshop: agenda and facilitation tips

A fishbone session works best with five to ten people who are close to the work, plus at least one person from outside the immediate team for a fresh view. This 75-minute agenda works for most project problems:

TimeActivityFacilitator notes
0–10 minPurpose, ground rules and problem statementAgree the wording of the head; remind people it is about the system, not individuals
10–15 minAgree categoriesPropose a set and let the group rename or add bones
15–35 minSilent brainstorming, then place notes on bonesOne idea per note; everyone writes before anyone speaks
35–55 minAsk why on each cause and fill thin bonesPush for causes the team can act on; prompt empty categories
55–65 minMulti-vote on likely root causesThree votes each, placed at the same time
65–75 minAgree how to check top causes and who will do itAssign owners and a date to report back with data
  • Keep it blame-free. "Why did the process allow this?" gets better answers than "who did this?"
  • Separate facts from opinions. Mark causes backed by data differently, for example with a tick.
  • Draw out quieter voices. Silent writing and round-robin sharing stop a few people dominating. These are the same techniques that improve expert judgment.
  • Park solutions. People will suggest fixes early; capture them on a separate "ideas" area for later.
  • Run it remotely if needed. Digital whiteboards with a fishbone template work well; ask people to add notes before the call to save time.

Fishbone diagram vs 5 whys, Pareto and other tools

The fishbone diagram is one of several root-cause and quality tools. They work best together.

ToolWhat it doesStrengthUse it when
Fishbone diagramMaps many possible causes of one problem by categoryBreadth; involves the whole teamThe cause is unclear and many factors may contribute
5 whysAsks why repeatedly down one chain of causeDepth; quick and simpleThe problem is fairly simple, or to drill into a cause on the fishbone
Pareto chartRanks causes by frequency, cost or impactFocus on the vital fewYou have data, or the fishbone has suggested what to count
Check sheetA simple form for counting occurrencesEasy data collectionValidating causes from the fishbone
Scatter diagramTests whether two variables are relatedShows correlationChecking whether a suspected cause moves with the problem
FMEARates potential failure modes by severity, occurrence and detectionPrevention before problems happenDesigning a new process or product
Fault tree analysisLogic diagram of events that combine to cause a failureRigour for safety-critical systemsComplex technical failures with AND/OR logic

A common sequence is: fishbone to list possible causes, 5 whys to dig into the most likely ones, a check sheet to collect data, and a Pareto chart to decide where to act. Our root cause analysis template gives you a structured 5 whys record to use alongside your fishbone diagram.

Fishbone diagrams in Lean, Six Sigma and quality management

In Six Sigma, the fishbone diagram is commonly used in the Analyse phase of DMAIC to list potential causes (often called Xs) that might drive the problem (the Y). The most likely causes are then tested statistically, for example with hypothesis tests or regression.

In Lean project management, fishbone diagrams support problem-solving on the shop floor and in A3 reports, often combined with the 5 whys. The emphasis is on going to where the work happens and seeing the problem directly before drawing the diagram.

In project quality management, PMI and other bodies list cause-and-effect diagrams among the data representation and quality tools used to find the sources of defects and non-conformance. Use it when quality control finds a pattern of problems, then feed the agreed corrective actions back into the quality management plan.

Using fishbone diagrams for project risks and retrospectives

Risk identification

Turn the diagram around: put a potential threat at the head, such as "Go-live slips past the contract date", and ask what could cause it. Each bone then produces risk causes that you can write in cause-event-effect form and add to the risk register. This often finds risks that a simple checklist misses, such as dependencies on another team's release calendar.

Retrospectives and lessons learned

When a retrospective keeps surfacing the same problem, a fishbone diagram is a good way to break the cycle. Spend one sprint retrospective on it: agree the problem, draw the bones, vote, then pick one or two improvement actions for the next sprint. Our sprint retrospective template has space to record them. At phase end or closure, use it to explain the biggest variances and record findings in a lessons learned template.

Advantages and limitations of fishbone diagrams

AdvantagesLimitations
Simple to learn and needs no softwareCan become cluttered when the problem is too broad
Encourages the team to look beyond the first explanationShows possible causes, not proven ones
Involves people who do the work, which builds ownership of fixesDoes not show how causes interact or which matters most
Gives structure to brainstorming through categoriesQuality depends on who is in the room
Creates a visual record of the reasoningCan drift into blame if not well facilitated
Works with other tools such as the 5 whys and Pareto chartsLess suited to complex, interacting technical failures than fault tree analysis

Most limitations are handled by a tight problem statement, good facilitation and a data check before action.

Fishbone diagram templates and tools

You do not need special software. Options include:

  • A whiteboard or flip chart with sticky notes, which is often best for in-person sessions.
  • Digital whiteboards, many of which include ready-made fishbone templates for remote teams.
  • Diagramming and presentation tools, useful for a tidy final version to share.
  • Spreadsheets: a simple table with columns for category, cause, sub-cause, votes, evidence and action works well for tracking, even if you draw the picture elsewhere.

Common fishbone diagram mistakes

  • A vague or solution-shaped problem statement, such as "we need more testers".
  • Stopping at symptoms, such as "bugs" or "delays", instead of asking why until you reach something actionable.
  • Blaming people instead of looking at the process, tools and conditions that let the problem happen.
  • Only managers in the room, missing the knowledge of people who do the work.
  • Treating the diagram as the answer and acting on causes without checking the data.
  • Trying to fix everything instead of focusing on the few causes that explain most of the problem.
  • No follow-up: actions without owners, dates or a measure of whether the problem improved.

Frequently asked questions

What is a fishbone diagram used for?

It is used to identify and organise the possible causes of a specific problem, such as recurring defects, delays or complaints, so a team can find and fix root causes rather than symptoms.

Why is it called an Ishikawa diagram?

It is named after Kaoru Ishikawa, the Japanese quality expert who popularised it in the 1960s. It is called a fishbone diagram because the finished drawing looks like a fish skeleton.

What are the 6Ms of a fishbone diagram?

Man (people), machine, method, material, measurement and mother nature (environment). Some teams add management, money or maintenance.

What categories should I use for a project?

Common project categories are people, process, tools, inputs and information, requirements, communication, planning, suppliers, management and environment. Pick four to seven that fit the problem.

How do you make a fishbone diagram?

Agree a specific problem statement, draw the spine and head, add four to seven category bones, brainstorm causes, ask why to add sub-causes, fill thin bones, vote on likely root causes, then check them with data and agree actions.

What is the difference between a fishbone diagram and the 5 whys?

A fishbone diagram maps many possible causes across categories, giving breadth. The 5 whys follows one chain of cause downwards, giving depth. They are often used together.

Is a fishbone diagram one of the seven basic quality tools?

Yes. The cause-and-effect diagram is one of the seven basic quality tools, alongside the check sheet, control chart, histogram, Pareto chart, scatter diagram and stratification (or flowchart in some lists).

How many causes should a fishbone diagram have?

There is no fixed number. A typical session produces 20 to 50 ideas. What matters is reaching actionable causes and then focusing on the three to five that seem most important.

Who should take part in a fishbone session?

Five to ten people who are close to the work, including those who experience the problem, plus a facilitator and ideally one person from outside the team for a fresh perspective.

How long does a fishbone analysis take?

The workshop usually takes 60 to 90 minutes. Allow extra time afterwards, often a few days, to collect data that confirms or rules out the main causes.

Can a fishbone diagram be used for project risks?

Yes. Put a potential threat at the head and ask what could cause it. Each cause can then be written as a risk and added to the risk register.

Can I make a fishbone diagram in Excel or Word?

Yes. You can draw one with shapes and connectors, or keep a simple table of category, cause, sub-cause, votes and evidence. Many diagramming and whiteboard tools also offer ready-made templates.

What are the disadvantages of a fishbone diagram?

It shows possible rather than proven causes, can become cluttered for broad problems, does not show how causes interact and depends on who takes part. Validating with data and good facilitation address most of these.

Is a fishbone diagram used in Six Sigma?

Yes. It is commonly used in the Analyse phase of DMAIC to list potential causes, which are then tested with data.
