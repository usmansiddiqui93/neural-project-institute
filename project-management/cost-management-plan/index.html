Cost Management Plan: What to Include, Example and Template

A cost management plan sets out how a project's costs will be estimated, budgeted, recorded, measured and controlled, and who can make which financial decisions. It is part of the project management plan and is agreed before the budget is set. This guide explains what to include, gives a 12-section template outline and shows a complete example for a $1.2 million system rollout.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team

  • Last updated 25 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • The cost management plan sets the rules for money on the project; the budget and cost baseline are the numbers produced by following those rules.
  • PMBOK 6 lists the core contents: units of measure, precision, accuracy, links to organisational procedures, control thresholds, rules of performance measurement and reporting formats.
  • Write down financial authority: who can spend, who can release contingency and who can approve baseline changes, with money limits.
  • Set thresholds with a ratio (such as CPI) and a money floor, so small packages do not cause noise and large ones do not drift unnoticed.
  • Choose earned value rules of credit by type of work, and avoid subjective percent complete.
  • Scale the plan to the project: two pages can be enough for a small internal project.

What is a cost management plan?

A cost management plan is the part of the project management plan that describes how project costs will be planned, structured, estimated, budgeted, recorded and controlled. It defines units, precision, thresholds, earned value rules, reporting, reserves and approval authority, so everyone handles project money the same way.

In the PMBOK Guide 6th edition, the plan is the output of the Plan Cost Management process, the first of the four project cost management processes. The 8th edition, published in November 2025, moves cost into a Finance performance domain whose first process is called Plan Financial Management. The name has changed, but the job is the same: agree the rules before you produce the numbers.

A good plan answers a handful of practical questions:

  • In what currency and units will we estimate and report, and how precisely?
  • How will costs be structured so actuals from the finance system match the work breakdown?
  • How will we estimate, and how accurate must the baseline estimate be?
  • How will progress be measured, and how will we calculate earned value?
  • What size of variance triggers an explanation, and what triggers escalation?
  • Who can approve spending, release contingency or change the budget?
  • What reports will be produced, for whom and how often?

Cost management plan vs budget, baseline and estimate

These four terms are often used interchangeably, but they are different things produced at different points.

ItemWhat it isProduced inChanges how?
Cost management planThe rules and procedures for managing project costsPlan cost managementUpdated when the approach changes, with sponsor agreement
Cost estimateA prediction of what each piece of work will cost, with a rangeEstimate costsRefined as the project is better understood
Cost baselineApproved, time-phased work package estimates plus contingency reserveDetermine budgetOnly through formal change control
Project budgetCost baseline plus management reserveDetermine budgetOnly through formal change control

A simple way to remember it: the plan is the rulebook, the estimate is the prediction, the baseline is the commitment you measure against, and the budget is all the money set aside for the project.

Why you need a cost management plan

  • It prevents arguments later. If the threshold for escalation is agreed in advance, a CPI of 0.92 triggers a known response instead of a debate.
  • It makes reports comparable. When everyone uses the same units, rounding and earned value rules, figures can be rolled up across work packages and projects.
  • It connects the project to finance. Mapping work packages to cost codes means actual costs land in the right place without manual re-coding.
  • It controls reserves. Clear rules on who can release contingency and management reserve stop reserves being spent quietly or not at all.
  • It supports audits and contracts. Clients, funders and auditors often expect to see how costs are controlled, not just what was spent.

Even on small projects, a one- or two-page version that covers units, thresholds, authority and reporting avoids most of the common disputes.

What to include: the PMBOK components

The PMBOK Guide 6th edition lists the typical contents of a cost management plan. The table translates each formal term into plain English with an example entry.

ComponentWhat it meansExample entry
Units of measureThe currency and the units for effort and quantitiesUS dollars; effort in person-hours; materials in units
Level of precisionHow far estimates are roundedWork packages to the nearest $100; reports to the nearest $1,000
Level of accuracyThe acceptable range for estimates, which may include contingencyBaseline estimate within -5% to +10%
Organisational procedures linksHow the WBS maps to control accounts and finance cost codesControl accounts at WBS level 2; code format PRJ-CA-WP
Control thresholdsVariances that trigger analysis or escalationCumulative CPI below 0.95 needs a written explanation
Rules of performance measurementEarned value methods, how progress is credited and the EAC formulas to useTasks under one month use 0/100; longer tasks use weighted milestones
Reporting formatsWhat cost reports are produced, for whom and how oftenMonthly EVM report to the steering group

Most organisations add three more sections that PMBOK treats as part of the wider plan: roles and financial authority, reserve management and cost change control. Without them the plan describes how to measure costs but not who acts on the results.

Tip

A control account is the management point where scope, budget, actual cost and schedule are brought together and compared with earned value. Each control account has one manager and contains one or more work packages.

Earned value rules of credit

The rules of performance measurement decide how much earned value a task earns as it progresses. Choosing them well is one of the most useful things the plan does, because overstated progress makes every cost measure look better than it is.

MethodHow earned value is creditedBest for
0/100Nothing until complete, then 100%Short tasks, within one reporting period
50/5050% at start, 50% at finishTasks spanning two reporting periods
25/75 or 20/80A small share at start, the rest at finishTasks over two periods where a cautious view is wanted
Weighted milestonesA set share at each objective milestoneLonger tasks with clear interim outputs, such as design drafts
Physical percent completeMeasured quantity done, such as metres laid or units installedRepetitive, measurable work
Level of effortEarned in line with time passingSupport work with no discrete output, such as project management
Apportioned effortA fixed share of a related task's earned valueWork tied to other work, such as quality inspection

Avoid subjective percent complete ("about 70% done") for anything significant. Tasks reported at 90% for weeks are a familiar sign that progress is being overstated. Our earned value management guide explains how earned value feeds the cost and schedule measures.

How to write a cost management plan step by step

  1. Check organisational requirements. Find the PMO or finance policies on reserves, capitalisation, approval limits and reporting. Your plan should apply them, not reinvent them.
  2. Agree units, precision and accuracy. Decide the currency, effort units and rounding, and the accuracy expected of the baseline estimate.
  3. Structure the costs. Using the work breakdown structure, set control accounts and agree cost codes with finance.
  4. Choose the estimating approach. Say which cost estimation methods will be used at which stage, which rates apply and how the basis of estimate is documented.
  5. Define the budget build-up. State how estimates, contingency and management reserve combine into the cost baseline and budget, and how funding will be released.
  6. Set reserve rules. Say how contingency is sized (for example, from the risk register) and who can release each reserve.
  7. Define how actuals are recorded. Cover timesheets, invoices, accruals and committed costs, and the status date used each period.
  8. Set performance measurement rules. Choose rules of credit by type of work and the default EAC method.
  9. Agree thresholds and reporting. Set variance thresholds, escalation routes and report formats with the sponsor.
  10. Agree change control and approve the plan. Define how cost baseline changes are requested and approved, then get the sponsor's sign-off and record the version.

Steps 1 to 3 need finance in the room. Steps 6, 9 and 10 need the sponsor, because they define the sponsor's own authority and what they will be told.

Cost management plan template (12 sections)

Copy this outline into your project management plan or a standalone document. Delete prompts that do not apply.

  1. Purpose, scope and references. What the plan covers, links to the project charter, organisational policies and contract terms.
  2. Roles and financial authority. Who prepares, approves, spends and reports, with money limits for each role.
  3. Units, precision and accuracy. Currency, effort units, rounding rules and target estimate accuracy.
  4. Cost structure. Control accounts, work packages, cost codes and how indirect costs are allocated.
  5. Estimating approach. Methods by stage, rate tables, the basis of estimate and review steps.
  6. Budget, cost baseline and funding. Build-up from estimates to budget, time-phasing, funding tranches and cash flow.
  7. Reserve management. How contingency and management reserve are sized, held, released and recorded.
  8. Recording actual costs. Timesheets, invoices, accruals, committed costs, status dates and the system of record.
  9. Performance measurement. Earned value rules of credit, EAC methods and measurement frequency.
  10. Control thresholds and escalation. Green, amber and red limits, the response to each and timescales.
  11. Reporting. Reports, audience, frequency, content and owner.
  12. Cost change control and plan maintenance. How baseline changes are requested and approved, review triggers, approval and version history.

Cost management plan example: warehouse system rollout

The extracts below show the 12 sections completed for a nine-month project to implement a warehouse management system (WMS) across two distribution centres. Adapt the values to your organisation; they are an example, not a standard.

1. Purpose, scope and references

This plan defines how costs for the WMS implementation will be estimated, budgeted, recorded and controlled from approval to closure. It applies the company's capital project policy and the PMO reporting standard. Ongoing support costs after handover are out of scope.

2. Roles and financial authority

RoleResponsibilitiesAuthority
Sponsor (Operations Director)Approves budget and plan, holds management reserveApproves baseline changes up to $50,000 and contingency releases over $10,000
Steering groupReviews monthly performance and major changesApproves changes over $50,000 and any use of management reserve
Project managerOwns the plan, baseline, forecasts and reportsApproves spend within the baseline; releases contingency up to $10,000 per listed risk
Control account managersManage budget and variances for their accountsApprove spend within their control account
Finance business partnerCost codes, actuals, accruals, capitalisationConfirms capital and operating split

3. Units, precision and accuracy

  • Currency: US dollars. Effort: person-hours. Internal labour costed at a blended rate of $95 per hour; contractors at contract rates.
  • Precision: work package estimates to the nearest $100; control accounts and reports to the nearest $1,000.
  • Accuracy: the baseline estimate is expected to be within -5% to +10% of the final cost.

4. Cost structure

Control accountManagerBudget (BAC)
CA1 Project managementProject manager$90,000
CA2 Process designOperations lead$150,000
CA3 Software and licencesIT procurement lead$320,000
CA4 Configuration and integrationSolution architect$260,000
CA5 Hardware and site readinessSite engineering lead$140,000
CA6 Testing and trainingTest and training lead$90,000
Total work package budgets$1,050,000

Cost codes follow the format WMS-CA-WP, for example WMS-04-03 for the carrier integration work package. Indirect costs are not allocated to the project.

5. Estimating approach

Initiation used a rough order of magnitude estimate from a similar past project. The baseline uses bottom-up estimates for labour, supplier quotes for software and hardware, and three-point estimates for integration work. Each work package has a basis of estimate listing assumptions, sources, exclusions and the estimator.

6. Budget, cost baseline and funding

LayerAmount
Work package budgets$1,050,000
+ Contingency reserve (from the risk register)$90,000
= Cost baseline$1,140,000
+ Management reserve (PMO policy, about 5%)$60,000
= Total project budget$1,200,000

The baseline is time-phased by month from the schedule. Funding is released in two tranches: $700,000 at approval and $500,000 at the month-5 stage gate.

7. Reserve management

  • Contingency is held at project level and linked to named risks. When released, it is moved into the relevant work package budget and recorded in the change log.
  • The project manager may release up to $10,000 per listed risk; larger releases need the sponsor.
  • Management reserve can only be used through a change request approved by the steering group.
  • Unused contingency is reported monthly and returned at closure, not spent on new scope.

8. Recording actual costs

Timesheets are submitted weekly. Supplier invoices are matched to purchase orders and coded by work package. Finance posts accruals at month end for work done but not invoiced. Committed costs (orders placed but not delivered) are reported separately. The status date is the last working day of each month. Hardware priced in euros is converted at the exchange rate fixed when the budget was approved; any exchange difference is reported as its own line.

9. Performance measurement

  • Work packages under one month: 0/100. One to two months: 50/50. Longer packages: weighted milestones agreed in the basis of estimate.
  • Hardware installation: physical percent complete (scanners and access points installed against plan). Project management: level of effort.
  • Default forecast: EAC = BAC / CPI. The project manager may use a bottom-up estimate to complete when the cause of a variance is known to be one-off, and must say so in the report.

10. Control thresholds and escalation

StatusCumulative CPI or SPIResponse
Green0.95 to 1.10Report as normal
Amber0.90 to 0.95, or 1.10 to 1.15Written cause and recovery action in the monthly report
RedBelow 0.90, or above 1.15Escalate to the sponsor within five working days with options and a revised EAC
  • Control account variances under $10,000 are reported but not escalated, whatever the ratio.
  • If the forecast variance at completion is larger than the remaining contingency, the project is escalated to the steering group regardless of CPI.
  • Favourable variances outside the band are investigated too, because they often mean missing actuals or overstated progress.

11. Reporting

ReportAudienceFrequencyContent
Cost snapshotProject team, control account managersWeeklyHours booked, spend and commitments by control account
Earned value reportSponsor, steering groupMonthlyPV, EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC, VAC, reserve status, threshold breaches
Finance forecastFinanceQuarterlyForecast spend by month, capital and operating split
Closure cost reportSponsor, PMOAt closureFinal cost against baseline, reserve use, lessons for estimating

The monthly cost section is included in the project's status report.

12. Cost change control and plan maintenance

Any change to the cost baseline needs a change request with the cost, schedule and benefit impact, routed through the project's change control process using the authority limits in section 2. The plan is reviewed at each stage gate and whenever the budget changes by more than 10%. Version 1.0 approved by the sponsor at project approval.

Applying the thresholds: a month-4 check

Here is how the example plan's rules work at the end of month four, when the project has earned 50% of its work package budgets.

Control accountEVACCVCPIStatus under the plan
CA1 Project management$40,000$41,000-$1,0000.98Green
CA2 Process design$150,000$148,000+$2,0001.01Green
CA3 Software and licences$160,000$176,000-$16,0000.91Amber: written explanation
CA4 Configuration and integration$78,000$87,000-$9,0000.90Red by ratio, but under $10,000: reported, not escalated
CA5 Hardware and site readiness$70,000$74,000-$4,0000.95Amber by ratio (0.946), but under $10,000: reported
CA6 Testing and training$22,000$22,000$01.00Green
Project total$520,000$548,000-$28,0000.95 (0.949)Amber
  • Project status. CPI = 520,000 / 548,000 = 0.949, just inside the amber band, so the monthly report must include a cause and recovery action.
  • Forecast. EAC = BAC / CPI = 1,050,000 / 0.949 = about $1,107,000, a variance at completion of about -$57,000. That is less than the $90,000 of contingency, so the steering group trigger is not met.
  • Contingency release. The CA3 overrun comes from a supplier price increase on additional licences, which was a listed risk. The release needed is $16,000, over the project manager's $10,000 limit, so it goes to the sponsor for approval.
  • Watch item. CA4 has the worst ratio but a small money variance. The plan keeps it out of escalation, but the project manager notes it as a trend to watch next month.

Without the money floor, CA4 and CA5 would both have been escalated, taking sponsor time for $13,000 of combined variance. Without the ratio, CA3's drift would have been hidden inside the project total. Using both is what makes the thresholds work. For the formulas, see cost variance, the cost performance index and the estimate at completion.

Tailoring the plan to your project

SituationWhat to emphasiseWhat to simplify
Small internal projectUnits, budget, simple thresholds (for example, more than 10% over), who approves extra spendSkip control accounts and formal earned value; a planned vs actual tracker may be enough
Capital or construction projectCost codes, commitments, retentions, inflation and exchange rates, capitalisation rulesLittle; these projects usually need the full plan
Agile deliveryCost per sprint, release budgets, how scope changes affect forecastsTask-level estimates and rules of credit; measure value at release level
Fixed-price contract (as supplier)Margin protection, change orders and claims, internal cost trackingClient reporting may be limited to milestones
Time-and-materials contractRate cards, approval of hours, not-to-exceed limitsEarned value may be replaced by burn against a cap
Programmes with formal EVM requirementsCompliance with the client's earned value system requirements, such as the EIA-748 standardNothing; follow the contract requirements

For Agile work, the plan typically fixes the team's cost per sprint and the release budget, then states how forecasts are updated from velocity. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs covers the measures most teams pair with cost. If much of the budget is bought in, align the plan with your procurement management approach.

Who writes and approves the plan, and when

The project manager writes the plan, with input from finance, the sponsor, the PMO and the people who will manage control accounts. The sponsor approves it, usually alongside the rest of the project management plan.

It is written during planning, before the detailed estimates and budget are produced, because it sets the rules those estimates must follow. In practice the first draft is often prepared in parallel with the WBS and refined once the cost structure is clear.

Review it at each stage gate, when the budget changes significantly, when the contract or funding model changes, and when thresholds are breached repeatedly, which can mean they were set at the wrong level. Record every change in the version history. Clear ownership of these decisions is part of good project governance.

Common cost management plan mistakes

  • Copying a template without tailoring it. A 20-page plan that nobody reads is worse than a two-page plan that everyone follows.
  • No financial authority limits. Without them, every spend decision becomes an email chain to the sponsor, or nobody checks spend at all.
  • Thresholds with only a percentage. Small work packages trigger constant false alarms.
  • Subjective progress measurement. Without rules of credit, earned value depends on optimism.
  • Not involving finance. If the cost codes do not match the WBS, actuals cannot be compared with the baseline.
  • Ignoring accruals and commitments. The project looks cheaper than it is until the invoices arrive.
  • Reserves without release rules. Contingency is either spent quietly or never used when it should be.
  • Writing it once and never reviewing it. The plan should change when the project's size, risk or funding changes.

Cost management plan review checklist

Before you ask the sponsor to approve the plan, check that you can answer yes to each question.

  • Are the currency, effort units and rounding rules stated?
  • Is the expected accuracy of the baseline estimate stated?
  • Does every work package have a cost code that finance recognises?
  • Is it clear how contingency and management reserve are sized and who can release them?
  • Are timesheets, invoices, accruals and commitments covered, with a fixed status date?
  • Are rules of credit defined for each type of work?
  • Do thresholds use both a ratio and a money amount, with a response for each level?
  • Are reports, audiences and frequencies listed?
  • Are authority limits for spend and baseline changes written down?
  • Is there a version number, approval and review schedule?

Frequently asked questions

What is a cost management plan?

A component of the project management plan that describes how project costs will be estimated, budgeted, recorded, measured and controlled, including thresholds, reporting, reserves and approval authority.

What are the components of a cost management plan?

PMBOK 6 lists units of measure, level of precision, level of accuracy, organisational procedures links, control thresholds, rules of performance measurement, reporting formats and additional details. Most plans also cover roles and authority, reserves and change control.

Who prepares the cost management plan?

The project manager, with input from finance, the PMO, the sponsor and control account managers. The sponsor approves it.

When is the cost management plan created?

During planning, before detailed estimates and the budget are produced, because it sets the rules they follow.

Is the cost management plan the same as the budget?

No. The plan describes how costs are managed. The budget is the approved amount of money: the cost baseline plus management reserve.

What is the difference between the cost management plan and the cost baseline?

The plan is the set of rules. The cost baseline is the approved, time-phased budget, including contingency, that performance is measured against.

What is a control threshold?

An agreed variance level, such as a cumulative CPI below 0.95, that triggers a defined response such as a written explanation or escalation.

What is a control account?

A management point where scope, budget, actual cost and schedule are integrated and compared with earned value. It contains one or more work packages and has a single manager.

What are earned value rules of credit?

Rules for how much earned value a task earns as it progresses, such as 0/100, 50/50, weighted milestones, physical percent complete or level of effort.

What is the level of precision in a cost management plan?

How far estimates and reports are rounded, for example to the nearest $100 for work packages and $1,000 for reports.

What is the level of accuracy in a cost management plan?

The acceptable range for cost estimates, such as -5% to +10% for the baseline estimate, which may include an allowance for contingency.

Do small or Agile projects need a cost management plan?

Yes, but a short one. A small project may need only units, budget, thresholds, authority and reporting. Agile plans usually fix cost per sprint and forecast from velocity.

How long should a cost management plan be?

Long enough to answer the key questions and no longer. Two pages can work for a small project; a large capital project may need ten or more.

What is the PMBOK 8 equivalent of Plan Cost Management?

The 8th edition places cost in the Finance performance domain, and its first process is called Plan Financial Management.
