Project Milestones: Definition, Examples and How to Set Them

Project milestones are significant points or events in a project, such as a sign-off, a handover or a go-live, that mark progress without taking any time themselves. This guide explains what counts as a milestone, gives examples for six types of project, shows you how to set and word milestones step by step, and walks through a full milestone plan, a status update and a milestone trend chart.

Key takeaways

  • A milestone is a zero-duration point in the schedule that marks a completion, decision, handover or external event.
  • Write each milestone as a completed state ("Design approved") with an owner and evidence that proves it has been reached.
  • Link milestones to the activities that lead to them so their dates calculate from the schedule rather than being typed in.
  • A handful of milestones per phase is usually enough; too many turns the milestone report into a task list.
  • Track baseline, forecast and actual dates for every milestone and use a trend chart to spot slippage early.
  • Payment milestones need clear acceptance criteria written into the contract before work starts.

What is a project milestone?

A project milestone is a significant point or event in a project. PMI's PMBOK Guide uses this wording, and in scheduling practice a milestone has zero duration: it marks the moment something is finished, decided or handed over, rather than the work that leads up to it. Typical milestones are approvals, phase completions, deliveries, contract signatures and go-live dates.

Because milestones take no time, they are the cleanest way to answer the question sponsors ask most often: "Are we where we said we would be?" A task can be 60% complete and still be in trouble. A milestone is either reached or not.

A good milestone has five characteristics:

  • Zero duration. It is an instant on the calendar, not a block of work.
  • Significance. Reaching it changes something: a decision can be made, a phase can close, money can be paid, another team can start.
  • Verifiable completion. There is objective evidence that it has happened, such as a signed document, a passed test or a live system.
  • An owner. One named person confirms it has been reached.
  • A place in the schedule. It is linked to the activities that must finish first, so its date moves when they move.

The milestone list: mandatory and optional milestones

In the PMBOK Guide (6th edition) the milestone list is an output of the Define Activities process. It records every milestone and normally shows whether each one is mandatory, such as a date required by a contract or a regulator, or optional, such as an internal checkpoint the team chose based on experience. The distinction matters when the schedule comes under pressure: optional milestones can be moved or merged, while mandatory ones usually cannot without a formal change.

In practice most project managers keep the milestone list as a few columns in the schedule or a tab in the project plan rather than a separate document. Our free project plan template and project schedule template both have room for it.

Milestone vs deliverable

A deliverable is a thing; a milestone is the moment something happens to it. "Design pack" is a deliverable. "Design pack approved by sponsor" is a milestone. Many milestones are built around deliverables being accepted, which is why the two are so often listed together.

A deadline, by contrast, is a date constraint rather than an event: a milestone's forecast date comes from the schedule, and the deadline tells you how much room you have.

Why project milestones matter

Milestones are one of the simplest planning tools, and one of the most useful. In our experience they help in five ways:

  • Clear reporting. Senior stakeholders do not need to see 300 tasks. A list of 10 to 20 milestones with forecast dates tells them what they need to know.
  • Early warning. A milestone that slips by a week is a visible signal before the end date is affected, giving you time to recover.
  • Decision points. Milestones mark the moments when the sponsor or a board decides whether to continue, change direction or stop, which is central to project governance.
  • Contract and payment control. Clients and suppliers can tie payments and acceptance to specific, verifiable events.
  • Team focus and morale. Long projects feel endless without visible checkpoints. Reaching a milestone is a natural moment to recognise the team's work.

Agreeing the milestone list early is also a quick way to check that every stakeholder has the same picture of how the project will unfold.

Types of project milestones

There is no official classification, but most milestones fall into one of the types below. Thinking through each type is a useful way to make sure you have not missed an important point in the plan.

Tip

External dependency milestones are often forgotten. If your plan relies on a supplier, a regulator or another project, add a milestone for that event so the dependency is visible and someone is watching it.

The five milestones most projects share

People often ask what "the five milestones of a project" are. There is no standard list, but almost every project, whatever its method, has a milestone at the end of each of the five classic phases:

  1. Project approved: the charter or business case is signed and the project manager is authorised to spend money.
  2. Plan baselined: scope, schedule and budget are agreed and saved as the project baseline.
  3. Key deliverable accepted: the main output is built and accepted (on larger projects there will be several of these).
  4. Go-live or handover: the result is in use or passed to the people who will run it.
  5. Project closed: lessons are captured, contracts closed and the team released.

How to set project milestones step by step

Setting milestones is part of project scheduling. Follow these steps once you have a first version of the scope and the work breakdown.

  1. Start from outcomes and structure. Take the work breakdown structure and the phases of your life cycle. The completion of each major deliverable or phase is a candidate milestone.
  2. Find the decision and handover points. Ask where the sponsor needs to decide something, where work passes from one team or supplier to another, and where a customer has to accept something.
  3. Add external and contractual dates. List anything that comes from outside the team: contract dates, regulatory deadlines, supplier deliveries, other projects you depend on, and fixed events such as trade shows. Mark these as mandatory.
  4. Write each milestone as a completed state. Use a noun plus a past participle, such as "Supplier contract signed" or "UAT passed", not an action like "Sign contract".
  5. Define completion criteria and an owner. For each milestone, record the evidence that proves it has been reached and the one person who confirms it.
  6. Link milestones into the schedule. Connect each milestone to the activities that must finish first using task dependencies, so its date is calculated rather than typed in.
  7. Check the critical path and float. Run the schedule and note which milestones sit on the critical path and how much float the others have. Critical milestones need the closest watch.
  8. Review, agree and baseline. Walk the sponsor and key stakeholders through the milestone list, agree the dates, then save them as part of the baseline so you can report variance later.

How many milestones should a project have?

There is no fixed number. As a rule of thumb from practice, aim for two to five milestones per phase, or roughly one every two to six weeks on a typical project, so that progress is visible at regular intervals. Fewer than that and slippage can hide for too long. More than that and the milestone report becomes a task list that senior readers stop reading.

If you need more detail for the team, use two levels: a short list of key milestones for sponsors and the steering group, and a longer list of internal milestones for the project team.

Worked example: an office move milestone plan

A company is moving 120 staff to a new office. The project runs for 16 weeks from charter approval to closure, and the sponsor is the operations director. The project manager builds the schedule, then pulls out these milestones.

M1Project charter approvedAuthorisationBusiness caseEnd week 1Charter signed by sponsorSponsor
M2Lease signedContractual (mandatory)Legal reviewEnd week 3Lease countersignedFacilities lead
M3Fit-out design approvedApprovalSpace planningEnd week 5Sponsor and HR sign floor planProject manager
M4Network and power installedDeliveryFit-out worksEnd week 10Supplier test certificates receivedIT lead
M5Furniture delivered and installedDeliveryFit-out worksEnd week 12Snag list closedFacilities lead
M6Move weekend completeGo-liveM4, M5, staff briefingsEnd week 14All staff working from new office on MondayProject manager

Notice that M2 and M7 are mandatory: they come from the lease, so moving them would need a negotiation with the landlord. M6 depends on two parallel streams (network and furniture), so whichever finishes later drives its date.

Status at the end of week 8

By week 8 the fit-out contractor reports a two-week delay to the network cabling. The project manager updates the schedule and the milestone report looks like this:

IDMilestoneBaselineForecastActualVarianceStatus
M1Project charter approvedWeek 1Week 10Complete
M2Lease signedWeek 3Week 30Complete
M3Fit-out design approvedWeek 5Week 6+1 weekComplete (late)
M4Network and power installedWeek 10Week 12+2 weeksRed
M5Furniture delivered and installedWeek 12Week 120Green
M6Move weekend completeWeek 14Week 140Amber
M7Old office handed backWeek 15Week 150Green

M4 has slipped two weeks, but M6 is still forecast for week 14 because there were two weeks of float between the network installation and the move. That float has now gone: any further delay to M4 will move the move weekend and, because M7 is fixed by the lease, squeeze the clear-out. Although M6 is still forecast on its baseline date, the project manager marks it amber because it now has no float, raises a risk, asks the contractor for a recovery plan and considers schedule compression options such as a second cabling crew.

Milestones on a Gantt chart, timeline and milestone chart

On a Gantt chart milestones are drawn as diamonds on the date they fall due. In most scheduling tools you create one by adding a task and setting its duration to zero; some tools also have a dedicated milestone task type or flag. Link the diamond to its predecessor bars so it moves when they do.

A milestone chart shows only the milestones on a timeline, with no task bars. It fits on one slide and is the best view for executives, boards and clients. A common layout shows a baseline diamond and a forecast diamond for each milestone, so slippage is visible at a glance.

Milestones are also the backbone of a project timeline and a project roadmap, which present the plan at a higher level than the full schedule. Our free Gantt chart template includes milestone rows if you are working in a spreadsheet.

How to track and report milestones

Tracking milestones is simple, but it only works if you do it consistently. A routine that works on most projects:

  1. Update the schedule first. Record actual progress on activities, then let the software (or your spreadsheet formulas) recalculate milestone forecast dates.
  2. Compare forecast with baseline. For each open milestone, calculate the variance in days or weeks.
  3. Apply a simple status rule. For example: green if forecast is on or before baseline, amber if it is later but the end date is protected by float, red if a key milestone or the end date is now at risk. Agree the rule with the sponsor so the colours mean the same thing every week.
  4. Explain every amber and red. Give the cause, the impact and what you are doing about it.
  5. Record actual dates when milestones are reached, along with the evidence, so the history is auditable.
  6. Report in the same format each time. A consistent milestone table in your project status report builds trust faster than any narrative.

Milestone trend analysis

Milestone trend analysis (MTA) shows how the forecast dates of key milestones change from one report to the next. You plot the reporting date on one axis and the forecast milestone date on the other, with one line per milestone. Using the office move above:

Report dateM4 forecastM5 forecastM6 forecast
Week 2Week 10Week 12Week 14
Week 4Week 10Week 12Week 14
Week 6Week 11Week 12Week 14
Week 8Week 12Week 12Week 14

Read the lines like this:

  • A flat line means the forecast is stable: the milestone is under control (M5 and M6 here).
  • A rising line means the milestone keeps slipping (M4 has slipped at each of the last two reports). A steady rise is often a sign of an optimistic plan or an unresolved problem, not bad luck.
  • A falling line means the milestone is forecast earlier than before, which can mean the original estimate was padded.
  • A line that reaches the diagonal (where reporting date equals milestone date) means the milestone has been reached.

Some scheduling tools include an MTA chart, and you can also draw one in a spreadsheet with a simple line chart.

What to do when a milestone slips

  • Confirm the new forecast with the people doing the work; do not guess.
  • Check whether the milestone is on the critical path and whether float absorbs the delay.
  • Assess the knock-on effect on later milestones, especially mandatory ones.
  • Agree recovery actions: re-sequencing, extra resources, fast-tracking or reducing scope.
  • If the baseline date cannot be recovered, raise a change request rather than quietly moving the baseline.
  • Tell stakeholders early. A slipped milestone reported promptly is a management issue; one discovered late is a trust issue.
Tip

Never re-baseline a milestone just to make the report look green. Keep the original baseline visible and show the approved change alongside it.

Payment milestones in contracts

A payment milestone is a contractual milestone that triggers an invoice when the agreed work is accepted. Fixed-price contracts and agency engagements often use them instead of monthly billing, because the client pays for verified progress and the supplier gets cash flow during the project.

Here is an example payment schedule for a £40,000 website build:

Payment milestoneAcceptance evidenceShare of feeAmount
Contract signedSigned statement of work20%£8,000
Designs approvedClient sign-off on agreed page designs25%£10,000
UAT passedClient confirms all agreed test cases passed35%£14,000
Site live and handover completeSite on production domain; training delivered; documentation handed over20%£8,000

To make payment milestones work:

  • Write the acceptance criteria into the statement of work so nobody has to negotiate them at invoice time.
  • Set a review period (for example ten working days) after which the milestone is deemed accepted if the client has not raised defects.
  • Avoid milestones that depend entirely on the other party, such as "client content received", unless the contract says what happens if they are late.
  • Keep payment milestones in your schedule like any other milestone so you can forecast cash flow.

Milestones in Agile projects

The Scrum Guide does not define milestones, and Agile teams plan in short iterations rather than a fixed sequence of phases. Even so, most Agile projects have milestones, because organisations and customers still need to know when something will be available.

Common Agile milestones include:

  • Release milestones: the date a release goes to customers, set during Agile release planning.
  • Sprint reviews: regular, fixed points where an increment is inspected. Some teams treat key reviews as milestones for stakeholders.
  • External commitments: a contract date, a regulatory deadline, a marketing launch or a trade show the product must be ready for.
  • Programme events: in scaled frameworks such as SAFe, the end of a planning interval is a natural milestone.
  • Outcome milestones: a measurable product result, such as the first paying customer or a feature reaching general availability.

On an Agile roadmap these milestones are usually shown as dates or time windows, with the scope of each release left flexible. That is the main difference from a predictive plan: the milestone date is fixed and the content adapts, rather than the other way round. Project milestone checklist

Before you share your milestone plan, check that each milestone:

  • Is written as a completed state with zero duration.
  • Marks a completion, decision, handover, payment or external event.
  • Has completion criteria, one named owner and a mandatory or optional flag.
  • Is linked to its predecessors, with its critical path and float status known.
  • Has an agreed baseline date and appears in the status report with forecast and actual dates.

Frequently asked questions

What is a project milestone?

A project milestone is a significant point or event in a project, such as an approval, a delivery, a phase completion or a go-live. It has zero duration and marks the moment something happens rather than the work leading up to it.

Do project milestones have a duration?

No. A milestone is a point in time with zero duration. The work that leads to it is shown as tasks or activities, and the milestone marks the moment that work is confirmed as complete.

How many milestones should a project have?

There is no fixed number. As a rule of thumb, two to five milestones per phase, or one every few weeks, keeps progress visible without turning the milestone report into a task list.

What is the difference between a milestone and a deliverable?

A deliverable is an output, such as a report, a design or a system. A milestone is an event, such as that deliverable being approved. Many milestones are defined as a deliverable being accepted.

What are the five milestones of a project?

There is no standard list, but most projects have at least five: project approved, plan baselined, key deliverable accepted, go-live or handover, and project closed. Most projects add more milestones within each phase.

What is a milestone chart?

A milestone chart shows only the milestones of a project on a timeline, usually with baseline and forecast dates side by side. It is a one-page view for sponsors, boards and clients who do not need the full schedule.

Can a milestone be on the critical path?

Yes. A milestone linked to critical activities has no float, so any delay to it delays the project finish. Identify these milestones and watch them most closely.

What is the difference between a milestone and a phase gate?

A phase gate is a particular kind of milestone at which a formal go or no-go decision is taken before the next phase starts. Every gate is a milestone, but most milestones are not gates.

What is a payment milestone?

A payment milestone is a contractual milestone that triggers an invoice once agreed work is accepted. It should have acceptance criteria written into the contract or statement of work.

What is the difference between mandatory and optional milestones?

Mandatory milestones are required by a contract, regulator or other external commitment and cannot easily move. Optional milestones are internal checkpoints the team chose and can be adjusted if the plan changes.

What is milestone trend analysis?

Milestone trend analysis tracks how the forecast date of each key milestone changes from one report to the next. A flat line means the milestone is stable; a rising line means it keeps slipping.

Are milestones used in Agile?

Yes. The Scrum Guide does not define milestones, but Agile teams commonly use release dates, sprint reviews, external deadlines and product launches as milestones on their roadmap.
