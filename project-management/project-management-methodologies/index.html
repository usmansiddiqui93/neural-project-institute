Project Management Methodologies: Types and How to Choose

Project management methodologies are defined systems of principles, processes, roles and practices for planning and delivering projects, such as Waterfall, Scrum, Kanban and PRINCE2. No single methodology is best for every project: the right choice depends on how stable your requirements are, whether the work can be delivered in pieces and how much governance applies. This guide compares the main methodologies, explains where standards such as the PMBOK Guide fit, and gives you a step-by-step way to choose, with a worked example.

Key takeaways

  • Approach, framework, methodology and standard mean different things: predictive and Agile are approaches, Scrum is a framework, PRINCE2 is a methodology and the PMBOK Guide is a standard.
  • Predictive methods such as Waterfall suit stable, well-understood work; adaptive methods such as Scrum and Kanban suit uncertain or evolving work.
  • Choose by scoring requirement stability, incremental delivery, customer availability, cost of change, governance and team experience.
  • Hybrid approaches, typically predictive governance with Agile delivery, are common and often the most practical.
  • Lean, Six Sigma, critical path and critical chain are usually combined with a main methodology rather than used alone.
  • Whatever you choose, tailor it, pilot it and review it after a few cycles.

What is a project management methodology?

A project management methodology is a defined set of principles, processes, roles, tools and rules that a team follows to plan, deliver and control a project. It sets out how work is organised, who decides what, which artefacts are produced and how changes are handled, so the team works in a consistent, predictable way.

In practice a methodology gives you five things:

  • A life cycle: the phases, stages or iterations the project moves through.
  • Roles and responsibilities: who sponsors, manages, decides and delivers.
  • Processes and events: how planning, reviews, approvals and reporting happen.
  • Artefacts: the plans, backlogs, logs and reports the team keeps.
  • Rules for change: how scope, schedule and budget changes are requested and agreed.

A shared methodology means less time arguing about how to work and more time doing it. It also makes projects easier to compare, govern and staff. But every methodology needs tailoring to the size, risk and context of the project. Our guide to what project management is gives the broader picture.

Approach, framework, methodology and standard

These words are often used interchangeably, which causes confusion when teams say they are "doing Agile" or "using PMBOK". The distinctions are worth knowing:

TermWhat it meansExamples
ApproachThe overall philosophy for delivering workPredictive, adaptive (Agile), hybrid
FrameworkA light structure with a few rules, leaving room to add your own practicesScrum, SAFe, the Kanban Method
MethodologyA more complete, defined system of processes, roles and artefactsPRINCE2, Waterfall, DSDM
TechniqueA specific tool used within any approachCritical path method, earned value, user stories
StandardA description of recognised good practice, not a step-by-step methodPMBOK Guide, ISO 21502

In everyday use, "methodology" covers all of these, and this guide follows that convention. The distinction matters when you choose: a framework such as Scrum gives you less out of the box than a methodology such as PRINCE2, so you will need to add practices for things like budgeting or contracts.

The main methodologies explained

Waterfall

Waterfall moves through fixed phases, typically requirements, design, build, test and deployment, each signed off before the next starts. Winston Royce described this sequence in a 1970 paper, while also warning that a purely sequential process is risky. It works well when requirements are clear and changes are expensive, as in construction or hardware. The risk is that problems surface late, at testing or handover. Our guide to Waterfall project management covers the phases in detail.

Agile

Agile is an approach based on the values and principles of the Agile Manifesto (2001): deliver working results in short cycles, collaborate closely with customers and respond to change. It is not one method but a family that includes Scrum, Kanban and XP. It suits work where requirements are uncertain and early feedback is valuable. Its main risk is losing sight of budgets, dates and dependencies if the organisation treats "Agile" as a reason not to plan. See our guide to the Agile methodology and the types of Agile methodologies.

Scrum

Scrum is the most widely known Agile framework. Under the 2020 Scrum Guide, a Scrum Team of usually ten or fewer people (a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and Developers) works in sprints of one month or less, with five events (the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective) and three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog and Increment). It suits product development where a usable increment can be produced every sprint. Our guide to what Scrum is explains each element.

Kanban

Kanban manages work as a continuous flow. Work items move across a visual board, work-in-progress (WIP) limits stop the team starting more than it can finish, and flow metrics such as cycle time and throughput drive improvement. There are no prescribed roles or sprints, so it fits support, operations, marketing and any team with a steady stream of requests. You can start by visualising how you already work and improve from there, which makes it one of the least disruptive methods to introduce. See what Kanban is.

Scrumban

Scrumban combines Scrum's planning and review cadence with Kanban's flow and WIP limits. It is popular with teams that handle planned feature work alongside unplanned requests, or teams moving from Scrum to Kanban. Compare the options in Kanban vs Scrumban.

Lean

Lean comes from the Toyota Production System. It focuses on delivering customer value with the least waste, using ideas such as value stream mapping, pull and continuous improvement. In projects it is used in construction (for example the Last Planner System), operations and alongside Agile. It works best as a mindset applied within another method rather than as a stand-alone project life cycle. See Lean project management.

Six Sigma

Six Sigma is a data-driven method for reducing defects and variation, run as projects that follow the DMAIC cycle (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control). It is strongest on repeatable processes with measurable output and is often combined with Lean as Lean Six Sigma. Our Six Sigma guide includes a worked DMAIC example.

PRINCE2

PRINCE2 (PRojects IN Controlled Environments) is a process-based method that originated with the UK government and is now owned by PeopleCert, which launched the current version, PRINCE2 7, in October 2023. It has seven principles, seven practices and seven processes. Projects are divided into management stages, a project board makes key decisions, a business case must remain valid throughout, and the project manager works within agreed tolerances (management by exception). It is widely used in the UK, Europe, Australia and the public sector, and can be combined with Agile delivery inside its stages. See our PRINCE2 guide.

Critical path method

The critical path method, developed in the late 1950s, is a scheduling technique rather than a full methodology. You list activities, durations and dependencies, then find the longest chain of dependent tasks, which sets the earliest finish date. It is used inside predictive and hybrid projects. Our guide to the critical path method shows how to calculate it.

Critical chain project management

Critical chain, developed by Eliyahu Goldratt from his theory of constraints, schedules around resource availability as well as dependencies. Safety time is removed from individual tasks and pooled into shared buffers, and progress is tracked by buffer consumption. It suits organisations where scarce specialists are shared across projects. Our guide to critical chain project management explains buffer management.

Extreme Programming (XP)

XP is an Agile software development method created by Kent Beck in the late 1990s. It centres on engineering practices such as pair programming, test-driven development, continuous integration and small, frequent releases. Many teams use XP practices inside Scrum or Kanban.

Extreme project management (XPM)

Extreme project management, described by Doug DeCarlo in 2004, is for projects where the goal, the solution or both are unclear and conditions change quickly, such as innovation and R&D. It works in rapid cycles and treats the plan as a hypothesis. Our guide to extreme project management covers DeCarlo's model.

Scaled Agile frameworks

When many Agile teams work on one product, scaling frameworks such as SAFe, LeSS and Scrum@Scale add coordination: shared planning events, roles across teams and portfolio management. Our guide to the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) covers the most widely adopted option.

Where the PMBOK Guide and ISO 21502 fit

The PMBOK Guide and ISO 21502 are often listed as methodologies, but they are standards: they describe what good project management involves, not a step-by-step process to follow.

  • The PMBOK Guide is published by the Project Management Institute (PMI). The eighth edition, published in November 2025, is organised around six principles and seven performance domains, and covers predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches. It underpins the PMP and CAPM exams.
  • ISO 21502:2020 is the international standard giving guidance on project management, published by the International Organization for Standardization.

Use a standard as a reference for what to cover, and a methodology or framework for how to do it. Most organisations tailor a methodology so that it meets the standard their sponsors or clients expect. Read our guide to the PMBOK Guide and the comparison of PMBOK vs PRINCE2.

How methodologies map to the project life cycle

Every project, whatever the method, needs to be started, planned, delivered, controlled and closed. Methodologies differ in how often they repeat those activities and what they call them. This mapping helps when you translate between methods or report to stakeholders used to a different one; see our guide to project management phases.

Life cycle activityWaterfallPRINCE2ScrumKanban
InitiatingProject charter, requirementsStarting up and initiating a project; business caseProduct goal, initial Product BacklogAgree the workflow, policies and WIP limits
PlanningFull plan and schedule up frontProject plan plus a detailed plan for each stageSprint Planning every sprint; backlog refinementContinuous replenishment of the board
ExecutingDesign, build, test in sequenceManaging product delivery within each stageDevelopers build a usable Increment each sprintItems pulled through the workflow as capacity allows
Monitoring and controllingProgress against baseline, change controlControlling a stage; tolerances and exception reportsDaily Scrum, Sprint ReviewFlow metrics, daily review of the board
ClosingHandover and sign-offClosing a project; lessons and benefits reviewSprint Retrospective each sprint; releaseDelivery of each item; regular retrospectives

The pattern is clear: predictive methods run each activity once, in sequence, while adaptive methods repeat planning, delivery and review every few weeks or continuously.

How to choose a project management methodology

There is no universally best methodology, only the best fit for a particular project, team and organisation. Work through these steps:

  1. Define what the project must deliver. Is the end product well understood, or will it be discovered along the way? Are there fixed dates, contracts or regulatory approvals?
  2. Assess requirement stability. Stable, well-documented requirements point to predictive methods; vague or changing requirements point to adaptive ones.
  3. Check whether the work can be delivered in pieces. Software, marketing campaigns and services can usually be released incrementally; a bridge or an office move mostly cannot.
  4. Consider the customer. Agile methods need a customer or product owner who can review work and make decisions every few weeks.
  5. Map governance and compliance needs. Regulated, contracted or publicly funded work may require stage gates, formal approvals and audit trails; see our guide to project governance.
  6. Look at your team and organisation. Consider team size, location, experience with each method and what the rest of the organisation already uses. Switching method on a critical project is risky.
  7. Score, decide and tailor. Score the shortlist against these factors, choose the closest fit, then document how you will tailor it: which roles, events, documents and approval points you will use.

Selection criteria at a glance

FactorPoints towards predictivePoints towards adaptive
RequirementsClear, stable, documentedUncertain, expected to change
DeliveryOnly valuable when completeUsable in increments
Customer availabilityLimited; sign-off at milestonesRegular, engaged reviewer
Cost of late changeHigh (physical work, contracts)Low (software, content)
GovernanceFormal approvals, audit trailsLight, outcome-focused
TeamSpecialists in sequence, distributedSmall, cross-functional, collaborative
Tip

If your factors split roughly evenly between the two columns, that is a strong sign a hybrid approach will work best.

In this illustrative example, a PMO scores three projects from 1 to 5 on each factor, where 1 points strongly towards a predictive approach and 5 strongly towards an adaptive one. The thresholds are a rule of thumb for discussion, not a formal standard: 6 to 13 suggests predictive, 14 to 22 hybrid and 23 to 30 adaptive.

Suggested approachPredictiveAdaptiveHybrid
  • Office relocation (10): predictive. The scope is clear, the move happens on fixed dates and nothing is useful half-finished. The PM uses a Waterfall life cycle with a Gantt chart template, critical path scheduling and formal sign-off from facilities and IT.
  • Customer mobile app (27): adaptive. The team does not yet know which features users value most and can release every two weeks. It runs Scrum with two-week sprints and a dedicated Product Owner, then moves to Kanban for maintenance after launch.
  • Regulated system upgrade (16): hybrid. An auditor requires approved stages and documented validation, but the configuration work benefits from short feedback cycles. The project uses PRINCE2-style stages, a project board and stage gates, with Scrum sprints inside each delivery stage and validation evidence produced every sprint.

The scores matter less than the conversation they prompt. If two people score the same factor very differently, that usually reveals a risk or assumption to resolve before the project starts.

Hybrid project management in practice

Hybrid project management combines predictive and adaptive elements in one project. It is common because most organisations need some fixed commitments (budgets, contracts, launch dates) and also benefit from short feedback loops. These are the patterns you will see most often:

PatternHow it worksTypical use
Predictive governance, Agile deliveryBusiness case, budget and milestones fixed up front; teams deliver in sprints within each stageCorporate IT, digital transformation
Phased by componentFixed elements (hardware, building works) run predictively; software or content runs in iterationsProduct launches, fit-outs with technology
Agile discovery, predictive buildIterate to define requirements, then plan and deliver the rest sequentiallyConstruction design, engineering
Kanban within milestonesA milestone plan sets dates; a Kanban board manages the flow of tasks between themMarketing campaigns, events

Hybrid example: a website relaunch

In this illustrative example, a company relaunches its website for a fixed trade-show date. The sponsor approves a business case, budget and three milestones: design approved, content migrated, site live. Within those dates, the web team runs two-week sprints from a prioritised backlog and demonstrates working pages at each sprint review. Content migration, which is repetitive, runs on a Kanban board with a WIP limit per editor. Hosting, legal review and launch communications follow a simple Gantt schedule because they have fixed lead times. The project manager reports progress against the milestones, using sprint and flow data as evidence.

To make hybrid work, agree which parts of the project are fixed and which are flexible, align reporting so sprint progress rolls up into milestone reports, and make one person clearly accountable for each decision. The main risk is a mix that keeps the overhead of both approaches and the benefits of neither. Our Agile project plan template shows how sprints can sit inside a milestone plan.

How to introduce a new methodology

  1. Explain the reason. Link the change to a problem people recognise, such as late delivery or unclear priorities.
  2. Start with a pilot. Choose one project or team with a supportive sponsor, not the most critical project in the portfolio.
  3. Train the people involved, including sponsors and stakeholders, not only the delivery team.
  4. Use the method as written first, then tailor it once the team understands why each element exists.
  5. Set up templates and tools that match the method, such as a project plan template for predictive work or a Kanban board template for flow.
  6. Agree how you will measure success, for example on-time delivery, cycle time or stakeholder satisfaction.
  7. Review after a few cycles or stages, adjust, then roll out more widely with lessons learned.

Common mistakes when choosing a methodology

  • Following fashion. Choosing Agile because it is popular, or Waterfall because it is familiar, without checking the project's characteristics.
  • Using one method for everything. Different projects in the same portfolio may need different approaches.
  • Adopting the vocabulary but not the practice. Calling meetings stand-ups or phases sprints changes nothing on its own.
  • Over-tailoring too early. Removing the uncomfortable parts of a method, such as retrospectives or stage reviews, before seeing their value.
  • Ignoring governance. Agile teams still need budget control, risk management and stakeholder reporting.
  • Forgetting the customer. Adaptive methods fail without someone available to review work and make decisions.
  • Letting the tool decide. Software should support the chosen method, not dictate it.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main project management methodologies?

The most widely used are Waterfall, Agile (including Scrum, Kanban, Scrumban and XP), PRINCE2, Lean and Six Sigma, together with scheduling techniques such as the critical path method and critical chain.

What is the most popular project management methodology?

Agile approaches, especially Scrum and Kanban, are the most common in software and digital work, while predictive and hybrid approaches remain the norm in construction, engineering and government. Many organisations use more than one.

What is the difference between a methodology and a framework?

A methodology prescribes a fuller set of processes, roles and documents, as PRINCE2 does. A framework gives a lighter structure and leaves you to add your own practices, as Scrum does.

Is Agile a methodology?

Strictly, Agile is an approach or mindset based on the Agile Manifesto. Scrum, Kanban and XP are the frameworks and methods that put it into practice, though people commonly call Agile a methodology.

Is the PMBOK Guide a methodology?

No. The PMBOK Guide is PMI's standard describing good project management practice. You use it alongside a methodology rather than instead of one.

How do I choose the right project management methodology?

Assess requirement stability, whether the work can be delivered in pieces, customer availability, the cost of late change, governance needs and team experience, then choose and tailor the closest fit.

Can you combine project management methodologies?

Yes. Hybrid approaches are common, for example a milestone plan and stage gates for governance with Scrum sprints or a Kanban board for delivery.

What is a hybrid project management methodology?

One that combines predictive and adaptive elements, such as fixing budget and key dates up front while delivering the work in short iterations with regular feedback.

What is the difference between Agile and Waterfall?

Waterfall plans everything up front and delivers in sequence at the end; Agile plans in short cycles, delivers working increments and adapts as it learns.

What is the difference between Agile and Scrum?

Agile is the broad approach; Scrum is one specific framework for applying it, with defined accountabilities, events and artifacts.

Which methodology is best for construction?

Predictive methods with critical path scheduling remain the norm, often combined with Lean construction techniques such as the Last Planner System, and Agile practices in design.

Which methodology is best for software development?

Usually an Agile framework such as Scrum or Kanban, often with XP engineering practices. Large or regulated programmes may add a scaling framework or hybrid governance.

Which methodology is best for small teams?

Kanban is often the easiest to start with because it adds few rules. Scrum suits small teams building a product that can be released in increments.

Do I need a methodology for a small project?

You need some structure, even if it is light: a clear goal, a simple plan or board, agreed roles and a way to handle changes. A small project can use a slimmed-down version of any method.
