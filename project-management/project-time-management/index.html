Project Time Management: Processes, Techniques and Example

Project time management is the set of processes that make sure a project finishes on time: planning how the schedule will be managed, defining and sequencing activities, estimating durations, building the schedule and controlling it. Recent editions of the PMBOK Guide call this area schedule management. This guide explains each process, the main techniques and tools, and works through a full example from three-point estimates to a mid-project status check.

Key takeaways

  • Project time management (now called schedule management) covers planning, building and controlling the project schedule.
  • The classic six processes run from planning the approach to controlling the schedule, and they still give you a practical sequence to follow.
  • Three-point estimates and the critical path tell you both the likely finish date and how much confidence to put in it.
  • Before reacting to a negative schedule variance, check whether the late work is on the critical path.
  • In Agile projects, time is fixed by timeboxes and scope flexes instead.
  • Good personal time habits help the project manager keep the schedule current and spot problems early.

What is project time management?

Project time management is the planning, scheduling and control of the activities needed to finish a project by an agreed date. It covers deciding how the schedule will be managed, breaking the work into activities, ordering them, estimating how long each will take, building a schedule baseline and tracking progress against it so delays are found and fixed early.

The term comes from the PMBOK Guide, which called this knowledge area Project Time Management until its 6th edition renamed it Project Schedule Management. Both names describe the same work, and "project time management" is still widely used in courses, job descriptions and exam preparation.

You will also see the phrase used for something different: how a project manager manages their own working time. That matters too, and this guide covers it in a separate section, but the core of project time management is the project's schedule. It sits alongside scope and cost as one of the three classic [project constraints: change one and the others usually move.

Why project time management matters

A late finish rarely costs only time. Labour and overhead costs rise, benefits arrive later, other projects waiting for the same people are pushed back, and commitments to customers or regulators may be broken. Because activities depend on each other, a few days lost early on the critical path move every later date. Good time management gives you:

  • A realistic finish date that is based on the work, not on hope or a date imposed without analysis.
  • Early warning when the project starts to slip, while there is still float, budget or scope to trade.
  • Credible commitments to sponsors and customers, backed by estimates and a stated level of confidence.
  • A baseline to measure performance against and to learn from for future estimates.

The six project time management processes

The PMBOK Guide 6th edition describes schedule management as six processes. They are a logical sequence rather than a rigid one: on most projects you repeat the middle steps as you learn more.

ProcessWhat happensMain output
1. Plan schedule managementDecide the methods, tools, units, rules and reporting for the scheduleSchedule management plan
2. Define activitiesBreak work packages from the WBS into schedule activities and milestonesActivity list and milestone list
3. Sequence activitiesIdentify dependencies, leads and lags between activitiesProject schedule network diagram
4. Estimate activity durationsEstimate the working periods each activity needs with the planned resourcesDuration estimates and basis of estimates
5. Develop scheduleCalculate dates, find the critical path, fit resources and agree the baselineSchedule baseline and project schedule
6. Control scheduleTrack progress, compare with the baseline, forecast and manage changesWork performance information, forecasts and change requests

How the name and processes have changed

  • 5th edition and earlier: the knowledge area was called Project Time Management and had seven processes, including Estimate Activity Resources.
  • 6th edition (September 2017): renamed Project Schedule Management. Estimate Activity Resources moved to Project Resource Management, leaving the six processes above.
  • 7th edition: replaced process-based knowledge areas with principles and performance domains; scheduling is covered mainly within planning and delivery.
  • 8th edition (November 2025): has six principles and seven performance domains, one of which is Schedule. According to PMI and published summaries, it brings back non-prescriptive process guidance for planning, developing, and monitoring and controlling the schedule.

The underlying work has not changed, so the six processes remain a practical way to learn scheduling. Our PMBOK Guide overview explains the editions.

How to manage project time step by step

The steps below follow the six processes and show what to do at each one. They assume you already have an agreed scope and a work breakdown structure; without those, any schedule is guesswork.

Step 1: Plan schedule management

Agree the rules before you build anything: half a page on a small project, a section of the project management plan on a large one.

Item in the planExample decision
Scheduling method and toolCritical path method in the team's project management software
Units and calendarsDurations in working days; five-day week; public holidays excluded
Level of detailActivities of 1 to 10 working days; rolling wave for work more than three months away
Estimating approachThree-point estimates for critical and uncertain work; analogous for routine work
Update cycleProgress updated every Friday; forecast issued with the weekly status report
Control thresholdsEscalate if a milestone forecast slips by more than five working days or SPI falls below 0.90
Baseline rulesBaseline changes only through approved change requests

Step 2: Define activities

Decompose each work package into activities: actions with a clear start, end, owner and output. "Testing" is a work package; "run user acceptance test scripts for finance users" is an activity. Add milestones for key decisions, approvals and handovers, since they carry no duration but anchor the schedule.

Step 3: Sequence activities

Link the activities in the order the work must happen. Most links are finish-to-start (B starts when A finishes), but start-to-start, finish-to-finish and, rarely, start-to-finish also occur. Mark which links are mandatory (physical or legal) and which are discretionary (a team preference), because only discretionary links can be overlapped later. Add leads and lags where work can overlap or must wait. See our guide to task dependencies for all four types with examples.

Step 4: Estimate activity durations

Estimate the working time each activity needs with the resources you expect to have. Separate effort (person-days of work) from duration (calendar time): two people on a four-person-day task may take two days, or more if they have other work. Three techniques cover most needs:

  • Analogous estimating: use the actual duration of a similar past activity, adjusted for differences. Quick, but only as good as the comparison.
  • Parametric estimating: multiply a quantity by a rate, such as 300 users ÷ 25 users per training session = 12 sessions at half a day each = 6 days.
  • Three-point estimating: ask for optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P) durations. The beta (PERT) formula gives an expected duration of (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6 and a standard deviation of (P − O) ÷ 6.

Record the assumptions behind each estimate. Our effort estimation guide covers these and other methods in more depth.

Step 5: Develop the schedule

Combine the activities, links and durations into a schedule. Use the critical path method: a forward pass gives the earliest start and finish of each activity, a backward pass gives the latest, and the difference is float. Activities with zero float form the critical path, which sets the project's duration.

Then check the schedule against reality. Are people double-booked? Use resource levelling to resolve conflicts, accepting that it may extend the finish date. Does the finish miss a required date? Consider schedule compression by crashing or fast tracking. When the schedule is realistic and agreed, save it as the project baseline.

Step 6: Control the schedule

Update progress at the agreed interval: actual start and finish dates, remaining duration for work in progress, and any new or changed activities. Recalculate, compare forecast dates with the baseline and check float on critical and near-critical paths. Use earned value management to measure progress in a consistent way, and route any change to the baseline through change control.

Worked example: scheduling an expense system rollout

This illustrative example applies steps 2 to 5 to a small project: rolling out a new expense system to 300 staff. All durations are in working days.

Activities, dependencies and three-point estimates

ActivityDepends onOMPExpected (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6
A. Requirements workshops-23104
B. Configure the systemA8101811

The critical path is A → B → E → F → G, with a total duration of 4 + 11 + 5 + 6 + 3 = 29 working days. Data migration (D) is near-critical, with only 3 days of float, so it needs watching too. Training material (C) and communications (H) have plenty of float, so they can absorb delays or be scheduled around people's other work. Our guide to the forward and backward pass explains the calculations step by step.

How confident is the 29-day date?

Add the variances (standard deviation squared) of the critical activities: A (8 ÷ 6)² = 1.78, B (10 ÷ 6)² = 2.78, E 0.44, F 0.44 and G 0.11, a total of about 5.56. The square root gives a standard deviation for the path of about 2.4 days.

  • 29 days is the expected finish, so there is roughly a 50% chance of finishing on or before it.
  • 29 + 2.4 ≈ 31.4 days gives roughly 84% confidence (one standard deviation).
  • 29 + 4.7 ≈ 33.7 days gives roughly 98% confidence (two standard deviations).

The project manager commits to 32 working days (about six and a half weeks) and treats the three days between 29 and 32 as schedule contingency, owned by the project manager rather than spread across activities. This simple PERT calculation ignores near-critical paths such as the one through D, so it slightly overstates confidence; a Monte Carlo simulation handles that properly on larger projects.

Status check at day 15

At the end of day 15, C and D are complete on time, but configuration (B) is only about 80% done and is forecast to finish on day 17. Because B is critical, the whole chain moves: testing now runs from day 17 to 22, training from 22 to 28 and go-live support from 28 to 31. The 31-day forecast is inside the 32-day commitment, but two of the three contingency days are gone with half the project left.

The project manager reviews the options: add a second tester to shorten acceptance testing from 5 days to 4 (crashing), or start training a pilot group during the last two days of testing (fast tracking, with some risk of retraining if testing finds problems). The team chooses the extra tester, recovering one day at a known cost. Our fast tracking vs crashing guide compares the two approaches.

Tip

Hold contingency at project level and report how much has been used. A schedule that is still "on time" but has used most of its contingency early is a warning sign.

Worked example: controlling the schedule with earned value

Earned value gives a consistent, numerical view of schedule performance on larger projects. In this illustrative example, a 20-week project has a budget at completion of $200,000, planned to be spent evenly. At the end of week 8, the planned value (PV) is $80,000 and the earned value (EV), the budgeted value of the work actually completed, is $68,000.

MeasureFormulaResultMeaning
Schedule variance (SV)EV − PV−$12,000Less work done than planned
SV%SV ÷ PV−15%15% of the planned work is not yet done
Schedule performance index (SPI)EV ÷ PV0.85Progressing at 85% of the planned rate
Simple time forecastPlanned duration ÷ SPI20 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 23.5 weeksIf performance continues at the same rate

The naive forecast says the project will finish about three and a half weeks late. But earned value does not know which activities are critical, so the project manager looks at where the shortfall sits:

  • $9,000 of the shortfall is on documentation and training material, which has four weeks of float. It can catch up without moving the finish date, as long as the float is not used up.
  • $3,000 of the shortfall is on supplier integration, which is on the critical path and is about one week behind.

The better forecast is therefore about 21 weeks, not 23.5, unless the critical week is recovered. The actions follow from that: focus recovery effort on supplier integration, agree a catch-up date for documentation inside its float, and keep reporting SPI alongside critical path dates.

Project time management tools

The main tools for recording and showing the schedule are:

  • Gantt charts: bars on a timeline showing activities, dependencies, milestones and progress. The most common way to communicate a schedule.
  • Network diagrams: show the logic of the schedule and make the critical path easy to see.
  • Schedule templates: a quick start for small projects. Our free project schedule template and Gantt chart template work in Excel and Google Sheets, and the critical path template calculates float for you.
  • Scheduling software: recalculates dates automatically when something changes, which matters once you have more than a few dozen activities.
  • Timesheets and time tracking: record actual effort, which improves future estimates and supports billing.

Most modern project management tools combine several of these. Wrike, our top-rated tool, offers Gantt charts with dependencies and the critical path highlighted, plus time tracking with approved timesheets, which suits teams running several date-driven projects. ClickUp supports all four dependency types, automatic rescheduling, the critical path and baselines on its Gantt charts. monday.com is very approachable for mixed teams, with time tracking on its Pro plan. Smartsheet suits teams that like a spreadsheet layout with Gantt charts on top, and Microsoft Planner premium plans add dependencies and, on Plan 3, baselines and the critical path.

Project time management in Agile projects

Agile approaches manage time differently. Instead of fixing scope and estimating how long it will take, they usually fix time and let scope flex.

  • Timeboxes: work happens in fixed-length iterations. In Scrum, a Sprint lasts one month or less, and events such as the Daily Scrum have a fixed maximum length.
  • Prioritised scope: the most valuable work is done first, so if time runs out, what is left is the least valuable.
  • Empirical forecasting: release dates are forecast from the team's measured pace rather than from activity estimates.
  • Visible progress: burndown or burnup charts show remaining work against time in each iteration and release.

For example (illustrative), a team with 240 story points in its release backlog and a recent velocity of between 28 and 34 points per two-week sprint can forecast 240 ÷ 34 ≈ 7.1 to 240 ÷ 28 ≈ 8.6, so 8 to 9 sprints, or 16 to 18 weeks. As the backlog changes and velocity settles, the forecast is updated every sprint. See Agile release planning for the method in full.

Personal time management for project managers

The second meaning of project time management is how you spend your own week. A schedule that is not updated quickly undoes good planning, and a simple weekly rhythm helps:

WhenActivityTypical time
Monday morningReview critical and near-critical paths; confirm the week's priorities with team leads45 to 60 minutes
TuesdayRisk and issue review; update the RAID log30 to 45 minutes
WednesdayStakeholder conversations and decisions needed this weekAs needed
ThursdayLook ahead two to four weeks: resources, approvals, supplier dates30 minutes
FridayCollect progress, update the schedule, issue the status report60 to 90 minutes
  • Prioritise deliberately: sort tasks by importance and urgency; our priority list template does this for you.
  • Block focus time for planning, analysis and writing, and protect it like a meeting.
  • Keep meetings short with a clear agenda, decisions and actions.
  • Delegate task-level tracking to team leads and focus your own attention on the critical path, risks and stakeholders.

Common project time management mistakes

  • Scheduling from a deadline backwards without checking whether the work fits, then discovering the gap months later.
  • Confusing effort with duration, so a 10-day task assigned to someone working half-time on the project is shown as 10 days instead of about 20.
  • Leaving out non-work time: holidays, approvals, procurement lead times and handovers.
  • Hard-coding dates instead of linking activities, so the schedule cannot recalculate when something slips.
  • Padding every activity instead of holding visible contingency, which hides the real risk and encourages work to expand.
  • Watching only the critical path and ignoring near-critical paths that can become critical after a small delay.
  • Changing the baseline informally, which makes the project look on time while it drifts.

Project time management checklist

Before you baseline the schedule:

  • Every work package in the WBS has at least one activity, and every activity has an owner.
  • Estimates have recorded assumptions, and uncertain ones use three points.
  • Calendars include holidays and part-time availability.
  • The critical path makes sense to the people doing the work.
  • Contingency is visible and owned, and the commitment date has a stated confidence level.

At each status update:

  • Actual dates and remaining durations are entered for all started activities.
  • Critical and near-critical paths have been reviewed after recalculation.
  • Variances beyond thresholds are explained, with actions and owners.
  • Any baseline change has an approved change request.

Frequently asked questions

What is project time management?

It is the set of processes used to plan, schedule and control the timing of a project so it finishes by an agreed date: planning the approach, defining and sequencing activities, estimating durations, developing the schedule and controlling it.

What are the six processes of project time management?

Plan schedule management, define activities, sequence activities, estimate activity durations, develop schedule and control schedule. Earlier PMBOK editions also included estimate activity resources, which moved to resource management in the 6th edition.

What is the difference between project time management and schedule management?

They describe the same area. The PMBOK Guide 6th edition, released in 2017, renamed Project Time Management to Project Schedule Management. The 8th edition covers it in the Schedule performance domain.

Why is time management important in project management?

Delays raise costs, postpone benefits, disrupt other projects and break commitments. Good time management produces a realistic finish date, gives early warning of slippage and shows the team which work drives the end date.

What tools are used in project time management?

Gantt charts, network diagrams, schedule templates, scheduling software, earned value reports and timesheets. Tools such as Wrike, ClickUp and Smartsheet combine Gantt charts, dependencies and progress tracking in one place.

What is a schedule management plan?

A short document that sets the rules for the schedule: the method and tool, units and calendars, level of detail, estimating approach, update cycle, control thresholds and how the baseline can be changed.

How do you estimate activity durations?

Use analogous estimates from similar past work, parametric estimates from unit rates, or three-point estimates. With three points, the expected duration is (optimistic + 4 × most likely + pessimistic) ÷ 6. Record the assumptions behind each estimate.

What is the critical path in project time management?

The longest sequence of dependent activities, which sets the shortest possible project duration. Critical activities have zero float, so any delay to them delays the finish. See our critical path method guide.

How do you control the schedule?

Update progress at a fixed interval, recalculate the schedule, compare forecast dates with the baseline, check critical and near-critical paths, use measures such as SPI, and act on variances through change control.

What is the difference between project time management and personal time management?

Project time management plans and controls the project's schedule. Personal time management is how individuals, including the project manager, organise their own working time. Both matter, but they use different techniques.

How does time management work in Agile projects?

Agile teams fix time with timeboxes such as sprints and let scope flex, delivering the highest-priority work first. Release dates are forecast from measured velocity or throughput and updated every iteration.

What causes project delays?

Common causes are unclear or changing scope, optimistic estimates, shared resources being pulled away, supplier and approval delays, hidden dependencies and schedules that are not updated, so problems surface late.

Is project time management on the PMP exam?

Yes. Planning and managing the schedule is part of the Process domain, which makes up 41% of the PMP exam from July 2026. See our PMP certification guide.
