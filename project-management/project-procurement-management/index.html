Project Procurement Management: Steps, Contracts, Examples

Project procurement management is the work of buying the goods, services and results a project needs from outside the project team: deciding what to buy, choosing suppliers, agreeing contracts and managing them until the work is accepted and closed. Done well, it protects your budget, schedule and quality; done badly, it is a common source of disputes, delays and overruns. This guide covers the four stages, the procurement management plan, RFI, RFQ and RFP documents, contract types with worked numbers, bid scoring and how to control and close contracts.

Key takeaways

  • Project procurement runs in four stages: plan, conduct (select suppliers), control (manage the contract) and close.
  • Start with a make-or-buy analysis over the full life of the asset, not just the upfront cost.
  • Contract type decides who carries cost risk: fixed price puts most risk on the seller, cost-reimbursable on the buyer, and incentive contracts share it.
  • Agree mandatory requirements, weighted criteria and the price-scoring formula before any bids arrive.
  • The PMBOK Guide 8th edition covers procurement in a dedicated appendix, and the July 2026 PMP exam includes the task "Plan and manage procurement".

What is project procurement management?

Project procurement management is the set of activities used to acquire products, services or results from outside the project team. It covers planning what to buy and how, inviting and evaluating bids, selecting suppliers, agreeing contracts, managing supplier performance and changes, and formally closing each contract once the work has been accepted and paid for.

Most projects procure something: contractors and specialist consultants, software licences, equipment, materials, venues or a complete deliverable such as a building or an IT system. On some projects, such as construction or major IT implementations, most of the budget is spent through contracts, so procurement decisions shape the project budget, the schedule and the risk profile.

Project procurement differs from routine purchasing. Purchasing is the transactional step of raising orders and paying invoices for things the organisation buys regularly. Project procurement is tied to one temporary project, often buys something unique, and has to fit the project's scope, schedule and risk plan.

Who does what in project procurement

RoleTypical responsibilities
Project managerDefines the need, contributes to make-or-buy decisions, writes or approves the statement of work, manages supplier delivery against the plan, accepts deliverables
Procurement or contracts officerOwns the formal process, chooses the route to market, issues bid documents, runs the evaluation, holds contract authority and signs
LegalDrafts or reviews terms and conditions, liability, intellectual property and dispute clauses
FinanceConfirms budget, payment terms and supplier financial checks; processes invoices
Technical evaluatorsScore bids against technical criteria and check deliverables meet specifications
SponsorApproves significant spend and the final supplier choice where governance requires it

In small organisations the project manager may do most of this alone. In larger and public-sector organisations only an authorised contracts officer can commit the organisation to a contract, so agree responsibilities early, ideally in a RACI matrix.

Procurement in the PMBOK Guide and the PMP exam

PMI's treatment of procurement has changed across editions of the PMBOK Guide, which explains why study materials describe it differently.

EditionHow procurement is covered
5th edition (2013)Four processes: Plan Procurement Management, Conduct Procurements, Control Procurements, Close Procurements
6th edition (2017)Three processes: Plan Procurement Management, Conduct Procurements, Control Procurements (closing was folded into Control Procurements)
7th edition (2021)No processes; procurement is discussed within the performance domains and models, methods and artifacts. The 6th-edition processes moved to PMI's Process Groups: A Practice Guide
8th edition (November 2025)Six principles, seven performance domains and 40 processes, plus a dedicated appendix, X4 Procurement, covering make-or-buy analysis, procurement strategy, bid documents, source selection, contract types and claims administration

For exam candidates, the PMP exam content outline in force from July 2026 includes the task "Plan and manage procurement" in the Process domain (41% of the exam). Its enablers include planning procurement, selecting contract types, negotiating agreements, managing suppliers and contracts, and evaluating vendor performance. The four stages of project procurement

StageWhat happensKey outputs
1. Plan procurementIdentify what to buy, carry out make-or-buy analysis, choose the procurement strategy and contract type, write the statement of work and selection criteriaProcurement management plan, make-or-buy decisions, statements of work, bid documents, source selection criteria
2. Conduct procurementsIssue bid documents, answer supplier questions, receive and evaluate bids, negotiate and awardSelected suppliers, signed contracts, updated schedule and risk register
3. Control procurementsMonitor delivery and quality, approve invoices, manage changes and claims, review supplier performanceAccepted deliverables, approved changes, performance records, payments
4. Close procurementsConfirm all work is accepted, settle final payments and claims, release retentions, archive recordsClosed contracts, supplier performance evaluation, lessons learned

The stages overlap with the rest of the project. You may be conducting one procurement while still planning another, and control continues for the whole contract. Procurement also links closely to project integration management, because supplier changes go through the same change control as everything else.

How to run a project procurement step by step

The steps below follow one procurement from need to closure. Scale the formality to the value and risk: a $2,000 purchase might need one quote and a purchase order, while a $2 million contract needs a full tender.

  1. Confirm the need. Check the requirement against the scope baseline and work breakdown structure, and confirm budget is available.
  2. Decide make or buy. Compare doing the work in house with buying it, over the full life of the asset (see the worked example below).
  3. Choose the procurement strategy. Decide the route to market (single quote, competitive quotes, open or restricted tender, framework or existing contract), the contract type and how many suppliers you need.
  4. Write the statement of work. Describe what the supplier must deliver, to what standard, by when and how it will be accepted. See our statement of work guide.
  5. Set the evaluation method before you see bids. Agree mandatory pass/fail requirements, weighted criteria, the scoring scale and the price formula, and record them in the bid documents.
  6. Issue the bid documents. Send the RFI, RFQ or RFP with clear deadlines, answer questions in writing and share every answer with all bidders.
  7. Evaluate and select. Check compliance, score bids independently, moderate the scores, check references and financial stability, and negotiate with the preferred bidder.
  8. Award and mobilise. Sign the contract, notify unsuccessful bidders, hold a kick-off meeting and add the supplier's milestones to the project schedule.
  9. Control delivery. Track milestones, quality, invoices, changes and risks, and hold regular supplier performance reviews.
  10. Close the contract. Confirm acceptance, settle final payments and claims, release any retentions and record supplier performance and lessons learned.
Tip

Allow for procurement lead time. A competitive tender can take weeks or months from approval to signature, and that time belongs in the schedule as activities with owners, not as a single milestone.

Make-or-buy analysis: a worked example

This is an illustrative example. A project needs a customer booking system. The team can build it in house or buy a hosted product. The comparison covers three years of use.

Cost itemMake (build in house)Buy (hosted product)
Initial cost600 hours x $90 = $54,000Setup and configuration $8,000
Annual running costMaintenance $12,000Subscription $1,500 x 12 = $18,000
Three-year total$54,000 + 3 x $12,000 = $90,000$8,000 + 3 x $18,000 = $62,000

Over three years, buying is $28,000 cheaper. Building only catches up when $54,000 + $12,000t = $8,000 + $18,000t, which gives t = $46,000 / $6,000 = about 7.7 years. Unless the system will last well beyond that, or needs features no product offers, buying is the better choice on cost.

Cost is only part of the decision. Weigh these factors as well:

FactorFavours makeFavours buy
Control and customisationUnique requirements, competitive advantageStandard needs met by existing products
Skills and capacitySkilled staff available nowSkills missing or team fully committed
Time to deliverNo suitable product existsProduct available immediately
RiskSupplier lock-in or insolvency would be seriousDelivery risk better held by a specialist
Data and intellectual propertyMust own the IP and data fullyLicensed use is acceptable

For larger decisions, discount the cash flows with net present value or set them out in a full cost-benefit analysis.

The procurement management plan

The procurement management plan is part of the project management plan. It explains how the project will buy what it needs and who may do what. On a small project it may be a page; on a major programme it is a controlled document.

Roles and authorityWho can approve, negotiate and sign, with spend limitsPM approves up to $10,000; contracts officer signs above
Procurement routesQuotes, tenders or frameworks by value bandUnder $25,000: three written quotes; above: competitive RFP
Contract typesPreferred type for each package and whyFit-out: firm fixed price on a detailed design
TimelineLead times and key procurement datesFit-out tender issued week 4, awarded week 10
Selection criteriaMandatory and weighted criteria for each packageQuality 60%, price 40%

Procurement documents: RFI, RFQ, RFP and SOW

Bid documents tell suppliers what you need and how you will choose. Use the simplest one that fits.

DocumentUse it whenWhat suppliers return
Request for information (RFI)You do not yet know what the market can offerCapabilities, approaches and rough costs
Request for quotation (RFQ)The item is clearly specified and price is the main differentiatorA price and delivery terms
Request for proposal (RFP)The need is complex and you want suppliers to propose a solutionA technical proposal and a price
Invitation to bid or tender (IFB/ITB)Formal, often public-sector, competitions for defined requirementsA sealed bid on standard terms

Every procurement also needs a procurement statement of work (SOW): the description of what the supplier must deliver. A good SOW states the objectives, scope and exclusions, deliverables and acceptance criteria, standards, schedule and milestones, reporting, and what the buyer will provide. Ongoing services often add a service level agreement with measurable targets.

Contract types and who carries the risk

The contract type decides how the supplier is paid and therefore who bears the cost if the work costs more than expected. There are three families: fixed price, cost-reimbursable and time and materials.

TypeHow payment worksRisk mainly withBest when
Firm fixed price (FFP)One agreed price for defined scopeSellerScope is clear and stable
Fixed price incentive fee (FPIF)Target cost and profit, a share ratio for over- or underruns, and a ceiling priceShared, mostly sellerPerformance targets can be measured
Fixed price with economic price adjustment (FP-EPA)Fixed price adjusted for inflation or commodity indicesSharedLong contracts with volatile input costs
Cost plus fixed fee (CPFF)Actual allowable costs plus a fixed feeBuyerScope is uncertain
Cost plus incentive fee (CPIF)Costs plus a fee that rises or falls with performance against target costShared, mostly buyerUncertain scope but measurable targets
Cost plus award fee (CPAF)Costs plus a fee awarded on the buyer's judgement of performanceBuyerPerformance is hard to measure objectively
Time and materials (T&M)Agreed rates per hour or day plus materials, often with a not-to-exceed capBuyerSmall or urgent work, staff augmentation

Worked example: the same overrun under four contract types

This is an illustrative example. A supplier estimates the work at $200,000, with a target profit or fee of $20,000 (a target price of $220,000). The work actually costs the supplier $240,000, a $40,000 overrun. For the incentive contracts the share ratio is 80/20 (buyer 80%, seller 20%), and the FPIF contract has a ceiling price of $250,000.

ContractCalculationBuyer paysSeller profit
FFP at $220,000Price is fixed$220,000-$20,000 (a loss)
FPIFProfit = 20,000 - 20% x 40,000 = 12,000; price = 252,000, capped at the 250,000 ceiling$250,000$10,000
CPIFFee = 20,000 - 20% x 40,000 = 12,000; price = 240,000 + 12,000$252,000$12,000
CPFFFee stays at 20,000; price = 240,000 + 20,000$260,000$20,000

Under FFP the seller absorbs the whole overrun; under CPFF the buyer does. The incentive contracts share it, which keeps the seller motivated to control cost.

The FPIF contract also has a point of total assumption (PTA): the cost above which the seller bears every extra dollar because the ceiling price has been reached. PTA = (ceiling price - target price) / buyer's share + target cost = (250,000 - 220,000) / 0.8 + 200,000 = $237,500. Here actual cost ($240,000) is above the PTA, so the buyer's price is capped at $250,000.

How to choose a contract type

  • Clear, stable scope and competitive market: firm fixed price.
  • Reasonably clear scope but long duration or volatile prices: fixed price with economic price adjustment.
  • Scope too uncertain for a sensible fixed price: cost-reimbursable with an incentive fee, plus strong cost reporting.
  • Small, urgent or open-ended work: time and materials with a not-to-exceed cap.

Whatever you choose, budget for it: fixed prices include the supplier's risk premium, and cost-reimbursable contracts need contingency reserves on the buyer's side. See our guide to project cost management.

Evaluating bids and selecting a supplier

A fair, defensible evaluation is set up before bids arrive. It has three layers:

  1. Mandatory requirements (pass/fail): for example, required insurance, data protection compliance, certifications or minimum experience. Any bid that fails is excluded.
  2. Weighted quality criteria: such as technical approach, relevant experience, team, delivery plan and risk management, each scored on a defined scale by several evaluators.
  3. Price: usually scored with a formula. A common one is: price score = (lowest compliant price / bid price) x maximum price points.

Worked example: weighted bid evaluation

This is an illustrative example. A project is buying a data migration service. Quality is weighted 70% and price 30%. Quality criteria are scored 0 to 10 and multiplied by their weight factor.

Criterion (max points)Supplier ASupplier BSupplier C
Bid price$180,000$150,000$165,000
Price (30): lowest / bid x 3025.030.027.3
Technical approach (40): score x 49 = 366 = 248 = 32
Relevant experience (20): score x 28 = 167 = 149 = 18
Financial stability and risk (10): score x 1968
Total (100)86.074.085.3

Supplier B is cheapest but scores lowest overall. Suppliers A and C are within one point of each other, so the team checks references, clarifies assumptions in both bids and may invite best and final offers before awarding. Keep the evaluation records: they are your defence if an unsuccessful bidder challenges the decision.

Tip

Have evaluators score independently first, then meet to agree a moderated score with written reasons. It reduces bias and gives you a clear audit trail.

Negotiating with the preferred supplier

Negotiation should clarify and improve the deal, not reopen the competition. Prepare by listing the points that matter most to the project, such as payment milestones, acceptance tests, key personnel, liability caps and change rates, and decide in advance what you can trade. Agree outcomes in writing, make sure nothing contradicts what other bidders were told, and check that the final price still matches the scope in the statement of work.

Controlling procurements and managing suppliers

Once the contract is signed, the project manager's job is to make sure the supplier delivers what was agreed, and that the buyer meets its own obligations, such as providing access, information and timely payment.

AreaWhat to monitorExample KPI
ScheduleSupplier milestones against the integrated planMilestones achieved on time (%)
QualityInspections, tests and acceptance resultsDeliverables accepted first time (%)
CostInvoices against milestones, approved changes, forecast final costForecast final cost vs contract value
Changes and claimsChange requests, variations and disputesOpen change requests and average days to decide
ServiceResponsiveness, reporting and service levelsService level targets met (%)
  • Route every change to scope, price or dates through formal change control and record it as a contract variation before the work starts. Our change request template helps.
  • Pay against accepted milestones or deliverables, not effort, wherever the contract allows.
  • Add supplier risks, such as late delivery, key-person loss or insolvency, to the risk register as soon as the contract is signed.
  • Hold regular performance reviews and keep minutes; problems raised early are cheaper to fix.
  • Handle claims and disputes using the process in the contract, escalating to negotiation, mediation or arbitration only as needed.

Closing procurements

Each contract is closed when its work is complete, which may be long before the project ends. Closing a contract is not the same as closing the project: the project closure happens once, at the end, while procurements close one by one.

  1. Confirm all deliverables have been formally accepted against the SOW.
  2. Resolve open change requests, claims and disputes.
  3. Reconcile invoices and make the final payment; release retentions or bonds when the defects period ends.
  4. Return or confirm transfer of buyer property, data and access rights.
  5. Complete a supplier performance evaluation for future procurements.
  6. Archive the contract file, correspondence and evaluation records.
  7. Send formal written notice that the contract is complete.
  8. Capture lessons learned about the procurement process itself.

Procurement in Agile and hybrid projects

Traditional fixed-price contracts assume scope can be defined upfront, which clashes with Agile project management, where scope evolves sprint by sprint. Common approaches include:

  • Time and materials with a cap for a team working through a prioritised backlog, with the buyer's product owner controlling priorities.
  • Fixed price per increment or release, where each increment has defined goals and acceptance criteria, and the buyer can stop after any increment.
  • Target cost contracts that share savings and overruns, so both sides are motivated to deliver value within budget.
  • Fixed budget, variable scope: the budget and dates are fixed, and the backlog is prioritised so the most valuable work is delivered first.

Whichever model you choose, write acceptance into the contract: a shared definition of done, regular reviews of working increments and a clear process for changing priorities without a formal contract variation each time.

Public-sector projects add legal rules. In the UK, for example, the Procurement Act 2023 came into force on 24 February 2025, and US federal agencies follow the Federal Acquisition Regulation. Our guide to government project management covers the wider context.

Common mistakes in project procurement

  • Vague statements of work. If acceptance criteria are unclear, disputes and claims follow.
  • Setting criteria after seeing bids. It makes the process unfair and open to challenge.
  • Choosing on price alone. The cheapest bid can be the most expensive once rework, delays and changes are added.
  • Ignoring lead times. Procurement activities missing from the schedule are a common cause of late starts.
  • Wrong contract type. Fixed price on undefined scope leads to inflated bids or constant variations.
  • Informal changes. Agreeing extra work in a meeting without a written variation creates unbudgeted cost.
  • Involving legal and finance too late. Bring them in during planning for anything significant.
  • Forgetting to close. Unclosed contracts leave retentions, warranties and liabilities untracked.

Frequently asked questions

What is project procurement management?

The processes for buying goods, services or results from outside the project team, from planning and supplier selection to contract management and closure.

What are the project procurement management processes in PMBOK?

The PMBOK Guide 6th edition lists three: Plan Procurement Management, Conduct Procurements and Control Procurements. The 5th edition added Close Procurements as a fourth. The 8th edition (November 2025) covers procurement in a dedicated appendix on make-or-buy, strategy, bid documents, source selection, contract types and claims.

What are the four stages of procurement management?

Plan procurement, conduct procurements (select suppliers and award contracts), control procurements (manage delivery, changes and payments) and close procurements.

What is included in a procurement management plan?

The items to procure, roles and approval limits, procurement routes, preferred contract types, timeline, selection criteria, supplier management approach, and supplier risks and constraints.

What is a make-or-buy analysis?

A comparison of the costs, risks and benefits of producing something in house against buying it from a supplier, ideally over the full life of the asset, used to decide whether to procure.

What are the main types of contracts in project procurement?

Fixed-price (FFP, FPIF, FP-EPA), cost-reimbursable (CPFF, CPIF, CPAF) and time and materials. Each shares cost risk differently between buyer and seller.

Which contract type carries the most risk for the buyer?

Cost-reimbursable contracts, especially cost plus fixed fee, because the buyer pays the seller's actual allowable costs, which may rise.

Which contract type carries the most risk for the seller?

Firm fixed price, because the seller must deliver the agreed scope for the agreed price even if its costs rise.

What is the point of total assumption?

In a fixed price incentive fee contract, the cost above which the seller bears all further overruns. PTA = (ceiling price - target price) / buyer's share ratio + target cost.

What is the difference between an RFI, an RFQ and an RFP?

An RFI gathers market information before you decide how to buy. An RFQ asks for a price for a clearly specified item. An RFP asks suppliers to propose a solution as well as a price.

What is a procurement statement of work?

A description of the work, products or services the supplier must deliver, detailed enough for suppliers to price it and for the buyer to accept it. See our statement of work guide.

Who is responsible for project procurement management?

The project manager defines the need and manages supplier performance, while a procurement or contracts team usually runs the formal process and holds the authority to sign contracts.

How is a project procurement closed?

By confirming all deliverables are accepted, settling final payments and claims, releasing retentions, archiving records, evaluating supplier performance and sending formal notice of completion.
