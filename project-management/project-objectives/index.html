Project Objectives: How to Write Them (With Examples)

Project objectives are the specific, measurable results a project must achieve by a set date, such as cutting order processing time from 48 to 24 hours by 30 September. They turn a broad goal into targets you can plan, track and judge success against. This guide gives you a simple formula, a step-by-step method, a fully worked example and more than 30 examples you can adapt.

Written byNeural Project InstituteProject management editorial team
  • Published 14 September 2026
  • 21 min read

Key takeaways

  • A project objective states a measurable result, a baseline, a target and a deadline; a goal states the broad direction.
  • Write objectives as outcomes (what changes), not as activities or deliverables (what you do or produce).
  • Use SMART as a quality check and a simple formula to draft: verb + metric + baseline + target + date + boundary.
  • Three to five objectives is usually enough; more than that dilutes focus and makes trade-offs harder.
  • Agree how and when each objective will be measured before work starts, and record it in the project charter.
  • Revisit objectives at every stage gate and change them only through formal change control.

What are project objectives?

Project objectives are the specific, measurable results a project is expected to achieve within a defined time frame. They describe what will be different when the project succeeds, for example lower costs, faster service or a new capability in use, and give the team, sponsor and stakeholders a shared basis for planning work and judging success.

A well-written objective usually contains five elements:

  • A result described as a change, not a task ("reduce", "increase", "launch and adopt").
  • A measure that everyone agrees on (hours, pounds, percentage, score, number of users).
  • A baseline showing where you are today.
  • A target showing where you need to be.
  • A deadline by which the target must be met.

Objectives sit between the strategic reason for the project and the detailed plan. They usually start life in the business case or feasibility study, are confirmed in the project charter or project initiation document, and then shape the scope, schedule, budget and quality plans in the project management plan.

Why project objectives matter

Clear objectives do more than describe success. In our experience they are the single most useful tool a project manager has for making decisions when time, money or people are short.

  • They guide trade-offs. When a feature, a deadline and a budget collide, the objectives tell you which matters most. An objective that says "live before the January sales peak" makes it clear that a late but perfect system is a failure.
  • They define scope. Work that does not contribute to an objective is a candidate for removal, which is your first defence against scope creep.
  • They align stakeholders. Sponsors, users and suppliers often have different ideas of success. Writing objectives down forces those differences into the open early, when they are cheap to resolve.
  • They make progress measurable. Status reports can show movement against targets rather than just tasks completed.
  • They make closure possible. Without measurable objectives you cannot say objectively whether the project succeeded, and lessons learned become a matter of opinion.

Research on project success has long pointed to the same thing. Studies of critical success factors repeatedly rank a clearly defined project mission or goal among the most important conditions for success.

Project goals vs objectives

Goals and objectives are related but not the same. A goal is the broad, often long-term aim the project serves. An objective is a specific, measurable step towards that goal. One goal usually breaks down into several objectives.

Project goalProject objective
PurposeSets direction and explains whyDefines exactly what must be achieved
Level of detailBroad, often qualitativeSpecific and quantified
Time frameOften longer than the projectWithin the project or a defined period after it
MeasurabilityHard to measure directlyMeasured against a baseline and target
NumberUsually one per projectTypically three to five
ExampleMake it easier for patients to book appointmentsIncrease online bookings from 15% to 50% of all appointments by 31 March

A simple test: if you cannot say whether it has been achieved on a given date, it is a goal, not an objective. Goals are still useful. They explain the purpose to the team and keep the objectives connected to strategy. Just make sure every goal is supported by at least one measurable objective.

Outputs vs outcomes

The most common confusion is between outputs (what the project produces) and outcomes (the change those outputs cause). "Deliver a new CRM system" is an output. "Reduce the time to produce a customer quote from three days to one" is an outcome. Strong objectives focus on outcomes, because an output can be delivered on time and on budget and still fail to make any difference.

That said, many organisations use two layers. Project objectives cover what the project team controls (time, cost, quality, scope of the outputs), while benefit objectives cover the outcomes the business expects to realise, often after the project has closed. If your organisation works this way, record both and be clear about who owns each one. The project manager usually owns delivery objectives; the sponsor or a business owner usually owns benefits.

Types of project objectives

Most projects need objectives from more than one category. Covering several types stops the project optimising one dimension at the expense of another, for example hitting the date by cutting quality.

TypeWhat it targetsExample
TimeDelivery dates and durationsOpen the new warehouse by 1 September 2027
Cost and financialBudget, savings, revenue, returnComplete the fit-out within the approved budget of £1.2 million
QualityStandards, defects, compliance with specificationsFewer than 5 high-severity defects open at go-live
PerformanceSpeed, capacity, reliabilityPage load time under 2 seconds for 95% of visits
Business and benefitChanges in the organisation's resultsRaise repeat purchases from 22% to 30% of customers within 12 months
Regulatory and complianceLegal, audit or safety requirementsPass the external data protection audit with no major findings by June

PRINCE2 offers a useful cross-check. PRINCE2 7 defines seven performance targets that every project should set and control: benefits, cost, time, quality, scope, risk and sustainability. Even if you do not use PRINCE2, asking whether you have an objective or tolerance for each of these seven helps you spot gaps.

SMART objectives explained

SMART is the most widely used checklist for writing objectives. Most sources today expand it as Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

LetterMeaningQuestion to askWeakStronger
SSpecificWhat exactly will change, and for whom?Improve the websiteReduce checkout abandonment on the UK website
MMeasurableWhich metric, what baseline, what target?Make it fasterCut average checkout time from 4 minutes to 2 minutes
AAchievableIs it realistic with our budget, people and skills?Zero abandonmentReduce abandonment from 70% to 60%
RRelevantDoes it support the business goal and strategy?Add a new fontSupports the goal of growing online revenue
TTime-boundBy when will it be achieved and measured?SoonBy 31 March, measured over April

The idea is usually credited to George T. Doran, whose short article "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives" appeared in *Management Review* in November 1981. In Doran's original version, A stood for assignable (someone is responsible) and R for realistic. Both are still worth checking: an objective with no owner tends to drift.

When SMART is not enough

SMART checks how an objective is written, not whether it is the right objective. A perfectly SMART objective can still measure the wrong thing, for example the number of training sessions held rather than whether staff can use the new system. Critics also note that the framework has little empirical research behind it. Use SMART as a quality check, then ask two more questions: does achieving this objective actually move us towards the goal? and could we hit the target while doing harm elsewhere? If the answer to the second is yes, add a balancing objective, such as a quality floor alongside a speed target.

Tip

Pair speed or cost objectives with a quality objective. "Cut call handling time by 20%" on its own can be achieved by rushing customers off the phone; adding "while keeping first-contact resolution at or above 75%" closes that loophole.

A simple formula for writing project objectives

SMART tells you what a good objective looks like. A formula helps you write one quickly. We use this structure:

[Action verb] + [metric] + from [baseline] to [target] + by [date] + [boundary or condition]

ElementPurposeExample
Action verbShows the direction of changeReduce
MetricWhat will be measuredaverage invoice processing time
BaselineWhere you are nowfrom 9 working days
TargetWhere you need to beto 3 working days
DateWhen it must be achievedby 30 June 2027
Boundary or conditionScope, quality floor or how it is measuredfor all UK supplier invoices, measured over July

Put together: "Reduce average invoice processing time from 9 working days to 3 working days by 30 June 2027 for all UK supplier invoices, measured over July." That is one sentence, it can be checked by anyone with access to the finance system, and it leaves the team free to decide how to achieve it.

Good verbs for outcomes include reduce, increase, achieve, adopt and eliminate. Avoid activity verbs such as support, explore or work on.

How to write project objectives step by step

This process works for a one-page project brief or a full charter. Allow a workshop of one to two hours with the sponsor and key stakeholders for a medium-sized project.

  1. Start from the business need. Read the business case, cost-benefit analysis or problem statement. Write the project goal in one sentence: why is the organisation spending money on this?
  2. Identify the stakeholders who define success. List the sponsor, the users, the operations owner and anyone who must approve the result. Our stakeholder management guide explains how to map them.
  3. Ask what will be different. For each stakeholder, ask: "If this project succeeds, what will you see or measure differently six months later?" Capture the answers in their own words.
  4. Turn answers into measurable results. Choose a metric for each result. If there is no reliable data source, decide now how you will collect it, or choose a different metric.
  5. Collect baselines. Measure the current position. An objective without a baseline cannot show improvement, and you may discover the problem is smaller (or larger) than assumed.
  6. Set realistic targets and dates. Check targets against budget, resources, past performance and benchmarks. Involve the people who will do the work; they know what is achievable.
  7. Draft each objective with the formula and test it against SMART. Keep each one to a single sentence and a single result. Split any objective that contains "and".
  8. Prioritise and limit the list. Rank the objectives so the team knows which wins when they conflict. Aim for three to five.
  9. Assign an owner and a measurement plan. For each objective record who owns it, the data source, and when it will be measured (at go-live, at closure or after closure).
  10. Agree and record. Review the objectives with the sponsor and stakeholders, then record them in the charter or project initiation document. From this point, changes go through change control.
Tip

If stakeholders disagree on the objectives, resolve it before planning. A disagreement about objectives that is postponed to the delivery phase usually returns as a dispute about scope, budget or acceptance.

Worked example: from business goal to project objectives

Here is the whole process applied to one project. A group of five medical clinics wants patients to book appointments online. Today most bookings come by phone, reception staff are overloaded and patients complain about waiting on hold.

Step 1: the goal

Goal: make it quicker and easier for patients to book appointments while reducing pressure on reception teams.

Step 2: first drafts from the workshop

  • "Launch online booking." (an output, not a result)
  • "Improve patient satisfaction." (no metric, baseline or date)
  • "Reduce phone calls." (no target)
  • "Do it within budget." (no figure)

Step 3: baselines collected

The practice manager pulls three months of data: 15% of appointments are booked online through an old form, reception answers about 2,400 calls a week, average time on hold is 6 minutes and the latest patient survey scored booking convenience at 3.1 out of 5. The approved budget is £85,000.

Step 4: final objectives

#ObjectiveTypeOwnerMeasured
1Launch online booking for all five clinics by 28 February 2027, within the approved budget of £85,000Time and costProject managerAt go-live
2Increase the share of appointments booked online from 15% to 50% by 31 August 2027Business and benefitOperations directorMonthly from March; final in September
3Reduce average weekly booking calls from 2,400 to 1,500 and average time on hold from 6 to 2 minutes by 31 August 2027PerformancePractice managerTelephony reports, monthly
4Raise the patient survey score for booking convenience from 3.1 to 4.0 out of 5 by the September 2027 surveyStakeholderPatient experience leadSeptember survey
5Meet the organisation's data protection and accessibility requirements, with sign-off from the data protection officer before go-liveComplianceProject managerBefore go-live

Notice what changed. Objective 1 covers what the project team controls. Objectives 2 to 4 describe the outcomes the clinics actually want, and they are owned by the people who run the service, because they will be measured after the project team has moved on. Objective 3 bundles two related call-centre measures; this is acceptable because they share an owner and a data source, but you could split them. Objective 5 is a pass/fail compliance condition.

The objectives also made two scope decisions easy. A request to add online prescription renewals did not support any objective, so it went to a later phase. A request for a text-message reminder feature did support objective 3, so it was added through change control with a small budget increase.

Project objectives examples by project type

Adapt these examples to your own baselines and dates. Figures are illustrative.

IT and software projects

  • Migrate all 1,200 user mailboxes to the new email platform by 15 May with no more than 4 hours of unplanned downtime per user.
  • Reduce average month-end close time from 8 to 5 working days within two months of the new finance system going live.
  • Achieve 99.9% monthly availability for the customer portal in the first quarter after launch.
  • Retire the legacy CRM and its £60,000 annual licence by 31 December.

Construction projects

  • Achieve practical completion of the 48-unit residential block by 30 November 2027.
  • Complete the project within the contract sum of £6.4 million plus approved variations.
  • Record zero RIDDOR-reportable incidents during the build.
  • Achieve the agreed energy performance rating for all units at handover.

Marketing projects

  • Generate 400 qualified leads from the spring campaign by 30 April at a cost per lead of £45 or less.
  • Increase organic search traffic to the product pages from 8,000 to 12,000 visits a month by the end of Q3.
  • Launch the rebrand across the website, packaging and all social channels by 1 March.

HR and training projects

  • Train all 220 customer service staff on the new case-management system by 31 January, with at least 90% passing the competency assessment first time.
  • Reduce average time-to-hire for engineering roles from 62 to 40 days by the end of the financial year.
  • Roll out the new performance review process to every department before the April review cycle.

Nonprofit and public sector

  • Provide 5,000 additional free school meals per week across the district from the start of the autumn term.
  • Reduce the waiting list for the housing advice service from 10 weeks to 4 weeks within 12 months.
  • Cut food waste at the three community kitchens by 30% compared with last year's baseline by December.

Process improvement

  • Reduce the error rate on outbound orders from 2.5% to below 1% by the end of Q2.
  • Cut the number of approval steps for purchase orders under £5,000 from five to two by 30 September.
  • Reduce average customer complaint resolution time from 14 to 7 days within six months.

Project objectives in Agile projects

Agile teams still need objectives, but they hold the scope more loosely and the outcome more tightly. Instead of fixing every feature up front, they fix the result they want and adjust the backlog as they learn.

  • Product goal. The Scrum Guide (2020) introduced the Product Goal: a long-term objective for the product that the Product Backlog serves. It works much like a project goal.
  • Sprint goals. Each sprint has a single sprint goal that gives the team focus for that iteration. Read more in what is a sprint.
  • Outcome-based roadmaps. Many teams express roadmap items as outcomes ("cut onboarding time in half") rather than features. See our guide to the Agile roadmap.
  • Release or programme objectives. In scaled frameworks such as SAFe, teams set objectives for each planning interval and the business assigns them a value.

Measure Agile objectives with outcome metrics, not just velocity. Our guide to Agile metrics and KPIs lists useful options such as lead time, customer satisfaction and adoption.

Project objectives and OKRs

OKRs (objectives and key results) are a goal-setting method developed at Intel under Andy Grove and later popularised by John Doerr. An OKR pairs a qualitative, inspiring objective with two to five measurable key results. They are usually set quarterly for teams and organisations rather than for individual projects.

The two work well together. A company OKR might be "Make self-service the easiest way to get help", with the key result "40% of support requests resolved without an agent". The customer portal project then adopts that key result as one of its objectives. Our free OKR template helps you set and score them each quarter.

How to track and evaluate project objectives

Writing objectives is only useful if you check them. Build the measurement into your normal project controls.

During delivery

  • Show each objective on the first page of your project status report, with its current forecast (on track, at risk, off track).
  • Review objectives at every stage gate or steering meeting, as part of your project governance.
  • Assess every change request against the objectives it affects.

At closure

  • Measure each delivery objective and record the result against its target.
  • Explain any variance and capture what caused it in lessons learned.
  • Hand over benefit objectives that will be measured later to a named business owner, with dates. See our guide to project closure.

After closure

Many benefit objectives can only be measured months later. Schedule a benefits review in advance, for example six or twelve months after go-live, and compare actual results with the baseline. If the project had a financial case, compare the realised figures with the forecast return on investment.

Common mistakes when setting project objectives

  • Writing activities, not results. "Hold weekly meetings" or "deploy the software" says what you will do, not what will change.
  • No baseline. "Reduce errors by 30%" is meaningless if nobody knows the current error rate.
  • Too many objectives. Ten objectives with equal weight give the team no guidance when they conflict.
  • Bundling several results. One objective containing three targets is hard to measure and impossible to prioritise.
  • Targets outside your control. Market share or share price depends on factors the project cannot influence; choose something the project can affect.
  • Unmeasurable words. Avoid "improve", "optimise", "enhance", "world-class" and "user-friendly" unless they are followed by a number.
  • Setting them alone. Objectives written by the project manager without the sponsor and users tend to be ignored or disputed later.
  • Changing them informally. Quietly lowering a target because the project is behind hides the real position. Change objectives openly through change control.

Project objectives checklist

Use this checklist before the charter is signed. Every answer should be yes.

CheckQuestion
LinkedDoes each objective clearly support the project goal and a business priority?
OutcomeDoes it describe a result, not an activity or deliverable?
SingleDoes it contain one result (or closely related results with one owner)?
MeasurableIs there a named metric, a baseline and a target?
DataDo we know where the data will come from and who will collect it?
Time-boundIs there a date for achievement and a date for measurement?
AchievableHave the people doing the work confirmed it is realistic?
OwnedIs there a named owner?
BalancedIs there a quality or risk safeguard against shortcuts?
PrioritisedIs it ranked against the other objectives?
AgreedHas the sponsor approved it and is it recorded in the charter?

When the objectives pass this check, use them to define the project requirements and build the work breakdown structure. Every major work package should trace back to at least one objective.

Frequently asked questions

What are project objectives?

Project objectives are the specific, measurable results a project must achieve within a set time, such as reducing processing time from 10 to 5 days by 31 July. They define success and guide planning and trade-offs.

What are the 5 SMART objectives in project management?

SMART is a checklist rather than five separate objectives. Each objective should be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. In George Doran's original 1981 version, A stood for assignable and R for realistic.

How many objectives should a project have?

Three to five is a practical number for most projects. That is enough to cover time, cost, quality and the main business outcome without diluting focus. Very large programmes may have more, grouped under a few goals.

What is the difference between a project goal and a project objective?

A goal is the broad aim, such as improving the patient booking experience. An objective is a specific, measurable result that moves you towards the goal, such as increasing online bookings from 15% to 50% by 31 August.

What is the difference between project objectives and deliverables?

Deliverables are the outputs the project produces, such as a system, report or building. Objectives are the results those outputs should achieve. Read our guide to project deliverables.

What is the difference between objectives and success criteria?

Objectives state what the project must achieve. Success criteria state how you will judge whether the project succeeded, and often include the objectives plus conditions such as stakeholder satisfaction or quality thresholds.

Can project objectives change during a project?

Yes, if the business need changes, but only through formal change control with sponsor approval. Record the reason and update the charter, plan and baselines so that success is judged against the agreed version.

Who sets project objectives?

The sponsor owns the objectives and approves them. The project manager usually facilitates and drafts them with input from stakeholders, users and the delivery team.

Where are project objectives documented?

In the business case and project charter or project initiation document, and then referenced in the project management plan, status reports and closure report.

What are the main types of project objectives?

Common types are time, cost and financial, quality, performance, business and benefit, regulatory and compliance, people and stakeholder, and sustainability objectives. Most projects need a mix.

How do you measure project objectives?

Agree the metric, data source, baseline and measurement date when you set each objective. Track leading indicators during delivery, measure delivery objectives at closure and measure benefit objectives at a scheduled review afterwards.

What is an example of a good project objective?

"Reduce average invoice processing time from 9 to 3 working days by 30 June 2027 for all UK supplier invoices." It states the result, metric, baseline, target, date and boundary in one sentence.

Are OKRs the same as project objectives?

Not quite. OKRs pair a qualitative objective with measurable key results and are usually set quarterly for teams or organisations. Project objectives apply to one project. A project is often the way a key result is achieved.

How long should a project objective be?

One sentence is ideal. If you need a paragraph to explain it, it probably contains more than one result or is mixing in the method. Put the detail in the scope statement or plan instead.
