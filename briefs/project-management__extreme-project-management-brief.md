## Search Intent

Informational. Most searchers are students, PMP/CAPM learners and practitioners who met the term in a methodology list and want a clear definition, how it differs from Agile and traditional project management, the phases and when to use it. A smaller group (innovation, R&D and start-up project leads) wants practical guidance on running a high-uncertainty project. The page should define XPM in the first sentence, then go further than competitors with the actual models (DeCarlo's Flexible Project Model and four business questions, Wysocki's goal/solution clarity grid and INSPIRE), a step-by-step process and a full illustrative worked example.

## Writer Notes and Fact Checks

- DeCarlo's book: *eXtreme Project Management: Using Leadership, Principles, and Tools to Deliver Value in the Face of Volatility*, Jossey-Bass/Wiley, published October 2004 (Booktopia lists 7 October 2004; Wikipedia references October 2004). Spell the cycle "Reevaluate" as the book does.
- Flexible Project Model and the four business questions verified from the O'Reilly chapter previews (chapters 8–14, 11 and 13).
- The 23-factor structure (4 accelerators, 10 shared values, 4 business questions, 5 critical success factors) is from the PDMA book review; do not name individual accelerators or values (not verified).
- Wysocki: *Effective Project Management: Traditional, Adaptive, Extreme* (with Rudd McGary, 2003) and later editions titled *Traditional, Agile, Extreme* (and *Hybrid*). INSPIRE phases (Initiate, Speculate, Incubate, Review) as summarised by Wrike and named in Wysocki's software PM book.
- The old page said XPM "was popularised by Doug DeCarlo in his 2004 book": keep, but note the term was also used by Wysocki from 2003.
- No statistics on XPM adoption or success exist from reliable sources: do not add any. The worked example is illustrative and labelled as such.
- No formal XPM certification body: say so without naming invented credentials.
