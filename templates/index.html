What is a project management template?

A project management template is a ready-made, pre-structured document, such as a spreadsheet or form, that you copy and fill in to plan, track or report on a project. It gives you the right headings, columns and formulas in advance, so you spend your time on the content of the project rather than on designing the document, and every project records the same information in the same way.

Templates do not replace project management judgement. A risk register template will not identify your risks, and a Gantt chart template will not tell you how long tasks take. What they do is make sure you ask the right questions, keep information in one consistent place and can show stakeholders a familiar format. For the method behind each document, our project management guide explains the processes the templates support.

The essential starter kit

You do not need all 55 templates on one project. For a typical project, these five documents cover most of what sponsors, team members and auditors expect to see:

  1. Project charter: authorises the project and records its purpose, objectives, high-level scope, milestones, budget and sponsor. Read our project charter guide for what to include.
  2. Project plan or project schedule: the plan sets out goals, scope, deliverables, owners and dates; the schedule focuses on tasks, durations and dependencies. Smaller projects can use one; larger projects usually need both.
  3. RACI matrix: makes it clear who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed for each deliverable or decision, which prevents gaps and duplicated effort.
  4. Risk register: lists each risk with its probability, impact, owner and response, so risks are managed rather than discovered.
  5. Project tracker: the working list of tasks, owners, deadlines, priority and status that the team updates every week.

Add a communication plan, a budget and a change request form as the project grows, and finish with a lessons learned review.

How to use a project management template

A template saves time only if it is set up and kept up to date. This is the process we recommend:

  1. Pick the right document for the decision. Ask what question the document must answer ("Who approves this?", "What could go wrong?", "When will we finish?") and choose the template that answers it.
  2. Choose the version and format. Beginner for a quick one-off, Advanced for most projects, Expert when you report to a sponsor or PMO. Use Google Sheets when several people will edit it, Excel if your organisation works in Microsoft 365, and PDF for printing.
  3. Make your own copy first. Use the Google Sheets button to copy the template into your Drive, or download the .xlsx file. Keep the original untouched so you can reuse it.
  4. Tailor it before you fill it in. Rename columns to your organisation's terms, delete what you will not use and edit the dropdown lists, such as status values or risk categories, so everyone uses the same options.
  5. Fill it in with the people who do the work. A schedule or risk register written alone by the project manager is usually wrong. Run a short workshop and complete the document together.
  6. Agree an owner and an update rhythm. Decide who updates each document and how often: a tracker weekly, a risk register at every status meeting, a RAID log whenever something changes.
  7. Save the finished version as your organisation's template. After the project, add what you learned, so the next project starts from a better document.

Common mistakes with project templates

  • Filling in every field because it is there. Templates are a checklist, not a test. Leave out sections that do not apply to a small project.
  • Creating the document and never updating it. A risk register or tracker that is three weeks out of date is worse than none, because people trust it.
  • Using different templates on every project. Standard templates let a PMO compare projects and let people move between teams without relearning the paperwork.
  • Confusing similar documents. A project charter authorises the project; a project plan explains how it will be delivered; a project brief is a short summary for stakeholders. A RAID log covers risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies together, while a risk register covers risks in more depth.
  • Keeping the spreadsheet when it no longer fits. If several people are overwriting each other's changes or you are copying data between files every week, it is time to move to software.

Templates or project management software?

Spreadsheet templates are ideal for small and medium projects, for one-off documents such as a charter or business case, and for teams that already work in Excel or Google Sheets. They are free, flexible and easy to share.

Project management software becomes the better choice when many people need to update the same plan at once, when you run several projects that share people, or when you need automatic dependencies, workload views, approvals and dashboards that update themselves. In our independent ranking, Wrike is our top pick for teams that want projects, resources, portfolios, approvals and reporting in one platform, while Trello and Asana are easy first steps up from a spreadsheet. Many tools also let you import a spreadsheet, so a well-structured template makes the move easier.
